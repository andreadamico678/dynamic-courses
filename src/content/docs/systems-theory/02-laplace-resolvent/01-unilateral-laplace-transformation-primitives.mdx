## Introduction

Solving coupled linear differential equations directly in the time domain requires guessing homogeneous and particular solutions and fitting initial conditions every time an input changes. The **Unilateral Laplace Transform** trades calculus for ordinary algebra: time derivatives become multiplication by $s$, integrals become division by $s$, and initial conditions at $t = 0^-$ enter automatically as algebraic terms.

In practical control work—whether analyzing a motor's response to a voltage step or predicting steady-state tracking error using the Final Value Theorem—working in the $s$-domain lets you inspect transient decay rates, delays, and final values before ever inverting back to time.

### Initial-Condition ODE Transform

In [First Order Differential State Equations](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/01-state-models/01-first-order-differential-state-equations/), we saw that physical systems with mass, springs, or capacitors store internal energy at the start of an experiment ($t = 0^-$). In classical time-domain calculus, solving a differential equation with non-zero initial energy requires finding a homogeneous solution, guessing a particular solution for the input, adding them together, and then solving simultaneous equations for the arbitrary constants. The **Unilateral Laplace Transform** replaces that multi-stage procedure with a single algebraic equation in the complex frequency variable $s$.

Consider a damped second-order mechanical axis excited at $t = 0$ by a constant step force $u(t) = 6 \cdot 1(t)$, starting from an initial displaced position $y(0^-) = 1\text{ m}$ and zero initial velocity $\dot{y}(0^-) = 0\text{ m/s}$:

$$\ddot{y}(t) + 3\dot{y}(t) + 2y(t) = 6 \cdot 1(t), \qquad y(0^-) = 1, \quad \dot{y}(0^-) = 0$$

To move this differential equation into the $s$-domain, we apply the Laplace differentiation formulas $\mathcal{L}\{\dot{y}(t)\} = sY(s) - y(0^-)$ and $\mathcal{L}\{\ddot{y}(t)\} = s^2 Y(s) - s y(0^-) - \dot{y}(0^-)$ to the left-hand side, and the step transform $\mathcal{L}\{6 \cdot 1(t)\} = \frac{6}{s}$ to the right-hand side. Substituting the initial values $y(0^-) = 1$ and $\dot{y}(0^-) = 0$ term by term gives:

$$\underbrace{\big(s^2 Y(s) - s(1) - 0\big)}_{\mathcal{L}\{\ddot{y}(t)\}} + 3\underbrace{\big(s Y(s) - 1\big)}_{\mathcal{L}\{\dot{y}(t)\}} + 2 Y(s) = \frac{6}{s}$$

Notice how the derivatives $\ddot{y}$ and $\dot{y}$ have turned into polynomial multipliers $s^2$ and $3s$ acting on $Y(s)$, while the initial position $y(0^-) = 1$ has produced the algebraic terms $-s$ and $-3$. Grouping all terms multiplying $Y(s)$ on the left-hand side and moving the initial-condition polynomial $(s + 3)$ to the right-hand side yields:

$$(s^2 + 3s + 2) Y(s) = (s + 3) + \frac{6}{s} = \frac{s(s + 3) + 6}{s} = \frac{s^2 + 3s + 6}{s}$$

Here the polynomial multiplying $Y(s)$ is the system's characteristic polynomial $p(s) = s^2 + 3s + 2 = (s + 1)(s + 2)$. Dividing both sides by $(s^2 + 3s + 2)$ solves for $Y(s)$ completely and separates the total response into two physically distinct pieces:

$$Y(s) = \underbrace{\frac{s + 3}{(s + 1)(s + 2)}}_{Y_{\text{free}}(s)} + \underbrace{\frac{6}{s(s + 1)(s + 2)}}_{Y_{\text{forced}}(s)} = \frac{s^2 + 3s + 6}{s(s+1)(s+2)}$$

The first fraction $Y_{\text{free}}(s)$ is the **free response**, generated exclusively by the initial stored energy $y(0^-) = 1$ even if the external force were zero. The second fraction $Y_{\text{forced}}(s)$ is the **forced response**, generated exclusively by the external step force $\frac{6}{s}$ acting from rest. Because the denominator of $Y(s)$ has roots (poles) at $s = 0$, $s = -1$, and $s = -2$, the rightmost pole lies at $\sigma_0 = 0$, meaning the Laplace integral converges absolutely for every complex frequency in the open right half-plane $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 0$, as illustrated in [the figure](#fig-laplace-roc).

Even before inverting $Y(s)$ back into a function of time $t$ (which we will do via partial fractions in [Partial Fraction Expansion](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/02-partial-fraction-expansion-heaviside-residues/)), we can check the starting position $y(0^+)$ and final resting equilibrium $y(\infty)$ directly from $Y(s)$. Multiplying $Y(s)$ by $s$ cancels the factor of $s$ in the denominator to give $sY(s) = \frac{s^2 + 3s + 6}{(s+1)(s+2)}$. Taking the high-frequency limit $s \to \infty$ by dividing numerator and denominator by $s^2$ checks the initial value right after $t = 0$:

$$y(0^+) = \lim_{s \to \infty} s Y(s) = \lim_{s \to \infty} \frac{s^2 + 3s + 6}{s^2 + 3s + 2} = \lim_{s \to \infty} \frac{1 + \frac{3}{s} + \frac{6}{s^2}}{1 + \frac{3}{s} + \frac{2}{s^2}} = 1$$

Because $y(0^+) = 1$ matches $y(0^-) = 1$, the mass position does not suffer an unphysical jump discontinuity at $t = 0$. Finally, because both poles of $sY(s) = \frac{s^2 + 3s + 6}{(s+1)(s+2)}$ lie strictly in the stable open left half-plane ($s = -1 < 0$ and $s = -2 < 0$), all exponential transients die out and the Final Value Theorem gives the steady-state displacement as $s \to 0$:

$$y(\infty) = \lim_{s \to 0} s Y(s) = \lim_{s \to 0} \frac{s^2 + 3s + 6}{(s+1)(s+2)} = \frac{0 + 0 + 6}{(0+1)(0+2)} = \frac{6}{2} = 3$$

Physically, once acceleration $\ddot{y}$ and velocity $\dot{y}$ settle to zero in the original ODE $\ddot{y} + 3\dot{y} + 2y = 6$, the spring force balances the external force at $2y(\infty) = 6 \implies y(\infty) = 3\text{ m}$, matching our $s$-domain limit.

### Definition and Convergence Region

In real engineering control loops, an actuator command or disturbance begins at a specific initial instant $t = 0$, while the plant may already hold stored energy just prior to switching on (at $t = 0^-$) or experience an impulsive hit $\delta(t)$ right at $t = 0$. To capture initial conditions at $t = 0^-$ and impulses at $t = 0$ without needing to know the system's history over $t \in (-\infty, 0)$, we integrate from the lower limit $t = 0^-$.

Let $f(t): [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}$ be a locally integrable signal of **exponential order**, meaning there exist constants $M > 0$ and $\sigma_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $|f(t)| \le M e^{\sigma_0 t}$ for all $t \ge 0$. The **Unilateral Laplace Transform** $F(s) = \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}$ projects $f(t)$ onto the complex exponential basis $e^{-st}$, where $s = \sigma + j\omega \in \mathbb{C}$ is the complex frequency variable:

$$F(s) \triangleq \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = \int_{0^-}^\infty f(t) e^{-st} \, dt$$

Because $s = \sigma + j\omega$ has real damping part $\sigma$ and imaginary oscillatory part $\omega$, the kernel factors as $e^{-st} = e^{-\sigma t} e^{-j\omega t}$ with $|e^{-j\omega t}| = 1$. Bounding the magnitude of the integrand shows when the improper integral is finite:

$$\int_{0^-}^\infty \left| f(t) e^{-st} \right| dt = \int_{0^-}^\infty |f(t)| e^{-\sigma t} \, dt \le \int_{0}^\infty M e^{\sigma_0 t} e^{-\sigma t} \, dt = M \int_0^\infty e^{-(\sigma - \sigma_0)t} \, dt = \frac{M}{\sigma - \sigma_0} < \infty$$

This upper bound converges if and only if the real damping $\sigma = \operatorname{Re}(s)$ is strictly greater than the signal's natural growth rate $\sigma_0$. The vertical half-plane of all complex frequencies $s$ for which the integral converges absolutely is called the **Region of Convergence (ROC)**:

$$\operatorname{ROC} = \left\{ s \in \mathbb{C} \;\middle|\; \operatorname{Re}(s) = \sigma > \sigma_0 \right\}$$

where the threshold $\sigma_0$ is the **abscissa of absolute convergence** (located at the real part of the rightmost pole of $F(s)$).

Using this integral definition, let us derive the fundamental transform pairs that build every rational transfer function:
- For the **Dirac impulse** $\delta(t)$ concentrated at $t = 0$ (which lies strictly inside $[0^-, \infty)$), the sifting property evaluates $e^{-st}$ at $t = 0$:
  $$\mathcal{L}\{\delta(t)\} = \int_{0^-}^\infty \delta(t) e^{-st} \, dt = e^{-s(0)} = 1, \qquad \operatorname{ROC} = \mathbb{C}$$
- For the **causal exponential** $f(t) = e^{at} \cdot 1(t)$ ($a \in \mathbb{C}$), integrating directly for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > \operatorname{Re}(a)$ yields:
  $$\mathcal{L}\{e^{at} \cdot 1(t)\} = \int_0^\infty e^{-(s - a)t} \, dt = \left[ -\frac{1}{s - a} e^{-(s - a)t} \right]_0^\infty = 0 - \left(-\frac{1}{s - a}\right) = \frac{1}{s - a}$$
  Setting $a = 0$ gives the **Heaviside unit step** transform $\mathcal{L}\{1(t)\} = \frac{1}{s}$ for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 0$.
- For **sinusoidal harmonics**, substituting Euler's identities $\cos(\omega t) = \frac{e^{j\omega t} + e^{-j\omega t}}{2}$ and $\sin(\omega t) = \frac{e^{j\omega t} - e^{-j\omega t}}{2j}$ into the exponential pair with $a = \pm j\omega$ gives:
  $$\mathcal{L}\{\cos(\omega t) \cdot 1(t)\} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{s - j\omega} + \frac{1}{s + j\omega}\right) = \frac{s}{s^2 + \omega^2}, \qquad \mathcal{L}\{\sin(\omega t) \cdot 1(t)\} = \frac{1}{2j}\left(\frac{1}{s - j\omega} - \frac{1}{s + j\omega}\right) = \frac{\omega}{s^2 + \omega^2}$$
  Notice that cosine has $s$ in the numerator (because $\cos(0) = 1$ requires high-frequency behavior $s/s^2 = 1/s$), whereas sine has the constant $\omega$ in the numerator (because $\sin(0) = 0$).

### Linearity and Shifting Theorems

Rather than computing improper integrals for every composite signal, operational theorems translate time delays, exponential envelopes, and linear combinations into immediate algebraic rules in the $s$-domain.

Because integration is a linear operator, the Laplace transform satisfies **superposition (linearity)** for any scalar weights $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}$:

$$\mathcal{L}\{\alpha f_1(t) + \beta f_2(t)\} = \int_{0^-}^\infty \big(\alpha f_1(t) + \beta f_2(t)\big) e^{-st} \, dt = \alpha F_1(s) + \beta F_2(s)$$

When a causal waveform $f(t)1(t)$ is delayed by $\tau > 0$ seconds—for example, across a digital communication buffer or fluid pipe—it becomes $f(t - \tau) 1(t - \tau)$, which is zero for $t < \tau$. Changing the integration variable to $\sigma = t - \tau$ (so $t = \sigma + \tau$ and $dt = d\sigma$) yields the **Time Translation (Pure Delay) Theorem**:

$$\mathcal{L}\{f(t - \tau) 1(t - \tau)\} = \int_\tau^\infty f(t - \tau) e^{-st} \, dt = \int_0^\infty f(\sigma) e^{-s(\sigma + \tau)} \, d\sigma = e^{-s\tau} \int_0^\infty f(\sigma) e^{-s\sigma} \, d\sigma = e^{-s\tau} F(s)$$

Delaying a signal by $\tau$ seconds in the time domain therefore multiplies its Laplace transform by the non-rational phase factor $e^{-s\tau}$.

Dually, multiplying a signal $f(t)$ by an exponential damping or growth factor $e^{at}$ in the time domain combines the exponents $e^{at}e^{-st} = e^{-(s-a)t}$ inside the integral, yielding the **Frequency Translation Theorem**:

$$\mathcal{L}\{e^{at} f(t)\} = \int_{0^-}^\infty f(t) e^{-(s - a)t} \, dt = F(s - a)$$

Geometrically, multiplying a waveform by a decaying envelope $e^{-\alpha t}$ ($a = -\alpha < 0$) replaces $s$ with $(s + \alpha)$ in $F(s)$, shifting every pole of $F(s)$ to the left by $\alpha$ in the complex $s$-plane—which turns undamped $\frac{s}{s^2+\omega^2}$ and $\frac{\omega}{s^2+\omega^2}$ immediately into the damped cosine $\frac{s+\alpha}{(s+\alpha)^2+\omega^2}$ and damped sine $\frac{\omega}{(s+\alpha)^2+\omega^2}$.

### Differentiation and Integration

The central power of the Laplace transform is that it reduces time differentiation and time integration to algebraic multiplication and division by $s$. Let us derive the **Time Differentiation Theorem** by integrating $\int_{0^-}^\infty \dot{f}(t) e^{-st}\,dt$ by parts, choosing $u = e^{-st}$ ($du = -s e^{-st}\,dt$) and $dv = \dot{f}(t)\,dt$ ($v = f(t)$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}\{\dot{f}(t)\} &= \int_{0^-}^\infty \dot{f}(t) e^{-st} \, dt = \left[ f(t) e^{-st} \right]_{0^-}^\infty - \int_{0^-}^\infty f(t) (-s e^{-st}) \, dt \\
&= \left( \lim_{t \to \infty} f(t) e^{-st} - f(0^-) e^{-s(0)} \right) + s \int_{0^-}^\infty f(t) e^{-st} \, dt = s F(s) - f(0^-)
\end{aligned}
$$

Inside the Region of Convergence ($\operatorname{Re}(s) > \sigma_0$), the upper boundary limit $\lim_{t\to\infty} f(t)e^{-st}$ vanishes identically, leaving $sF(s)$ minus the initial condition $f(0^-)$ stored at $t = 0^-$. To find the transform of the second derivative $\ddot{f}(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\big(\dot{f}(t)\big)$, we apply the first-derivative formula to $g(t) = \dot{f}(t)$ and substitute $G(s) = sF(s) - f(0^-)$:

$$\mathcal{L}\{\ddot{f}(t)\} = s G(s) - g(0^-) = s\big(s F(s) - f(0^-)\big) - \dot{f}(0^-) = s^2 F(s) - s f(0^-) - \dot{f}(0^-)$$

Repeating this induction $n$ times yields the general **$n$-th Order Time Differentiation Theorem**:

$$\mathcal{L}\{f^{(n)}(t)\} = s^n F(s) - s^{n-1} f(0^-) - s^{n-2} \dot{f}(0^-) - \dots - s f^{(n-2)}(0^-) - f^{(n-1)}(0^-) = s^n F(s) - \sum_{k=1}^n s^{n-k} f^{(k-1)}(0^-)$$

When all initial conditions at $t = 0^-$ are zero, $n$-th order differentiation simplifies purely to multiplication by $s^n$.

Conversely, to transform a causal **time integral** $g(t) = \int_{0^-}^t f(\tau)\,d\tau$, note that $\dot{g}(t) = f(t)$ and $g(0^-) = \int_{0^-}^{0^-} f(\tau)\,d\tau = 0$. Applying the differentiation formula gives $F(s) = \mathcal{L}\{\dot{g}(t)\} = s G(s) - g(0^-) = s G(s)$, so dividing by $s$ yields the **Time Integration Theorem**:

$$\mathcal{L}\left\{ \int_{0^-}^t f(\tau) \, d\tau \right\} = \frac{1}{s} F(s)$$

This confirms why every integrator block $\int dt$ in a state-space diagram has transfer function $\frac{1}{s}$. Finally, differentiating $F(s) = \int_{0^-}^\infty f(t)e^{-st}\,dt$ with respect to the complex variable $s$ brings down $-t$ from the exponent $e^{-st}$, establishing **Frequency Differentiation**:

$$\frac{d}{ds} F(s) = \int_{0^-}^\infty \frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(f(t) e^{-st}\right) dt = -\int_{0^-}^\infty t f(t) e^{-st} \, dt \implies \mathcal{L}\{t f(t)\} = -\frac{d}{ds} F(s)$$

Applying $-\frac{d}{ds}$ repeatedly $n$ times gives $\mathcal{L}\{t^n f(t)\} = (-1)^n \frac{d^n}{ds^n} F(s)$. In particular, starting from $\mathcal{L}\{1(t)\} = \frac{1}{s}$, differentiating $n$ times gives $\mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{t^n}{n!} 1(t)\right\} = \frac{1}{s^{n+1}}$, and shifting $s \to s - a$ gives $\mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{t^n}{n!} e^{at} 1(t)\right\} = \frac{1}{(s - a)^{n+1}}$.

### Initial and Final Value

When analyzing a step or impulse response $F(s)$, we often want to know its starting value $f(0^+)$ right after $t = 0$ and its steady-state resting value $\lim_{t\to\infty} f(t)$ without performing a full partial fraction inversion. Both endpoints follow directly from taking limits of the time-differentiation identity:

$$\int_{0^-}^\infty \dot{f}(t) e^{-st} \, dt = s F(s) - f(0^-)$$

To find the initial value $f(0^+)$ (allowing for a possible jump impulse $\big[f(0^+) - f(0^-)\big]\delta(t)$ in $\dot{f}(t)$ at $t = 0$), split the integral at $t = 0^+$:

$$\int_{0^-}^{0^+} \dot{f}(t) e^{-st} \, dt + \int_{0^+}^\infty \dot{f}(t) e^{-st} \, dt = \big(f(0^+) - f(0^-)\big) + \int_{0^+}^\infty \dot{f}(t) e^{-st} \, dt = s F(s) - f(0^-)$$

Taking the limit as $s \to \infty$ (along the positive real axis) drives $e^{-st} \to 0$ for all $t > 0$, making $\int_{0^+}^\infty \dot{f}(t)e^{-st}\,dt \to 0$. Cancelling $-f(0^-)$ from both sides proves the **Initial Value Theorem (IVT)**:

$$f(0^+) = \lim_{s \to \infty} s F(s)$$

Physically, high frequencies $s \to \infty$ correspond to tiny time scales $t \to 0^+$.

To find the steady-state value as $t \to \infty$, we instead take the low-frequency limit $s \to 0$ in $\int_{0^-}^\infty \dot{f}(t)e^{-st}\,dt = sF(s) - f(0^-)$. Provided that **every pole of $sF(s)$ lies strictly in the open left half-plane** ($\operatorname{Re}(s) < 0$), $s = 0$ lies inside the Region of Convergence of $\dot{f}(t)$, allowing us to pass the limit $s \to 0$ inside the integral ($e^{-0\cdot t} = 1$):

$$\lim_{s \to 0} \big(s F(s) - f(0^-)\big) = \int_{0^-}^\infty \dot{f}(t) \, dt = \lim_{t \to \infty} f(t) - f(0^-)$$

Adding $f(0^-)$ to both sides proves the **Final Value Theorem (FVT)**:

$$\lim_{t \to \infty} f(t) = \lim_{s \to 0} s F(s) \qquad \big(\text{valid iff all poles of } sF(s) \text{ satisfy } \operatorname{Re}(p_i) < 0\big)$$

<Callout type="warning" title="Final Value Theorem Precondition">

The Final Value Theorem is **invalid** whenever $s F(s)$ has any poles on the imaginary axis or in the right half-plane. For instance, $f(t) = \sin(\omega t)$ has $F(s) = \frac{\omega}{s^2 + \omega^2}$. Evaluating $\lim_{s \to 0} s F(s) = 0$ is meaningless because $sF(s)$ has poles at $\pm j\omega$, and $\lim_{t \to \infty} \sin(\omega t)$ does not exist.

</Callout>

<Callout type="bridge" title="Robotics Bridge: Telemetry Network Latency">

In remote drone operations over wireless links, communication latency $\tau$ between the ground station and the vehicle acts as a pure transport delay. By the time translation theorem, this delay multiplies the open-loop transfer function by $e^{-s\tau}$. On the imaginary axis $s = j\omega$:

$$e^{-j\omega\tau} = 1 \cdot e^{-j\omega\tau}$$

The delay leaves gain unchanged ($|e^{-j\omega\tau}| = 1$) while adding phase lag:

$$\phi_{\text{delay}}(\omega) = -\omega \tau \text{ radians} = -\frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \omega \tau$$

Because phase lag grows linearly with $\omega$, excessive latency erodes phase margin and destabilizes position loops.

</Callout>

### Transform Procedure

1. **Apply Differentiation Rules:** Replace each derivative $y^{(k)}(t)$ with $s^k Y(s)$ minus its initial-condition polynomial evaluated at $t = 0^-$.
2. **Transform Inputs:** Convert every forcing function $u(t)$ into $U(s)$ using linearity, shifting, and standard pairs.
3. **Separate Responses:** Factor $P(s)Y(s)$ to isolate $Y_{\text{free}}(s)$ (from initial conditions) and $Y_{\text{forced}}(s)$ (from $U(s)$).
4. **Verify Endpoints:** Check $y(0^+)$ with IVT and, once LHP stability of $sY(s)$ is confirmed, verify $y(\infty)$ with FVT.

### Key Proofs

- **Time Derivative Rule:** Integrating $\int_{0^-}^\infty \dot{f}(t)e^{-st}\,dt$ by parts yields $[f(t)e^{-st}]_{0^-}^\infty + s\int_{0^-}^\infty f(t)e^{-st}\,dt = sF(s) - f(0^-)$.
- **Time Delay Rule:** Substituting $\sigma = t - \tau$ in $\int_\tau^\infty f(t-\tau)e^{-st}\,dt$ pulls out $e^{-s\tau}$ to give $e^{-s\tau}F(s)$.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistakes">

1. **Unchecked Final Value Theorem:** Computing $\lim_{s\to 0} sF(s)$ when $sF(s)$ has poles on the imaginary axis or in the RHP produces false finite numbers for oscillating or diverging signals.
2. **Dropped Initial Conditions:** Using $\mathcal{L}\{\ddot{y}\} = s^2 Y(s)$ without $-sy(0^-) - \dot{y}(0^-)$ discards the entire free response.
3. **Delay Argument Mismatch:** The delay theorem requires $f(t - \tau)1(t - \tau)$; if a function is written in terms of $t$ instead of $(t-\tau)$, rewrite it in shifted form before pulling out $e^{-s\tau}$.

</Callout>

   F_1(s) &= 3\mathcal{L}\{e^{-2t}1(t)\} - 5\mathcal{L}\{e^{-4t}1(t)\} = \frac{3}{s + 2} - \frac{5}{s + 4} \\
   &= \frac{3(s + 4) - 5(s + 2)}{(s + 2)(s + 4)} = \frac{3s + 12 - 5s - 10}{(s + 2)(s + 4)} = \frac{-2s + 2}{(s + 2)(s + 4)}
   \end{aligned}
   $$
   Setting the numerator $-2s + 2 = 0$ shows a real right-half-plane zero at $s = 1$, with Region of Convergence $\operatorname{Re}(s) > -2$.
2. Starting from the unit ramp $g(t) = t \cdot 1(t) \leftrightarrow G(s) = \frac{1}{s^2}$ and applying the frequency translation theorem $\mathcal{L}\{e^{-3t}g(t)\} = G(s + 3)$:
   $$F_2(s) = \mathcal{L}\{t e^{-3t} 1(t)\} = \left. \frac{1}{u^2} \right|_{u = s + 3} = \frac{1}{(s + 3)^2}, \qquad \operatorname{ROC}: \operatorname{Re}(s) > -3$$
3. Expanding $\cos(4t) = \frac{e^{j4t} + e^{-j4t}}{2}$ via Euler's identity and transforming each complex exponential term:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   F_3(s) &= \frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}\{e^{j4t}1(t)\} + \frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}\{e^{-j4t}1(t)\} = \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{1}{s - j4} + \frac{1}{s + j4} \right) \\
   &= \frac{1}{2}\frac{(s + j4) + (s - j4)}{(s - j4)(s + j4)} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{2s}{s^2 - (j4)^2} = \frac{s}{s^2 + 16}, \qquad \operatorname{ROC}: \operatorname{Re}(s) > 0
   \end{aligned}
   $$

1. Identify the unshifted base waveform $f(t) = t^2 \cdot 1(t) = 2 \cdot \frac{t^2}{2!} 1(t)$, whose Laplace transform is $F(s) = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{s^{2+1}} = \frac{2}{s^3}$.    \end{aligned}
