## Introduction

Any physical system whose present behavior depends on its past history—whether a car suspension compressing over a bump, a capacitor holding charge, or a spinning motor rotor—stores energy internally. Knowing only the current external input is not enough to predict what happens next; you also need a compact snapshot of that stored energy right now. That snapshot is the **state vector**, and rewriting physical laws as first-order differential equations in that state gives us one uniform matrix template ($\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{D}$) across mechanics, circuits, and robotics.

In real engineering systems—such as a two-mass vehicle suspension model, a quadrotor altitude axis, or a DC motor drive—the state variables map directly to physical energy reservoirs (positions, velocities, currents, and voltages). Once translated into first-order state equations, every downstream tool in this course (Laplace inversion, stability tests, Kalman rank, and feedback synthesis) works identically without caring whether the hardware is mechanical or electrical.

### Coupled Two-Cart System

Before introducing general matrix definitions, let us build a state-space model from first principles on a concrete mechanical bench. High-order differential equations become cumbersome as soon as two moving bodies interact, because each mass accelerates in response to forces exerted by its neighbors. Converting those coupled second-order equations into a single vector of first-order equations gives us a clean, systematic way to track every energy-storing element at once.

Consider the horizontal translational system in [the figure](#fig-two-mass-system): two rigid carts of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ slide on a frictionless rail and are coupled by a linear spring of stiffness $k$. Mass $m_1$ is tethered to a fixed left wall through a viscous damper of coefficient $B_1$ and is driven to the right by an external horizontal control force $F(t) = u(t)$.

</figure>

Let $p_1(t)$ and $p_2(t)$ denote the horizontal displacements of $m_1$ and $m_2$ measured to the right from their static unstretched equilibrium positions. To derive the equations of motion, we isolate each cart as a free body and sum all horizontal forces according to Newton's second law ($\sum F = m\ddot{p}$).

For **mass $m_1$**, three horizontal forces act on the cart: the external actuator force $+u(t)$ pushing right, the wall damper resisting velocity with force $-B_1 \dot{p}_1(t)$, and the coupling spring between the two carts. Whenever cart 2 moves farther right than cart 1 ($p_2(t) > p_1(t)$), the spring stretches by $(p_2(t) - p_1(t))$ and pulls cart 1 to the right with force $+k\big(p_2(t) - p_1(t)\big)$. Summing these three forces and dividing through by $m_1$ isolates the acceleration $\ddot{p}_1(t)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_1 \ddot{p}_1(t) &= u(t) - B_1 \dot{p}_1(t) + k\big(p_2(t) - p_1(t)\big) \\
\ddot{p}_1(t) &= -\frac{k}{m_1} p_1(t) - \frac{B_1}{m_1} \dot{p}_1(t) + \frac{k}{m_1} p_2(t) + \frac{1}{m_1} u(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

In this acceleration balance, $-\frac{k}{m_1}p_1(t)$ is the elastic restoring pull toward equilibrium, $-\frac{B_1}{m_1}\dot{p}_1(t)$ dissipates kinetic energy into heat in the wall damper, $+\frac{k}{m_1}p_2(t)$ transmits elastic force from the second cart, and $+\frac{1}{m_1}u(t)$ is the direct acceleration produced by the external control force.

For **mass $m_2$**, no external actuator or wall damper touches the cart; the only horizontal force is the coupling spring pulling in the opposite direction (Newton's third law) with force $-k\big(p_2(t) - p_1(t)\big)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_2 \ddot{p}_2(t) &= -k\big(p_2(t) - p_1(t)\big) = k p_1(t) - k p_2(t) \\
\ddot{p}_2(t) &= \frac{k}{m_2} p_1(t) - \frac{k}{m_2} p_2(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

Because cart 2 has no direct damper attached to it, its acceleration $\ddot{p}_2(t)$ is proportional purely to the relative displacement $p_1(t) - p_2(t)$ across the coupling spring, scaled by its reciprocal inertia $1/m_2$.

Notice that the physics is governed by two coupled second-order differential equations. Each cart stores both **potential energy** (via its displacement compressing or stretching the spring) and **kinetic energy** (via its translational velocity), giving $n = 4$ independent energy reservoirs in total. To reduce these two second-order equations to four first-order equations, we assign one state variable to each position and velocity:

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \\ x_3(t) \\ x_4(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} p_1(t) \\ \dot{p}_1(t) \\ p_2(t) \\ \dot{p}_2(t) \end{bmatrix}
$$

Here $x_1$ and $x_3$ track the cart positions (in meters), while $x_2$ and $x_4$ track the cart velocities (in meters per second). Differentiating each component of $\mathbf{x}(t)$ with respect to time links the position derivatives directly to the velocity states ($\dot{x}_1 = \dot{p}_1 = x_2$ and $\dot{x}_3 = \dot{p}_2 = x_4$) and substitutes our two Newton acceleration equations for $\dot{x}_2 = \ddot{p}_1$ and $\dot{x}_4 = \ddot{p}_2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{x}_1(t) &= x_2(t) \\
\dot{x}_2(t) &= -\frac{k}{m_1} x_1(t) - \frac{B_1}{m_1} x_2(t) + \frac{k}{m_1} x_3(t) + \frac{1}{m_1} u(t) \\
\dot{x}_3(t) &= x_4(t) \\
\dot{x}_4(t) &= \frac{k}{m_2} x_1(t) - \frac{k}{m_2} x_3(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

Every time derivative on the left-hand side is now first-order, and every right-hand side is a purely linear combination of the four current state variables $x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4$ and the input $u(t)$. Collecting the coefficients of $x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4$ row by row into a $4 \times 4$ matrix $\mathbf{A}$ and the coefficients of $u(t)$ into a $4 \times 1$ column vector $\mathbf{B}$ casts the entire mechanical bench into the standard state equation $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B} u(t)$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \dot{x}_1(t) \\ \dot{x}_2(t) \\ \dot{x}_3(t) \\ \dot{x}_4(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
-\frac{k}{m_1} & -\frac{B_1}{m_1} & \frac{k}{m_1} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
\frac{k}{m_2} & 0 & -\frac{k}{m_2} & 0
\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \\ x_3(t) \\ x_4(t) \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \frac{1}{m_1} \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} u(t)
$$

In this matrix layout, the $1$ entries in rows 1 and 3 enforce the kinematic identity that velocity is the derivative of position, while rows 2 and 4 encode Newton's force balances on $m_1$ and $m_2$. Because the external force $u(t)$ pushes directly on mass $m_1$, the input column $\mathbf{B}$ is non-zero only in row 2 (the acceleration of cart 1).

Now let us substitute concrete bench values: $m_1 = 1\text{ kg}$, $m_2 = 2\text{ kg}$, $k = 4\text{ N/m}$, and $B_1 = 3\text{ N}\cdot\text{s/m}$. Evaluating each physical ratio explicitly gives $\frac{k}{m_1} = \frac{4}{1} = 4\text{ s}^{-2}$, $\frac{B_1}{m_1} = \frac{3}{1} = 3\text{ s}^{-1}$, $\frac{1}{m_1} = 1\text{ kg}^{-1}$, and $\frac{k}{m_2} = \frac{4}{2} = 2\text{ s}^{-2}$, yielding the numerical state equation:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
-4 & -3 & 4 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
2 & 0 & -2 & 0
\end{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}(t) + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} u(t)
$$

Notice how the coupling between the two carts appears as the off-diagonal entries $A_{23} = +4$ (cart 2 pulling cart 1) and $A_{41} = +2$ (cart 1 pulling cart 2), weighted inversely by their respective masses.

While $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ capture the internal mechanics of the carts, the **output equation** $y(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}(t) + D u(t)$ depends entirely on which physical sensor we bolt onto the rail. If an optical encoder measures **cart 2 position** ($y(t) = p_2(t) = x_3(t)$), we pick out the third state with $\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. If a tachometer measures **cart 2 velocity** ($y(t) = \dot{p}_2(t) = x_4(t)$), we use $\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. If a strain gauge measures **spring elongation** ($y(t) = p_2(t) - p_1(t) = -x_1(t) + x_3(t)$), the output row becomes $\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. In all three sensor configurations, an applied force produces finite acceleration that must be integrated over time before velocity or position can change, so $u(t)$ cannot jump instantaneously to $y(t)$ and the feedthrough scalar is $D = 0$.

### Dynamical State Definition

A single high-order differential equation linking one input $u(t)$ to one output $y(t)$ treats the system as a sealed black box: it hides internal forces between components, requires clumsy reconfiguration whenever we add a second actuator or sensor, and buries initial stored energy inside high-order output derivatives at $t = 0^-$. To analyze any dynamical system with a single universal toolkit, we need a minimal internal summary that captures all stored energy, matter, or information at a given instant.

Formally, the **state** $\mathbf{x}(t_0)$ of a dynamical system at initial time $t_0$ is the minimal vector of internal variables $\{x_1(t_0), x_2(t_0), \dots, x_n(t_0)\}$ such that knowing $\mathbf{x}(t_0)$ together with the future input trajectory $\mathbf{u}(t)$ for all $t \ge t_0$ uniquely determines the state trajectory $\mathbf{x}(t)$ and sensor output $\mathbf{y}(t)$ for all $t \ge t_0$. In physical terms, the state vector summarizes the entire past history $t < t_0$ of the hardware; once $\mathbf{x}(t_0)$ is specified, how the system reached that state no longer affects its future motion.

For continuous-time, linear, time-invariant (LTI) systems, the differential laws governing those $n$ state variables and the algebraic readout of the $p$ sensors take the standard state-space form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) &= \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}(t) \\
\mathbf{y}(t) &= \mathbf{C} \mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{D} \mathbf{u}(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is the **state differential equation**, which tells us the instantaneous velocity $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)$ of the state vector as the sum of internal restoring/damping forces ($\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t)$) and external actuator forces ($\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t)$). The second line is the **output equation**, an algebraic (memoryless) projection that combines the current internal state ($\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}(t)$) with any direct input feedthrough ($\mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}(t)$) to form the measured output $\mathbf{y}(t)$.

Specifically, the **state vector** $\mathbf{x}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ fixes the system **order** $n$, while $\mathbf{u}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^m$ stacks the $m$ control inputs and $\mathbf{y}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^p$ stacks the $p$ sensor outputs. The **dynamic matrix** $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ governs autonomous internal coupling between states, and the **input matrix** $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ distributes actuator effort into each state derivative. On the sensor side, the **output matrix** $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times n}$ weights internal states into measured channels, while the **feedthrough matrix** $\mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times m}$ represents any instantaneous algebraic path from input to output (which is $\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{0}$ in physical plants with mass, inertia, or inductance).

### Integrator Block Topology

Why are the outputs of integrators the natural state variables of any continuous-time circuit or mechanical simulation? Because an integrator $\int_{t_0}^t (\cdot)\,d\tau$ accumulates its input over time, it is the fundamental continuous-time memory element: you cannot predict an integrator's output without knowing its initial stored value $x_i(t_0)$. Rewriting an $n$-th order differential system in first-order state-space form isolates all time memory inside a bank of $n$ parallel scalar integrators.

As shown in [the figure](#fig-state-space-architecture), the signal entering the integrator bank is the derivative vector $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t)$, and the signal emerging from the integrator bank is the state vector $\mathbf{x}(t)$ itself. The four surrounding matrix blocks ($\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{D}$) contain zero memory—they act as pure instantaneous gain networks that route signals around the integrator core.

### Phase-Variable Companion Form

In many control problems, a plant is specified not by a multi-body drawing, but by a single $n$-th order scalar input-output differential equation. To apply state-space tools (such as resolvent inversion or pole placement) to that scalar ODE, we need a systematic algorithm that converts any $n$-th order equation into an $n$-dimensional state realization $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{D})$ by inspection.

Consider a normalized single-input single-output (SISO) $n$-th order differential equation with no input derivatives on the right-hand side, where we have already divided through by the leading coefficient so that $y^{(n)}(t)$ has coefficient $1$:

$$y^{(n)}(t) + a_{n-1} y^{(n-1)}(t) + \dots + a_1 \dot{y}(t) + a_0 y(t) = b_0 u(t)$$

Here $y^{(k)}(t) = \frac{d^k y(t)}{dt^k}$ denotes the $k$-th time derivative of the output, and $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_{n-1}, b_0$ are constant real coefficients. We construct the **phase-variable state vector** by chaining the output $y(t)$ and its first $n - 1$ time derivatives in ascending order:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_1(t) &= y(t) \\
x_2(t) &= \dot{y}(t) = \dot{x}_1(t) \\
x_3(t) &= \ddot{y}(t) = \dot{x}_2(t) \\
&\;\;\vdots \\
x_n(t) &= y^{(n-1)}(t) = \dot{x}_{n-1}(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

By this chained definition, the derivative of each state $x_k(t)$ for $k = 1, 2, \dots, n - 1$ is simply the very next state variable $x_{k+1}(t)$. Only the last state $x_n(t) = y^{(n-1)}(t)$ differentiates into $y^{(n)}(t)$, which we express in terms of $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and $u(t)$ by moving all lower-derivative terms in the original ODE to the right-hand side:

$$\dot{x}_n(t) = y^{(n)}(t) = -a_0 x_1(t) - a_1 x_2(t) - \dots - a_{n-1} x_n(t) + b_0 u(t)$$

Notice that the coefficients $-a_0, -a_1, \dots, -a_{n-1}$ appear negated and ordered from lowest derivative ($x_1 = y$) to highest state derivative ($x_n = y^{(n-1)}$). Stacking the $n - 1$ kinematic chain equations above this final dynamic row produces the **Frobenius Companion Form**:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \dot{x}_1(t) \\ \dot{x}_2(t) \\ \vdots \\ \dot{x}_{n-1}(t) \\ \dot{x}_n(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1 & \dots & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 1 \\
-a_0 & -a_1 & -a_2 & \dots & -a_{n-1}
\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \\ \vdots \\ x_{n-1}(t) \\ x_n(t) \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \\ b_0 \end{bmatrix} u(t)
$$

Because our first phase variable was chosen as $x_1(t) = y(t)$, the companion output equation simply reads out the first component of $\mathbf{x}(t)$ with zero feedthrough:

$$y(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}(t)$$

Geometrically, the first $n - 1$ rows of the companion matrix $\mathbf{A}$ form a pure shift-register chain of integrators (with $1$s along the superdiagonal and zeros everywhere else), while the bottom row closes the feedback loop from all $n$ states back into the highest derivative $\dot{x}_n(t)$.

<Callout type="bridge" title="Robotics Bridge: Multirotor Pitch Dynamics">

In aerial robotics autopilots (such as PX4), quadcopter pitch dynamics around hover equilibrium follow:

$$J_{yy} \ddot{\theta}(t) + d_r \dot{\theta}(t) = l \Delta T(t)$$

where $J_{yy}$ is pitch moment of inertia, $d_r$ is rotational damping, $l$ is the rotor arm length, and $u(t) = \Delta T(t)$ is differential thrust. Assigning $x_1 = \theta$ (pitch angle) and $x_2 = \dot{\theta} = q$ (pitch rate) yields:

$$\begin{bmatrix} \dot{x}_1 \\ \dot{x}_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -\frac{d_r}{J_{yy}} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \frac{l}{J_{yy}} \end{bmatrix} u(t)$$

This two-state model underlies inner angular-rate controllers and Extended Kalman Filters (EKF) in ROS 2 flight stacks.

</Callout>

### Coordinate Non-Uniqueness

While the external input-output behavior ($u(t) \to y(t)$) of a physical machine is fixed by nature, its internal state vector $\mathbf{x}(t)$ is **not unique**. In our two-cart example, we chose absolute displacements $p_1$ and $p_2$ as state variables, but another engineer might choose cart 1 position $p_1$ and spring stretch $\Delta p = p_2 - p_1$, or measure positions in millimeters instead of meters. Both choices describe the exact same physical carts, yet they produce different numbers inside $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C})$.

To formalize how any two valid state representations of the same system are related, let $\mathbf{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ be a constant invertible matrix ($\det(\mathbf{T}) \neq 0$) that maps a new coordinate vector $\mathbf{z}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ onto the original state vector $\mathbf{x}(t)$:

$$\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{T} \mathbf{z}(t) \iff \mathbf{z}(t) = \mathbf{T}^{-1} \mathbf{x}(t)$$

Here the columns of $\mathbf{T}$ act as the new basis vectors expressed in the original coordinates. Because $\mathbf{T}$ is constant in time, differentiating $\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}(t)$ yields $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{T}\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t)$. Substituting $\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}(t)$ and $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{T}\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t)$ into the original state-space equations gives:

$$\mathbf{T}\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}(t) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t), \qquad \mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}(t) + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}(t)$$

Premultiplying the differential equation on the left by $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ cancels $\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{I}$ on the left-hand side and isolates $\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t)$:

$$\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t) = \underbrace{\left(\mathbf{T}^{-1} \mathbf{A} \mathbf{T}\right)}_{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}} \mathbf{z}(t) + \underbrace{\left(\mathbf{T}^{-1} \mathbf{B}\right)}_{\tilde{\mathbf{B}}} \mathbf{u}(t), \qquad \mathbf{y}(t) = \underbrace{\left(\mathbf{C} \mathbf{T}\right)}_{\tilde{\mathbf{C}}} \mathbf{z}(t) + \underbrace{\mathbf{D}}_{\tilde{\mathbf{D}}} \mathbf{u}(t)$$

The transformed quadruple $(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}, \tilde{\mathbf{B}}, \tilde{\mathbf{C}}, \tilde{\mathbf{D}}) = (\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{D})$ is completely equivalent to $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{D})$: $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}$ expresses how inputs enter the new $\mathbf{z}$ coordinates, $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ evolves $\mathbf{z}(t)$ internally, and $\tilde{\mathbf{C}}$ translates $\mathbf{z}(t)$ back into the physical sensor output $\mathbf{y}(t)$. In [Coordinate Basis Similarity Transformations](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/01-state-models/02-coordinate-basis-similarity-transformations/), we will prove that this transformation leaves both the system eigenvalues and the input-output transfer function strictly invariant.

<Divider label="3 · Summary" />

| Matrix / Vector | Dimension | Physical & Structural Role |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **State Vector $\mathbf{x}(t)$** | $n \times 1$ | Integrator outputs capturing stored energy (positions, velocities, currents, voltages) |
| **Dynamic Matrix $\mathbf{A}$** | $n \times n$ | Internal coupling among state variables; off-diagonal zeros indicate decoupled modes |
| **Input Matrix $\mathbf{B}$** | $n \times m$ | Maps actuator inputs $\mathbf{u}(t)$ into state derivatives $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)$ |
| **Output Matrix $\mathbf{C}$** | $p \times n$ | Projects internal states onto measured sensor channels $\mathbf{y}(t)$ |
| **Feedthrough Matrix $\mathbf{D}$** | $p \times m$ | Direct instantaneous algebraic path from $\mathbf{u}$ to $\mathbf{y}$ ($0$ in inertial plants) |

### Modeling Procedure

1. **Count Energy Reservoirs:** Identify each independent energy-storing degree of freedom to fix the system order $n$ (e.g., two translating masses give $n = 4$ states).
2. **Write Physical Laws:** Apply Newton's second law or Kirchhoff's laws to obtain coupled differential equations free of higher derivatives on the right-hand side.
3. **Assign State Variables:** Pick physical states (positions/velocities) or phase variables ($x_1 = y, x_2 = \dot{y}, \dots, x_n = y^{(n-1)}$) and express every $\dot{x}_i$ as a linear combination of $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{u}$.
4. **Extract Matrices:** Read off $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ row by row from $\dot{\mathbf{x}}$, then read $\mathbf{C}$ and $\mathbf{D}$ from the sensor output expression $\mathbf{y}$.

### Key Proofs

- **Companion Matrix Construction:** Differentiating $x_k = y^{(k-1)}$ yields $\dot{x}_k = x_{k+1}$ for $k < n$, while the $n$-th row isolates $y^{(n)}$ with negated coefficients $[-a_0, -a_1, \dots, -a_{n-1}]$.
- **Coordinate Equivalence:** Substituting $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}$ with $\det(\mathbf{T}) \neq 0$ leaves the input-output trajectory $\mathbf{y}(t)$ unchanged under $(\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{D})$.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistakes">

1. **Reversed Companion Order:** Putting $[-a_{n-1}, \dots, -a_0]$ instead of $[-a_0, -a_1, \dots, -a_{n-1}]$ in the bottom row of $\mathbf{A}$ flips the characteristic polynomial. Always match each column to $[x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n]$.
2. **Spring Force Sign Errors:** When cart 2 moves farther than cart 1 ($p_2 > p_1$), the coupling spring pulls cart 1 forward ($+k(p_2 - p_1)$) and cart 2 backward ($-k(p_2 - p_1)$).
3. **Forgetting to Normalize Highest Derivative:** Before reading companion coefficients from $c_n y^{(n)} + \dots + c_0 y = d_0 u$, divide through by $c_n$ so the coefficient of $y^{(n)}$ is $1$.

</Callout>

<Exercise client:load id="ex-1-1" title="Exercise 1.1 — Decoupled First-Order Equations" level="Warm-Up">

Consider a system with two independent state variables and a single input $u(t)$:

$$\dot{x}_1(t) = -3 x_1(t) + 2 u(t)$$

$$\dot{x}_2(t) = -7 x_2(t) - u(t)$$

with observed output:

$$y(t) = x_1(t) + 4 x_2(t)$$

Consider the coupled dynamic system:

<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Reading the coefficients of $x_1(t), x_2(t), u(t)$ directly from the two first-order state equations and the output equation:
   $$\begin{bmatrix} \dot{x}_1(t) \\ \dot{x}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 4 \\ -1 & -5 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} \begin{bmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \end{bmatrix} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{B}} u(t)$$
   $$y(t) = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{C}} \begin{bmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \end{bmatrix} + \underbrace{[0.5]}_{\mathbf{D}} u(t)$$

If $u(t)$ jumps by $\Delta u$ at $t = 0$, the output $y(t)$ immediately jumps by $0.5\Delta u$ at $t = 0^+$ before the state variables $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ have had time to change.
3. In the integrator block diagram topology:
   - Two parallel integrator blocks $\int dt$ take inputs $\dot{x}_1(t)$ and $\dot{x}_2(t)$ and produce the state outputs $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$.
   - The input $u(t)$ branches into three paths: through gain $B_1 = 1$ into the $\dot{x}_1$ summing junction, through gain $B_2 = 3$ into the $\dot{x}_2$ summing junction, and through the direct feedthrough gain $D = 0.5$ straight to the output summing junction.
   - Internal state feedback and cross-coupling: $x_1(t)$ feeds back to $\dot{x}_1$ with self-damping gain $A_{11} = -2$ and couples into $\dot{x}_2$ with gain $A_{21} = -1$; meanwhile $x_2(t)$ feeds back to $\dot{x}_2$ with self-damping gain $A_{22} = -5$ and couples into $\dot{x}_1$ with gain $A_{12} = +4$.
   - The output summer adds $C_1 x_1(t) = 2x_1(t)$, $C_2 x_2(t) = -x_2(t)$, and $D u(t) = 0.5u(t)$ to form $y(t)$.

</details>

<Exercise client:load id="ex-1-3" title="Exercise 1.3 — Second-Order Companion Form" level="Intermediate">

A physical system governed by Newton's law with viscous friction is described by:

$$\ddot{y}(t) + 5\dot{y}(t) + 6 y(t) = 2 u(t)$$

1. Choose phase canonical state variables $x_1(t) = y(t)$ and $x_2(t) = \dot{y}(t)$.
2. Express $\dot{x}_1(t)$ in terms of $x_2(t)$.
3. Express $\dot{x}_2(t)$ in terms of $x_1(t)$, $x_2(t)$, and $u(t)$.
4. Construct the standard companion matrices $\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{D}$. Notice how the coefficients of the ODE appear directly in the bottom row of $\mathbf{A}$.

<StepCheck
  client:load
  prompt="In the companion matrix $\mathbf{A}$, what is entry $A_{21}$ (multiplying $x_1 = y$)?"
  expected={-6}
  tolerance={0.05}
  unit=""
  explanation="Isolating $\ddot{y} = -6y - 5\dot{y} + 2u$ gives $\dot{x}_2 = -6x_1 - 5x_2 + 2u$, so $A_{21} = -6$."
/>

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. The differential equation is already normalized so that the highest derivative $\ddot{y}(t)$ has leading coefficient $1$, with $a_1 = 5$, $a_0 = 6$, and $b_0 = 2$. We assign the phase variables in ascending derivative order:
   $$x_1(t) = y(t), \qquad x_2(t) = \dot{y}(t)$$
2. Differentiating $x_1(t)$ once links it directly to $x_2(t)$:
   $$\dot{x}_1(t) = \dot{y}(t) = (0)x_1(t) + (1)x_2(t) + (0)u(t)$$
3. Differentiating $x_2(t)$ gives $\dot{x}_2(t) = \ddot{y}(t)$. Solving the scalar ODE for $\ddot{y}(t)$ and substituting $y(t) = x_1(t)$ and $\dot{y}(t) = x_2(t)$ yields:
   $$\dot{x}_2(t) = \ddot{y}(t) = -6 y(t) - 5\dot{y}(t) + 2u(t) = -6 x_1(t) - 5 x_2(t) + 2u(t)$$
4. Packing the two scalar equations and the output read-out $y(t) = x_1(t)$ into matrix form produces the second-order Frobenius companion realization:
   $$\begin{bmatrix} \dot{x}_1(t) \\ \dot{x}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -6 & -5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} u(t)$$
   $$y(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \end{bmatrix} + [0] u(t)$$
   Thus $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -6 & -5 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, and $D = 0$, with the bottom row of $\mathbf{A}$ holding $[-a_0, -a_1] = [-6, -5]$.

<Exercise client:load id="ex-1-4" title="Exercise 1.4 — Third-Order Companion Form" level="Exam Standard">

A continuous-time dynamic process is described by the third-order scalar differential equation:

$$\dddot{y}(t) + 6\ddot{y}(t) + 11\dot{y}(t) + 6 y(t) = 4 u(t)$$

1. Select phase variables $x_1(t) = y(t)$, $x_2(t) = \dot{y}(t)$, and $x_3(t) = \ddot{y}(t)$ to transform the scalar ODE into a set of first-order differential equations.
2. Formulate the Frobenius companion canonical state-space model:
   $$\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B} u(t), \quad y(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}(t) + D u(t)$$
3. Identify the state matrices $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$, $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 1}$, $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 3}$, and $D \in \mathbb{R}$.
4. Verify the state dimension $n$ and explain why $D = 0$ for this physical system.

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. The third-order ODE is normalized (coefficient of $\dddot{y}(t)$ is $1$) with parameters $a_0 = 6$, $a_1 = 11$, $a_2 = 6$, and $b_0 = 4$. Defining the three phase variables:
   $$x_1(t) = y(t), \qquad x_2(t) = \dot{y}(t), \qquad x_3(t) = \ddot{y}(t)$$
   Differentiating $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ gives the two kinematic shift equations:
   $$\dot{x}_1(t) = x_2(t), \qquad \dot{x}_2(t) = x_3(t)$$
   Differentiating $x_3(t)$ gives $\dot{x}_3(t) = \dddot{y}(t)$, which we isolate from the third-order ODE and rewrite in terms of $x_1, x_2, x_3$:
   $$\dot{x}_3(t) = \dddot{y}(t) = -6 y(t) - 11\dot{y}(t) - 6\ddot{y}(t) + 4u(t) = -6 x_1(t) - 11 x_2(t) - 6 x_3(t) + 4 u(t)$$
2. Stacking the three state equations and the output identity $y(t) = x_1(t)$ into matrix form yields:
   $$\begin{bmatrix} \dot{x}_1(t) \\ \dot{x}_2(t) \\ \dot{x}_3(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ -6 & -11 & -6 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \\ x_3(t) \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} u(t)$$
   $$y(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \\ x_3(t) \end{bmatrix} + [0] u(t)$$
3. The extracted companion matrices are:
   $$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ -6 & -11 & -6 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = 0$$
4. The state dimension is $n = 3$, matching the order of the highest time derivative $\dddot{y}(t)$. The feedthrough scalar is $D = 0$ because the external input $u(t)$ enters only into the third derivative $\dot{x}_3 = \dddot{y}$ and must pass through three cascaded integrations before reaching $y(t) = x_1(t)$.

<Exercise client:load id="ex-1-5" title="Exercise 1.5 — Linear Coordinate Rescaling" level="Exam Standard">

Consider the system from Exercise 1.1:

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 0 \\ 0 & -7 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = 0$$

Suppose we rescale the state variables by changing units (e.g. from meters to millimeters for $x_1$, and radians to degrees for $x_2$) via the transformation $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}$ where:

$$\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Invert $\mathbf{T}$ by inspection.
2. Compute the transformed system matrices $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}$, and $\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}$.
3. Verify that $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{A}$. Explain why diagonal similarity transformations leave diagonal matrices invariant while re-weighting the input and output matrices.

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Because $\mathbf{T}$ is diagonal with non-zero diagonal entries $10$ and $2$ ($\det(\mathbf{T}) = 20 \neq 0$), its inverse is obtained by taking the reciprocal of each diagonal entry:
   $$\mathbf{T}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/10 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}$$
2. Applying the similarity transformation formulas $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}$, and $\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}$ step by step:
   $$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 0 \\ 0 & -7 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (-3)(10) + (0)(0) & (-3)(0) + (0)(2) \\ (0)(10) + (-7)(0) & (0)(0) + (-7)(2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -30 & 0 \\ 0 & -14 \end{bmatrix}$$
   $$\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}) = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -30 & 0 \\ 0 & -14 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (0.1)(-30) & 0 \\ 0 & (0.5)(-14) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 0 \\ 0 & -7 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{A}$$
   $$\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (0.1)(2) + (0)(-1) \\ (0)(2) + (0.5)(-1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 \\ -0.5 \end{bmatrix}$$
   $$\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (1)(10) + (4)(0) & (1)(0) + (4)(2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 8 \end{bmatrix}$$
3. Because any two diagonal matrices commute under multiplication ($\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{A}$), we have $\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{I}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}$, so pure unit rescaling leaves the decoupled decay rates $-3$ and $-7$ untouched. Meanwhile, dividing $B_1$ by $10$ ($\tilde{B}_1 = 0.2$) is exactly compensated by multiplying $C_1$ by $10$ ($\tilde{C}_1 = 10$), and dividing $B_2$ by $2$ ($\tilde{B}_2 = -0.5$) is compensated by multiplying $C_2$ by $2$ ($\tilde{C}_2 = 8$), keeping the input-to-output path products $\tilde{C}_1 \tilde{B}_1 = 2 = C_1 B_1$ and $\tilde{C}_2 \tilde{B}_2 = -4 = C_2 B_2$ strictly invariant.

</details>

4. What is the value of the direct feedthrough scalar $D$? Justify your answer physically.

  client:load
  prompt="For $m_1 = 2\;\mathrm{kg}$, $m_2 = 1\;\mathrm{kg}$, $k = 4\;\mathrm{N/m}$, and $B_1 = 6\;\mathrm{N\cdot s/m}$, what is the numerical value of entry $B_2 = 1/m_1$?"

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Choosing the positions and velocities of the two carts as our four energy-storing state variables:
   $$\mathbf{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \\ x_3(t) \\ x_4(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} p_1(t) \\ \dot{p}_1(t) \\ p_2(t) \\ \dot{p}_2(t) \end{bmatrix}$$

   $$\frac{k}{m_1} = \frac{4}{2} = 2\text{ s}^{-2}, \qquad \frac{B_1}{m_1} = \frac{6}{2} = 3\text{ s}^{-1}, \qquad \frac{1}{m_1} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5\text{ kg}^{-1}, \qquad \frac{k}{m_2} = \frac{4}{1} = 4\text{ s}^{-2}$$

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \dot{x}_1(t) &= x_2(t) \\
   \dot{x}_2(t) &= -2 x_1(t) - 3 x_2(t) + 2 x_3(t) + 0.5 u(t) \\
   \dot{x}_3(t) &= x_4(t) \\
   \dot{x}_4(t) &= 4 x_1(t) - 4 x_3(t)
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   $$
   \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}
   0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
   -2 & -3 & 2 & 0 \\
   0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
   4 & 0 & -4 & 0
   \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
   \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0.5 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}

   $$\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
4. The direct feedthrough scalar is $D = 0$.
