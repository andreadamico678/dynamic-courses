## Introduction

Real engineering architectures are rarely built as one monolithic block: a drone autopilot combines a guidance controller, an attitude rate loop, electronic speed controllers, propeller aerodynamics, and an IMU sensor filter wired in nested feedback and feedforward paths. Attempting to write raw differential equations for every internal wire quickly turns intractable.

**Block diagram algebra** lets you treat interconnected subsystems graphically and simplify complex multi-loop networks step by step into a single equivalent input-output transfer function $W(s)$, without altering the signals at the external terminals.

### Six-Block Loop Reduction

In [First Order Differential State Equations](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/01-state-models/01-first-order-differential-state-equations/), we saw how an individual component can be represented by its internal state equations; in large control architectures, several such components are wired together in nested loops. Before listing general reduction rules, let us reduce a realistic multi-loop architecture step by step by writing out the signal balances at each summing node.

Consider the six-block interconnected system shown in [the figure](#fig-exam-block-diagram), which pairs an inner negative feedback loop ($E$), a parallel feedforward branch ($C$), a forward cascade block ($D$), and an outer global negative feedback loop ($F$).

<figure id="fig-exam-block-diagram" className="dc-svg-wrap">
  <img src="/dynamic-courses/diagrams/systems-theory/fig-exam-block-diagram.svg" alt="Complex interconnected 6-block diagram from the UniCT examination paper." />
  <figcaption>Complex interconnected 6-block diagram from the UniCT examination paper.</figcaption>
</figure>

Our goal is to collapse the entire diagram into a single input-output ratio $W(s) = Y(s)/U(s)$ without altering the external signals $U(s)$ and $Y(s)$. Working systematically from the innermost loop outward keeps every intermediate fraction transparent.

First, examine the **inner feedback loop** between the output $R_1(s)$ of the outer summer $\Sigma_1$ and the intermediate node $V(s)$ situated right between blocks $A$ and $B$. Why track $V(s)$ explicitly? Because the feedforward branch $C$ taps off directly from $V(s)$! Tracing the signals around the inner loop: the signal entering $B$ is $V(s)$, so the output of $B$ is $Y_B(s) = B V(s)$, and the feedback signal returning through $E$ to the minus port of summer $\Sigma_2$ is $E Y_B(s) = E B V(s)$. Therefore, the error signal emerging from $\Sigma_2$ is $R_1(s) - E B V(s)$, and passing that signal through block $A$ gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V(s) &= A\big(R_1(s) - E B V(s)\big) = A R_1(s) - A B E V(s) \\
(1 + A B E) V(s) &= A R_1(s) \implies V(s) = \frac{A}{1 + A B E} R_1(s)
\end{aligned}
$$

Here the denominator $1 + ABE$ reflects the negative feedback around the inner loop of loop gain $ABE$. Multiplying $V(s)$ by $B$ immediately gives the output of the inner loop from $R_1(s)$ to $Y_B(s)$:

$$Y_B(s) = B V(s) = \frac{A B}{1 + A B E} R_1(s) \implies G_1(s) = \frac{Y_B(s)}{R_1(s)} = \frac{A B}{1 + A B E}$$

Second, combine the **two parallel paths** from $V(s)$ to the output summing junction $\Sigma_3$. The main upper path passes $Y_B(s) = B V(s)$ through block $D$ to contribute $D B V(s)$, while the lower feedforward path passes $V(s)$ through block $C$ to contribute $C V(s)$. Adding both branches at $\Sigma_3$ expresses the output $Y(s)$ in terms of $R_1(s)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Y(s) &= D B V(s) + C V(s) = (B D + C) V(s) \\
&= (B D + C) \frac{A}{1 + A B E} R_1(s) = \underbrace{\frac{A(B D + C)}{1 + A B E}}_{G_{\text{forward}}(s)} R_1(s)
\end{aligned}
$$

The entire inner network between $\Sigma_1$ and $Y(s)$ has now collapsed into a single forward transfer function $G_{\text{forward}}(s) = \frac{ABD + AC}{1 + ABE}$.

Third, close the **outer global feedback loop** returning from $Y(s)$ through block $F$ to the minus port of $\Sigma_1$. At $\Sigma_1$, the signal balance is $R_1(s) = U(s) - F Y(s)$. Substituting this into $Y(s) = G_{\text{forward}}(s) R_1(s)$ and clearing the compound fraction by multiplying numerator and denominator by $(1 + ABE)$ yields:

$$
\begin{aligned}
W(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} &= \frac{G_{\text{forward}}(s)}{1 + G_{\text{forward}}(s) F} = \frac{\frac{A(B D + C)}{1 + A B E}}{1 + \frac{A(B D + C)}{1 + A B E} F} \\
&= \frac{A(B D + C)}{1 + A B E + A F(B D + C)} = \frac{A B D + A C}{1 + A B E + A B D F + A C F}
\end{aligned}
$$

Every term in this final expression has a clear physical meaning: the numerator $ABD + AC$ sums the two forward paths from $U(s)$ to $Y(s)$, while the denominator $1 + ABE + ABDF + ACF$ sums $1$ plus the three individual negative-feedback loop gains ($ABE$ around the inner loop, $ABDF$ around the outer main path, and $ACF$ around the outer feedforward path).

To see how this symbolic reduction simplifies concrete dynamics, substitute $A = 2$, $B(s) = \frac{1}{s + 1}$, $C = 1$, $D = 3$, $E = 2$, and $F = 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
W(s) &= \frac{2 \cdot \frac{1}{s+1} \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 1}{1 + 2 \cdot \frac{1}{s+1} \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot \frac{1}{s+1} \cdot 3 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 1} = \frac{\frac{6}{s+1} + 2}{3 + \frac{4 + 6}{s+1}} \\
&= \frac{\frac{6 + 2(s + 1)}{s + 1}}{\frac{3(s + 1) + 10}{s + 1}} = \frac{2s + 8}{3s + 13}
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiplying numerator and denominator by $(s + 1)$ clears the internal pole at $s = -1$ and reveals a clean first-order closed-loop system with a single pole at $s = -13/3\text{ rad/s}$ and a zero at $s = -4\text{ rad/s}$.

### Block Diagram Primitives

In multi-loop control engineering—such as flight autopilots, robotic joint servos, and power converters—each physical device (actuator, sensor, filter, compensator) is modeled as an individual transfer block. **Block diagram algebra** provides a rigorous graphical calculus to connect those modular blocks together and reduce the resulting network to a single overall input-output transfer function $W(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)}$.

Every linear block diagram is constructed from three ideal, unidirectional building blocks:
- A **functional block** $G(s)$ multiplies its incoming Laplace signal $U(s)$ by the transfer operator $G(s)$ to produce $Y(s) = G(s)U(s)$. Critically, block diagrams assume **unidirectional signal flow** (infinite input impedance and zero output impedance), meaning a downstream block never loads or alters the transfer function of an upstream block.
- A **summing junction** ($\Sigma$) acts as an instantaneous linear comparator that adds or subtracts incoming signal branches according to the $+$ or $-$ sign printed at each arrowhead, such as $E(s) = U(s) - B(s)$.
- A **takeoff point** (branch node) taps a wire to route identical copies of the same signal $X(s)$ to multiple downstream destinations without splitting or attenuating its amplitude.

### Cascade and Parallel Connections

Whenever two blocks are wired together without an intervening feedback loop, they form either a series chain or a parallel split. We derive their combined equivalent block by tracing the intermediate signal algebraically.

When the output of block $G_1(s)$ feeds directly into the input of block $G_2(s)$ with no takeoff point or summing junction in between, the two subsystems are in **cascade** (series):

<figure id="fig-cascade-connection" className="dc-svg-wrap">
  <img src="/dynamic-courses/diagrams/systems-theory/fig-cascade-connection.svg" alt="Cascade (series) connection of two dynamic blocks." />
  <figcaption>Cascade (series) connection of two dynamic blocks.</figcaption>
</figure>

Letting $X(s) = G_1(s) U(s)$ denote the signal on the wire linking the two blocks and substituting $X(s)$ into $Y(s) = G_2(s) X(s)$ gives the cascade product rule:

$$Y(s) = G_2(s) X(s) = G_2(s)\big(G_1(s) U(s)\big) \implies W(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = G_2(s) G_1(s) = G_1(s) G_2(s)$$

Because scalar transfer functions commute under multiplication, cascading two SISO blocks multiplies their transfer functions in either order (though for multi-input multi-output matrix blocks $\mathbf{G}_2(s)\mathbf{G}_1(s)$, the matrix order must be kept strictly right-to-left).

Conversely, when a single input signal $U(s)$ splits at a takeoff point into two separate forward branches $G_1(s)$ and $G_2(s)$ whose outputs recombine at a summing junction, the subsystems are in **parallel**:

<figure id="fig-parallel-connection" className="dc-svg-wrap">
  <img src="/dynamic-courses/diagrams/systems-theory/fig-parallel-connection.svg" alt="Parallel connection of dynamic blocks." />
  <figcaption>Parallel connection of dynamic blocks.</figcaption>
</figure>

Summing the upper branch output $Y_1(s) = G_1(s)U(s)$ and the lower branch output $Y_2(s) = G_2(s)U(s)$ at the comparator and factoring out the common input $U(s)$ yields the parallel addition rule:

$$Y(s) = G_1(s) U(s) \pm G_2(s) U(s) = \big(G_1(s) \pm G_2(s)\big) U(s) \implies W(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = G_1(s) \pm G_2(s)$$

Parallel paths therefore combine by direct algebraic addition, which always places the resulting transfer function over the common denominator of $G_1(s)$ and $G_2(s)$ and introduces new numerator zeros.

### Closed-Loop Feedback

Unlike open-loop cascade or parallel interconnections, a **feedback loop** routes the output $Y(s)$ back through a sensor or return path $H(s)$ to modify the actuating signal entering the forward path $G(s)$. This cyclic dependency is the foundation of automatic control, allowing a controller to correct errors against unknown disturbances and shift unstable plant poles into the stable left half-plane.

<figure id="fig-canonical-feedback" className="dc-svg-wrap">
  <img src="/dynamic-courses/diagrams/systems-theory/fig-canonical-feedback.svg" alt="Canonical closed-loop feedback structure. The bottom junction sign is negative for negative feedback and positive for positive feedback." />
  <figcaption>Canonical closed-loop feedback structure. The bottom junction sign is negative for negative feedback and positive for positive feedback.</figcaption>
</figure>

To derive the equivalent closed-loop transfer function $W(s) = Y(s)/U(s)$ for [the canonical feedback loop](#fig-canonical-feedback), we first write the comparator equation for the actuating error signal $E(s)$ (using $-$ for **negative feedback** and $+$ for **positive feedback**) and then substitute $E(s)$ into the forward block $Y(s) = G(s)E(s)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E(s) &= U(s) \mp H(s) Y(s) \\
Y(s) &= G(s) E(s) = G(s)\big[U(s) \mp H(s) Y(s)\big] = G(s) U(s) \mp G(s) H(s) Y(s)
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that $Y(s)$ appears on both sides of the equation because the signal travels around the closed loop. Collecting all $Y(s)$ terms on the left-hand side flips the $\mp$ sign to $\pm$:

$$\big[1 \pm G(s) H(s)\big] Y(s) = G(s) U(s)$$

Dividing both sides by $\big[1 \pm G(s) H(s)\big] U(s)$ yields the fundamental **Closed-Loop Feedback Formula**:

$$W(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = \frac{G(s)}{1 \pm G(s) H(s)}$$

In this formula, $G(s)$ is the **forward-path transfer function**, $H(s)$ is the **feedback return-path transfer function** (with $H(s) = 1$ for unity feedback), and the product $L(s) \triangleq G(s)H(s)$ is the **open-loop (or loop) transfer function**. Always remember the sign reversal caused by moving $G(s)H(s)Y(s)$ across the equals sign: negative feedback ($-$ at the summing junction) produces $+G(s)H(s)$ in the denominator, whereas positive feedback ($+$ at the summing junction) produces $-G(s)H(s)$ in the denominator.

### Topological Equivalence Rules

In tangled multi-loop diagrams, a takeoff point from one loop frequently sits *inside* another feedback loop, preventing us from collapsing either loop directly with the cascade, parallel, or feedback formulas. To untangle interleaved loops, we slide a summing junction or takeoff point past an adjacent block $G(s)$ and insert a compensating transfer function on the moved branch so that every signal leaving the sub-network remains algebraically identical.

Let us verify the four fundamental node-shift operations by equating the outgoing signals before and after the move:
1. **Moving a summing junction from before $G(s)$ to after $G(s)$:** Before the move, two inputs $U_1(s)$ and $\pm U_2(s)$ sum first and then pass through $G(s)$, producing output:
   $$Y(s) = G(s)\big(U_1(s) \pm U_2(s)\big) = G(s) U_1(s) \pm G(s) U_2(s)$$
   By the distributive law, if we move the summing junction *after* $G(s)$ so that $U_1(s)$ passes through $G(s)$ alone, we must multiply the side branch $U_2(s)$ by an extra block $G(s)$ before adding it at the shifted summer.
2. **Moving a summing junction from after $G(s)$ to before $G(s)$:** Before the move, $U_1(s)$ passes through $G(s)$ first and then adds to $\pm U_2(s)$, giving output:
   $$Y(s) = G(s) U_1(s) \pm U_2(s) = G(s)\left( U_1(s) \pm \frac{1}{G(s)} U_2(s) \right)$$
   Factoring $G(s)$ out on the left shows that if we shift the summing junction *before* $G(s)$, we must pre-multiply the side branch $U_2(s)$ by the inverse block $\frac{1}{G(s)}$ so that passing through $G(s)$ restores $\pm U_2(s)$.
3. **Moving a takeoff point from before $G(s)$ to after $G(s)$:** Before the move, the main branch outputs $Y(s) = G(s)U(s)$ while the tapped wire carries the unscaled input $X(s) = U(s)$. If we slide the takeoff point *after* $G(s)$, the wire now taps $Y(s) = G(s)U(s)$, so we must insert $\frac{1}{G(s)}$ on the tapped branch to recover:
   $$X(s) = \frac{1}{G(s)} Y(s) = \frac{1}{G(s)}\big(G(s) U(s)\big) = U(s)$$
4. **Moving a takeoff point from after $G(s)$ to before $G(s)$:** Before the move, the tapped wire carries $X(s) = G(s)U(s)$. If we slide the takeoff point *before* $G(s)$ where the wire only carries $U(s)$, we must insert a duplicate block $G(s)$ on the tapped branch so its output remains $X(s) = G(s)U(s)$.

All four node-shift rules—plus the fact that two adjacent summing junctions can be reordered freely by associativity of addition—are summarized in the lookup table in Section 3.

In PX4 flight control stacks, attitude loops are wired in a nested, cascaded topology:
1. **Outer Attitude Loop ($\theta$):** A proportional gain $K_p$ turns angle error into a desired angular rate $\omega_{\text{ref}}$.

<Divider label="3 · Summary" />

| Original Topology | Desired Movement | Equivalent Modification | Invariant Guaranteed |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Summing Junction** before $G$ | Move **after $G$** | Multiply arriving branch by $G(s)$ | $Y = G(U_1 \pm U_2) = GU_1 \pm GU_2$ |
| **Summing Junction** after $G$ | Move **before $G$** | Insert $\frac{1}{G(s)}$ on arriving branch | $Y = GU_1 \pm U_2 = G\!\left(U_1 \pm \frac{1}{G}U_2\right)$ |
| **Takeoff Point** before $G$ | Move **after $G$** | Insert $\frac{1}{G(s)}$ on tapped branch | $X = U = \frac{1}{G}(GU)$ |
| **Takeoff Point** after $G$ | Move **before $G$** | Insert $G(s)$ on tapped branch | $X = GU$ |
| **Adjacent Summers** | Swap $\Sigma_1$ and $\Sigma_2$ | Direct permutation (no blocks) | $A + B + C = A + C + B$ |

### Reduction Procedure

1. **Collapse Local Blocks:** Combine purely series blocks ($G_1 G_2$) and purely parallel branches ($G_1 \pm G_2$).
2. **Eliminate Inner Loops:** Reduce self-contained single-loop feedback blocks via $\frac{G}{1 \pm GH}$.
3. **Shift Crossing Nodes:** If loops overlap, move a summing junction or takeoff point across one block using the equivalence table above.
4. **Close Outer Loop:** Repeat steps 1–3 from inside out until only $W(s) = Y(s)/U(s)$ remains.

### Key Proofs

- **Closed-Loop Feedback Formula:** Substituting $E = U \mp HY$ into $Y = GE$ gives $Y(1 \pm GH) = GU$, hence $W(s) = \frac{G(s)}{1 \pm G(s)H(s)}$.
- **Node-Shift Invariance:** Every equivalence transformation multiplies or divides the moved branch by $G(s)$ or $1/G(s)$ so all downstream signal values remain algebraically identical.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistakes">

1. **Feedback Sign Inversion:** Negative feedback ($-H$) produces $+G(s)H(s)$ in the denominator; positive feedback ($+H$) produces $-G(s)H(s)$.
2. **Non-Unity Feedback ($H \neq 1$):** Remember the closed-loop numerator is the forward gain $G(s)$ alone, whereas the denominator uses the full loop gain $L(s) = G(s)H(s)$.
3. **Illegal Node Swaps:** A summing junction and a takeoff point do **not** commute; swapping them directly alters intermediate signals.

</Callout>

<Exercise client:load id="ex-3-1" title="Exercise 3.1 — Three-Block Feedback Loop" level="Warm-Up">

Consider an elementary closed-loop control loop where a proportional forward controller $G_1(s) = K$ drives a first-order plant $G_2(s) = \frac{1}{s + 2}$ with sensor feedback $H(s) = 2$ returning to a subtractive summing junction (negative feedback).

1. Determine the forward path transfer function $G(s) = G_1(s)G_2(s)$ and the loop transfer function $L(s) = G(s)H(s)$.
2. Apply the canonical feedback formula $W(s) = \frac{G(s)}{1 + L(s)}$ to derive the closed-loop transfer function $W(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)}$.
3. Determine the closed-loop pole location as a function of $K$. For what value of gain $K > 0$ does the closed-loop system place its pole at $s = -10$?

<StepCheck

  expected={4}
  tolerance={0.05}
  unit=""
  explanation="The closed-loop denominator is $s + 2 + 2K = 0 \implies s_p = -(2 + 2K)$. Setting $2 + 2K = 10$ gives $K = 4$."

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Because the proportional controller $G_1(s) = K$ and the plant $G_2(s) = \frac{1}{s + 2}$ are wired directly in series along the forward path, we multiply them to obtain the forward transfer function:
   $$G(s) = G_1(s) G_2(s) = K \cdot \frac{1}{s + 2} = \frac{K}{s + 2}$$
   Multiplying $G(s)$ by the feedback sensor gain $H(s) = 2$ yields the open-loop transfer function $L(s)$:
   $$L(s) = G(s) H(s) = \frac{K}{s + 2} \cdot 2 = \frac{2K}{s + 2}$$
2. Because the return path enters a subtractive summing junction (negative feedback), we apply $W(s) = \frac{G(s)}{1 + L(s)}$ and multiply both numerator and denominator by $(s + 2)$ to clear the nested fraction:
   $$W(s) = \frac{G(s)}{1 + L(s)} = \frac{\frac{K}{s + 2}}{1 + \frac{2K}{s + 2}} = \frac{\frac{K}{s + 2}}{\frac{(s + 2) + 2K}{s + 2}} = \frac{K}{s + 2 + 2K}$$
3. The closed-loop pole $s_p$ is the root of the denominator polynomial $D(s) = s + 2 + 2K = 0$:
   $$s_p = -(2 + 2K)$$
   Setting $s_p = -10$ and solving for $K$:
   $$-(2 + 2K) = -10 \implies 2 + 2K = 10 \implies 2K = 8 \implies K = 4$$
   Physically, closing the negative feedback loop with $K = 4$ shifts the natural pole from its open-loop value $s = -2$ out to $s = -10$, making the exponential transient decay five times faster.

</details>

</Exercise>

<Exercise client:load id="ex-3-2" title="Exercise 3.2 — Multi-Branch Loop Reduction" level="Exam Standard">

Reduce the block diagram described below to a single transfer function $W(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)}$:
A forward path contains blocks $G_1(s)$ and $G_2(s)$ in series, with an inner negative feedback loop around $G_2(s)$ through block $H_1(s)$. A feedforward branch taps before $G_1(s)$, passes through $G_3(s)$, and adds positively to the output $Y(s)$.

1. Derive the general symbolic transfer function $W(s)$ in terms of $G_1, G_2, H_1, G_3$.
2. Substituting numerical values $G_1(s) = \frac{2}{s}$, $G_2(s) = \frac{1}{s+1}$, $H_1(s) = 3$, and $G_3(s) = 4$, compute the rational expression for $W(s)$ and determine its closed-loop poles and zeros.

<StepCheck
  client:load

  expected={-4}
  tolerance={0.05}
  unit="$\mathrm{rad/s}$"
  explanation="The inner loop reduces to $\frac{1/(s+1)}{1 + 3/(s+1)} = \frac{1}{s+4}$, giving closed-loop denominator $s(s+4)$ with poles at $s = 0$ and $s = -4$."

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Working from the innermost loop outward:
   - First, collapse the negative feedback loop around forward block $G_2(s)$ and feedback block $H_1(s)$:
     $$G_{2,\text{cl}}(s) = \frac{G_2(s)}{1 + G_2(s) H_1(s)}$$
   - Second, multiply $G_{2,\text{cl}}(s)$ by the upstream cascade block $G_1(s)$ to obtain the main upper branch transfer function:
     $$G_{\text{series}}(s) = G_1(s) G_{2,\text{cl}}(s) = \frac{G_1(s) G_2(s)}{1 + G_2(s) H_1(s)}$$
   - Third, add the parallel feedforward branch $G_3(s)$ which taps directly from $U(s)$ and sums positively into $Y(s)$:
     $$W(s) = G_{\text{series}}(s) + G_3(s) = \frac{G_1(s) G_2(s)}{1 + G_2(s) H_1(s)} + G_3(s) = \frac{G_1(s) G_2(s) + G_3(s)\big(1 + G_2(s) H_1(s)\big)}{1 + G_2(s) H_1(s)}$$
2. Substituting the numerical blocks $G_1(s) = \frac{2}{s}$, $G_2(s) = \frac{1}{s+1}$, $H_1(s) = 3$, and $G_3(s) = 4$:
   - Inner closed loop:
     $$G_{2,\text{cl}}(s) = \frac{\frac{1}{s+1}}{1 + \frac{3}{s+1}} = \frac{\frac{1}{s+1}}{\frac{s + 1 + 3}{s+1}} = \frac{1}{s + 4}$$
   - Series upper path:
     $$G_{\text{series}}(s) = \frac{2}{s} \cdot \frac{1}{s + 4} = \frac{2}{s(s + 4)}$$
   - Combining with the parallel branch $G_3(s) = 4$ over the common denominator $s(s + 4)$:
     $$W(s) = \frac{2}{s(s + 4)} + 4 = \frac{2 + 4s(s + 4)}{s(s + 4)} = \frac{4s^2 + 16s + 2}{s(s + 4)} = \frac{4(s^2 + 4s + 0.5)}{s(s + 4)}$$
   - **Closed-loop poles** (roots of $D(s) = s(s + 4) = 0$): $p_1 = 0$ and $p_2 = -4\text{ rad/s}$.
   - **Closed-loop zeros** (roots of $N(s) = 4(s^2 + 4s + 0.5) = 0$):
     $$s = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{4^2 - 4(1)(0.5)}}{2} = -2 \pm \sqrt{3.5} \implies z_1 \approx -0.129\text{ rad/s}, \quad z_2 \approx -3.871\text{ rad/s}$$

</details>

</Exercise>
