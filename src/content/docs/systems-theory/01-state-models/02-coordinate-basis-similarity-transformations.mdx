## Introduction

Describing a physical system in state space always involves an arbitrary choice of coordinates: you can track a pendulum's angle in radians or degrees, express a circuit via node voltages or mesh currents, or measure drone motion in body coordinates versus Earth coordinates. Changing coordinates scrambles the numbers inside the matrices $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C})$, yet the physical machine has not changed at all.

This chapter introduces **similarity transformations** ($\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}$) so you can switch coordinate bases cleanly—most powerfully to **modal coordinates** where dynamic modes decouple into independent scalar equations—while proving that intrinsic physical signatures (eigenvalues, natural frequencies, and the input-output transfer function) remain strictly invariant.

### Coupled Two-State Basis Change

In [First Order Differential State Equations](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/01-state-models/01-first-order-differential-state-equations/), we saw that an invertible change of variables $\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}(t)$ rewrites the same physical system in a new coordinate frame. Why would an engineer deliberately change coordinates? Because in raw physical coordinates, state equations are almost always coupled—state $x_2$ drives $\dot{x}_1$ or vice versa—whereas in a well-chosen basis, the cross-coupling vanishes and every state evolves on its own.

Consider a second-order continuous-time system with upper-triangular dynamics:

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = 0$$

Writing out the scalar equations shows that $\dot{x}_1(t) = -2x_1(t) + x_2(t) + u(t)$ is pulled by the cross-coupling term $+x_2(t)$, whereas $\dot{x}_2(t) = -5x_2(t) + 3u(t)$ evolves independently. Geometrically, as illustrated in [the figure](#fig-coordinate-similarity-basis), the physical state point $\mathbf{x}(t)$ stays fixed in the plane while we re-measure its coordinates along two new basis vectors $\mathbf{T} = [\mathbf{t}_1 \; \mathbf{t}_2]$.

To eliminate the off-diagonal coupling entry $A_{12} = 1$, we align our new coordinate axes $\mathbf{t}_1$ and $\mathbf{t}_2$ with the **eigenvectors** of $\mathbf{A}$. Because $\mathbf{A}$ is upper triangular, its characteristic polynomial is simply the product of its diagonal entries, $p(\lambda) = \det(\lambda\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = (\lambda + 2)(\lambda + 5) = 0$, giving eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = -2$ and $\lambda_2 = -5$. Let us solve $(\lambda_i\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{t}_i = \mathbf{0}$ for each eigenvector step by step:

- For $\lambda_1 = -2$, substituting $\lambda = -2$ into $(\lambda\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{t}_1 = \mathbf{0}$ gives:
  $$\begin{bmatrix} -2 - (-2) & -1 \\ 0 & -2 - (-5) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} t_{11} \\ t_{21} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} t_{11} \\ t_{21} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies t_{21} = 0, \quad \mathbf{t}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
  Any non-zero multiple of $[1, 0]^T$ lies along the first eigenmode axis.
- For $\lambda_2 = -5$, substituting $\lambda = -5$ into $(\lambda\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{t}_2 = \mathbf{0}$ gives:
  $$\begin{bmatrix} -5 - (-2) & -1 \\ 0 & -5 - (-5) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} t_{12} \\ t_{22} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} t_{12} \\ t_{22} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies -3t_{12} - t_{22} = 0 \implies \mathbf{t}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$$
  Choosing $t_{12} = 1$ forces $t_{22} = -3$, pointing along the second eigenmode direction in the state plane.

Packing $\mathbf{t}_1$ and $\mathbf{t}_2$ side by side as the columns of $\mathbf{T}$, we verify that $\det(\mathbf{T}) \neq 0$ and compute $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ by swapping the diagonal elements, negating the off-diagonal elements, and dividing by $\det(\mathbf{T}) = -3$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{T} &= \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{t}_1 & \mathbf{t}_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(\mathbf{T}) = (1)(-3) - (1)(0) = -3 \neq 0 \\
\mathbf{T}^{-1} &= \frac{1}{-3} \begin{bmatrix} -3 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \frac{1}{3} \\ 0 & -\frac{1}{3} \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $\det(\mathbf{T}) = -3 \neq 0$, the two eigenvectors span all of $\mathbb{R}^2$ and form a valid coordinate basis. Now we apply $\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}(t)$ to compute the transformed dynamic matrix $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}$ in two explicit matrix multiplications:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T} &= \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (-2)(1) + (1)(0) & (-2)(1) + (1)(-3) \\ (0)(1) + (-5)(0) & (0)(1) + (-5)(-3) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -5 \\ 0 & 15 \end{bmatrix} \\
\tilde{\mathbf{A}} &= \mathbf{T}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \frac{1}{3} \\ 0 & -\frac{1}{3} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -5 \\ 0 & 15 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 + 0 & -5 + 5 \\ 0 + 0 & 0 - 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & -5 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice how the $(1,2)$ coupling entry cancels out via $-5 + \frac{1}{3}(15) = 0$, leaving the eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = -2$ and $\lambda_2 = -5$ sitting cleanly on the main diagonal of $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$. Next, we transform the input vector $\mathbf{B}$ and sensor row $\mathbf{C}$ into the new $\mathbf{z}$-coordinates:

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \frac{1}{3} \\ 0 & -\frac{1}{3} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (1)(1) + \frac{1}{3}(3) \\ (0)(1) - \frac{1}{3}(3) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

In the new modal coordinates $\mathbf{z}(t)$, the system splits into two completely uncoupled first-order differential equations $\dot{z}_1(t) = -2z_1(t) + 2u(t)$ and $\dot{z}_2(t) = -5z_2(t) - u(t)$ with sensor readout $y(t) = z_1(t) + z_2(t)$. Even though every entry of $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{C}}$ changed numerically, the eigenvalues $\{-2, -5\}$ and the external input-output transfer function (which we will study systematically in [Transfer Function Matrix](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/04-transfer-function-matrix-invariants/)) remain strictly identical in both coordinate systems:

$$\tilde{W}(s) = \frac{\tilde{C}_1 \tilde{B}_1}{s - \lambda_1} + \frac{\tilde{C}_2 \tilde{B}_2}{s - \lambda_2} = \frac{1 \cdot 2}{s + 2} + \frac{1 \cdot (-1)}{s + 5} = \frac{2(s + 5) - (s + 2)}{(s + 2)(s + 5)} = \frac{s + 8}{(s + 2)(s + 5)} = W(s)$$

Thus, changing coordinates alters how internal variables are labeled and scaled, but never alters how the physical machine responds at its external terminals.

### Algebraic Coordinate Transformation

Because an internal state vector $\mathbf{x}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is chosen by the modeler—such as node voltages versus mesh currents in an electrical filter, or body-frame versus Earth-frame axes on an aircraft—any single physical system admits infinitely many valid state-space realizations. We need an exact algebraic dictionary that translates the matrices $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{D})$ from one coordinate basis to another without losing or distorting the underlying dynamics.

Let $\mathbf{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ be any constant non-singular matrix ($\det(\mathbf{T}) \neq 0$), defining a bijective linear mapping between the original state vector $\mathbf{x}(t)$ and a new state vector $\mathbf{z}(t)$:

$$\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{T} \mathbf{z}(t) \iff \mathbf{z}(t) = \mathbf{T}^{-1} \mathbf{x}(t)$$

Geometrically, column $j$ of $\mathbf{T}$ represents the $j$-th new coordinate axis expressed in the old $\mathbf{x}$-coordinates. Because $\mathbf{T}$ is constant with respect to time, differentiating $\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}(t)$ yields $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{T}\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t)$. Substituting both $\mathbf{x}(t)$ and $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)$ into the state-space equations $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t)$ and $\mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}(t)$ from [First Order Differential State Equations](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/01-state-models/01-first-order-differential-state-equations/) gives:

$$\mathbf{T} \dot{\mathbf{z}}(t) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{T} \mathbf{z}(t) + \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}(t), \qquad \mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{C} \mathbf{T} \mathbf{z}(t) + \mathbf{D} \mathbf{u}(t)$$

Premultiplying the differential equation on the left by $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ uses $\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{I}$ to isolate the derivative vector $\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t) &= \left( \mathbf{T}^{-1} \mathbf{A} \mathbf{T} \right) \mathbf{z}(t) + \left( \mathbf{T}^{-1} \mathbf{B} \right) \mathbf{u}(t) \\
\mathbf{y}(t) &= \left( \mathbf{C} \mathbf{T} \right) \mathbf{z}(t) + \mathbf{D} \mathbf{u}(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

Two state-space realizations $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{D})$ and $(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}, \tilde{\mathbf{B}}, \tilde{\mathbf{C}}, \tilde{\mathbf{D}})$ are therefore called **algebraically similar** (or coordinate-equivalent) if and only if there exists an invertible matrix $\mathbf{T}$ such that:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1} \mathbf{A} \mathbf{T}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1} \mathbf{B}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C} \mathbf{T}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{D}} = \mathbf{D}$$

In this dictionary, $\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}$ projects the external input directions onto the new $\mathbf{z}$-basis, $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}$ updates $\mathbf{z}(t)$ inside the new basis, $\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}$ maps $\mathbf{z}(t)$ back into $\mathbf{x}(t)$ before reading out the sensors, and the direct bypass $\tilde{\mathbf{D}} = \mathbf{D}$ stays untouched because it never enters the state space.

### Characteristic Polynomial Invariance

When a coordinate change replaces $\mathbf{A}$ with $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}$, every individual entry of the matrix can change. If the system's natural decay rates and oscillation frequencies—its eigenvalues—depended on our arbitrary choice of coordinates, state-space stability analysis would be meaningless. We must prove that the characteristic polynomial and eigenvalue spectrum remain completely unchanged under any invertible coordinate transformation $\mathbf{T}$.

Consider the characteristic polynomial $\tilde{p}(\lambda) = \det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \tilde{\mathbf{A}})$ of the transformed matrix. Writing the identity matrix as $\mathbf{I} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{I}\mathbf{T}$ lets us factor $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ on the left and $\mathbf{T}$ on the right inside the determinant, after which Binet's determinant product rule $\det(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}) = \det(\mathbf{P})\det(\mathbf{Q})\det(\mathbf{R})$ separates the scalar determinants:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{p}(\lambda) &= \det\left(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \tilde{\mathbf{A}}\right) = \det\left(\lambda \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{I}\mathbf{T} - \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}\right) \\
&= \det\left(\mathbf{T}^{-1}(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{T}\right) = \det\left(\mathbf{T}^{-1}\right) \det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) \det(\mathbf{T}) \\
&= \frac{1}{\det(\mathbf{T})} \det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) \det(\mathbf{T}) = \det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = p(\lambda)
\end{aligned}
$$

Because the nonzero scalar factors $\det(\mathbf{T})^{-1}$ and $\det(\mathbf{T})$ cancel each other out, $\tilde{p}(\lambda)$ and $p(\lambda)$ have the exact same coefficients for every power of $\lambda$. In particular, the **trace** $\operatorname{Tr}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}) = \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A})$ (sum of eigenvalues), the **determinant** $\det(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}) = \det(\mathbf{A})$ (product of eigenvalues), and the entire **eigenvalue spectrum** are strictly invariant:

$$\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}) = \sigma(\mathbf{A})$$

Physically, this theorem confirms that natural time constants, damping ratios, and internal stability belong to the physical hardware itself, never to the coordinate system chosen on paper.

### Transfer Function Invariance

Just as internal natural frequencies must not depend on coordinate labels, the external relationship between actuator inputs $\mathbf{U}(s)$ and sensor outputs $\mathbf{Y}(s)$ measured at the system terminals cannot depend on internal coordinate axes. In [Transfer Function Matrix](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/04-transfer-function-matrix-invariants/), we will show from the Laplace transform that zero-initial-state inputs and outputs are linked by the transfer function matrix $\mathbf{W}(s) = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D}$. Let us verify right now that $\mathbf{W}(s)$ is invariant under $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}$.

Substituting $(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}, \tilde{\mathbf{B}}, \tilde{\mathbf{C}}, \tilde{\mathbf{D}}) = (\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{D})$ into $\tilde{\mathbf{W}}(s)$ and applying the three-matrix inverse rule $(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R})^{-1} = \mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1}$ to $\left[\mathbf{T}^{-1}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{T}\right]^{-1} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{T}$ yields:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}(s) &= \tilde{\mathbf{C}} (s\mathbf{I} - \tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{-1} \tilde{\mathbf{B}} + \tilde{\mathbf{D}} \\
&= (\mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}) \left[ s\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{I}\mathbf{T} - \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T} \right]^{-1} (\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}) + \mathbf{D} \\
&= \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T} \left[ \mathbf{T}^{-1}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{T} \right]^{-1} \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D} \\
&= \mathbf{C}\underbrace{(\mathbf{T} \mathbf{T}^{-1})}_{\mathbf{I}} (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} \underbrace{(\mathbf{T} \mathbf{T}^{-1})}_{\mathbf{I}}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D} \\
&= \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D} = \mathbf{W}(s)
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice how the right-multiplication by $\mathbf{T}$ in $\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}$ cancels against the $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ emerging from the left of the inverted resolvent, while the $\mathbf{T}$ emerging from the right of the inverted resolvent cancels against $\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}$. Every external step response, impulse response, and frequency response is therefore completely blind to internal similarity transformations.

### Modal Diagonalization

Since we are free to pick any invertible matrix $\mathbf{T}$ without altering the physics, which coordinate basis makes the differential equations easiest to solve? If we can transform $\mathbf{A}$ into a **diagonal matrix** $\mathbf{\Lambda}$, all off-diagonal coupling disappears and the $n \times n$ coupled vector differential equation fractures into $n$ independent first-order scalar equations.

Suppose $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ has $n$ linearly independent right eigenvectors $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\}$ associated with eigenvalues $\{\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n\}$ (which is always guaranteed when all $n$ eigenvalues are distinct):

$$\mathbf{A} \mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{v}_i, \qquad i = 1, 2, \dots, n$$

Each eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_i$ defines an invariant direction in state space along which the matrix $\mathbf{A}$ acts by pure scalar multiplication $\lambda_i$. Assembling these $n$ eigenvectors side by side as columns defines the **Modal Matrix** $\mathbf{M}$:

$$\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{M} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{v}_2 & \dots & \mathbf{v}_n \end{bmatrix}$$

Because the $n$ eigenvectors are linearly independent, $\det(\mathbf{M}) \neq 0$, so $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$ exists. Multiplying $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{M}$ scales each column $\mathbf{v}_i$ by its own eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, which we can factor on the right as $\mathbf{M}$ times the diagonal eigenvalue matrix $\mathbf{\Lambda} = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$:

$$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{M} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_2 & \dots & \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1 & \lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2 & \dots & \lambda_n\mathbf{v}_n \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{M} \mathbf{\Lambda}$$

Premultiplying both sides of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{M}\mathbf{\Lambda}$ on the left by $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$ diagonalizes the dynamic matrix:

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathbf{A} \mathbf{M} = \mathbf{\Lambda} = \begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\
0 & \lambda_2 & \dots & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \dots & \lambda_n
\end{bmatrix}
$$

In these **modal coordinates** $\mathbf{z}(t) = \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{x}(t)$, every off-diagonal entry is zero, so the coupled state equation decouples into $n$ isolated scalar differential equations:

$$\dot{z}_i(t) = \lambda_i z_i(t) + \tilde{b}_i u(t), \qquad i = 1, \dots, n$$

where $\tilde{b}_i$ is the $i$-th entry of $\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{B}$. Physically, $\tilde{b}_i$ tells us how strongly the actuator excites mode $i$, $z_i(t)$ tracks the amplitude of mode $i$ evolving at natural rate $e^{\lambda_i t}$, and the $i$-th column of $\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{M}$ tells us how strongly mode $i$ appears in the sensor measurements.

<Callout type="bridge" title="Robotics Bridge: Body-to-Inertial Transformations">

In aerial robotics, coordinate transformations occur continuously between the **Body-fixed frame** $\mathcal{F}_B$ (aligned with IMU accelerometer axes) and the **Inertial World frame** $\mathcal{F}_W$ (North-East-Down or ENU coordinates). For body velocities $\mathbf{v}_B = \begin{bmatrix} u & v & w \end{bmatrix}^T$ and inertial positions $\mathbf{p}_W = \begin{bmatrix} x & y & z \end{bmatrix}^T$:

$$\dot{\mathbf{p}}_W(t) = \mathbf{R}_{WB}(\mathbf{q}) \mathbf{v}_B(t)$$

where $\mathbf{R}_{WB} \in SO(3)$ is the orthogonal rotation matrix parameterized by unit quaternions $\mathbf{q}$. State estimation nodes in ROS 2 use similarity transformations to rotate error covariance matrices $\mathbf{P}$ between sensor frames and robot-centric tracking coordinates.

</Callout>

### Diagonalization Procedure

1. **Compute Characteristic Polynomial:** Solve $p(\lambda) = \det(\lambda\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = 0$ for eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$.
2. **Find Eigenvectors:** For each $\lambda_i$, solve $(\lambda_i\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}$ to obtain $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors.
3. **Assemble & Invert $\mathbf{T}$:** Form $\mathbf{T} = [\mathbf{v}_1 \;\dots\; \mathbf{v}_n]$, confirm $\det(\mathbf{T}) \neq 0$, and compute $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$.
4. **Transform Matrices:** Compute $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{\Lambda}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}$, and $\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}$.

### Key Proofs

- **Spectrum Invariance:** $\det(\lambda\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}) = \det(\mathbf{T}^{-1})\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})\det(\mathbf{T}) = \det(\lambda\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})$.
- **Transfer Invariance:** Factoring $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ and $\mathbf{T}$ out of $(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T})^{-1}$ cancels $\mathbf{T}\mathbf{T}^{-1} = \mathbf{I}$ against $\mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}$ and $\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}$.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistakes">

1. **Swapping $\mathbf{T}$ and $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$:** Under $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}$, always multiply $\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}$ on the left by $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}$ on the right by $\mathbf{T}$.
2. **Singular Transformation Matrices:** If two columns of $\mathbf{T}$ are collinear ($\det(\mathbf{T}) = 0$), $\mathbf{T}$ is not a valid coordinate change.
3. **Defective Matrices:** If an eigenvalue with algebraic multiplicity $m_a > 1$ lacks $m_a$ independent eigenvectors ($m_g < m_a$), $\mathbf{A}$ cannot be diagonalized and requires a Jordan form.

</Callout>

1. Because $\mathbf{T} = \operatorname{diag}(2, 3)$ is diagonal with $\det(\mathbf{T}) = (2)(3) = 6 \neq 0$, its inverse is obtained by taking the reciprocal of each diagonal entry:
   $$\mathbf{T}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/3 \end{bmatrix}$$
2. Carrying out the similarity transformation multiplications step by step:
   $$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & -12 \end{bmatrix}$$
   $$\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}) = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & -12 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (1/2)(-2) & 0 \\ 0 & (1/3)(-12) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{A}$$
   $$\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (1/2)(1) + (0)(2) \\ (0)(1) + (1/3)(2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ 2/3 \end{bmatrix}$$
   $$\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (1)(2) + (1)(0) & (1)(0) + (1)(3) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$
3. The diagonal entries of $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ give the eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = -1$ and $\lambda_2 = -4$, identical to those of $\mathbf{A}$. Furthermore, multiplying each modal input weight by its corresponding output weight shows that the mode-by-mode residues are completely unchanged:
   $$\tilde{C}_1 \tilde{B}_1 = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1 = C_1 B_1, \qquad \tilde{C}_2 \tilde{B}_2 = 3 \cdot \frac{2}{3} = 2 = C_2 B_2$$
   Thus the transfer function $\tilde{W}(s) = \frac{1}{s+1} + \frac{2}{s+4} = W(s)$ is preserved.

<Exercise client:load id="ex-2-2" title="Exercise 2.2 — Modal Matrix Diagonalization" level="Exam Standard">

Consider the coupled symmetric second-order system:

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -5 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Compute the eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ by solving the characteristic equation $\det(\lambda\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = 0$.
2. Compute the corresponding eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2$.
3. Form the modal transformation matrix $\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{v}_2 \end{bmatrix}$ and compute its inverse $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ using the adjugate matrix formula.
4. Calculate the decoupled state matrices $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}$, and $\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}$, verifying that $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ is diagonal.

<StepCheck
  client:load
  prompt="What is the determinant $\det(\mathbf{T})$ when using $\mathbf{v}_1 = [1, 2]^T$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = [-2, 1]^T$?"
  expected={5}
  tolerance={0.05}
  unit=""
  explanation="For $\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $\det(\mathbf{T}) = (1)(1) - (-2)(2) = 5$."
/>

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Forming $\lambda\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}$ and expanding its determinant:
   $$\det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = \det\begin{bmatrix} \lambda + 5 & -2 \\ -2 & \lambda + 2 \end{bmatrix} = (\lambda + 5)(\lambda + 2) - (-2)(-2) = \lambda^2 + 7\lambda + 10 - 4 = \lambda^2 + 7\lambda + 6$$
   Factoring the quadratic gives $(\lambda + 1)(\lambda + 6) = 0$, so the eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = -1$ and $\lambda_2 = -6$.
2. Solving $(\lambda_i\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}$ for each eigenvalue:
   - For $\lambda_1 = -1$:
     $$(-1 \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 + 5 & -2 \\ -2 & -1 + 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_{11} \\ v_{12} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_{11} \\ v_{12} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
     Both rows reduce to $2v_{11} - v_{12} = 0 \implies v_{12} = 2v_{11}$. Setting $v_{11} = 1$ gives $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
   - For $\lambda_2 = -6$:
     $$(-6 \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -6 + 5 & -2 \\ -2 & -6 + 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_{21} \\ v_{22} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -2 \\ -2 & -4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_{21} \\ v_{22} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
     Both rows reduce to $v_{21} + 2v_{22} = 0 \implies v_{21} = -2v_{22}$. Setting $v_{22} = 1$ gives $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
3. Assembling the modal matrix $\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{v}_2 \end{bmatrix}$ and computing its $2\times 2$ adjugate inverse:
   $$\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(\mathbf{T}) = (1)(1) - (-2)(2) = 1 + 4 = 5 \neq 0$$
   $$\mathbf{T}^{-1} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
4. Computing the transformed realization $(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}, \tilde{\mathbf{B}}, \tilde{\mathbf{C}})$ explicitly:
   $$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} -5 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (-5)(1) + (2)(2) & (-5)(-2) + (2)(1) \\ (2)(1) + (-2)(2) & (2)(-2) + (-2)(1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 12 \\ -2 & -6 \end{bmatrix}$$
   $$\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}) = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 12 \\ -2 & -6 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} -1 - 4 & 12 - 12 \\ 2 - 2 & -24 - 6 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} -5 & 0 \\ 0 & -30 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -6 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{\Lambda}$$
   $$\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/5 \\ -2/5 \end{bmatrix}$$
   $$\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + 2 & -2 + 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$$
   Checking the characteristic polynomial confirms $\det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \tilde{\mathbf{A}}) = (\lambda + 1)(\lambda + 6) = \lambda^2 + 7\lambda + 6 = \det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$.

</details>
