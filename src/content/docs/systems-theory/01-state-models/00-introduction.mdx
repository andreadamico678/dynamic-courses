## Introduction

Before analyzing stability or designing controllers, an engineer must choose a mathematical language to describe how a physical system evolves over time. High-school and introductory physics courses model mechanical or electrical setups as a single high-order differential equation linking one input to one output. That input-output view breaks down as soon as a system has multiple actuators, multiple sensors, or internal energy-storing elements whose internal behavior matters even when the output looks quiet.

Part I builds the universal **state-space representation**. Instead of tracking derivatives up to order $n$ of a single output, you pack the system's instantaneous memory—positions, velocities, capacitor voltages, inductor currents—into a vector $\mathbf{x}(t)$ and write every physical law as a clean system of coupled first-order differential equations $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}$.

### Practical Real-World Perspective

- **Suspension & Robotics:** A two-cart spring-damper bench or a flexible robotic joint stores potential energy in springs and kinetic energy in moving masses. Choosing position and velocity as states lets one $4 \times 4$ matrix model predict both oscillation and sensor output regardless of whether you bolt an accelerometer, tachometer, or encoder onto the hardware.
- **Coordinate Frames in Drones:** An inertial measurement unit (IMU) measures angular rates in the drone's body frame, while navigation requires coordinates in the ground Earth frame. Similarity transformations $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}$ formalize why rotating or rescaling internal coordinates leaves physical eigenvalues and input-output behavior completely unchanged.
- **Modular Control Architecture:** Real flight stacks and industrial plants cascade motor drivers, gearboxes, sensors, and feedback compensators. Block diagram algebra turns those interconnected loops into a single clean operator for analysis.
