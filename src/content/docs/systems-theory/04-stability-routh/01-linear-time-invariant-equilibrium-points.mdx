## Introduction

Real machines rarely sit at mathematical zero ($\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$): a hovering drone spins its rotors at steady hover throttle to balance gravity, a cruise-controlled car holds $130\;\text{km/h}$ against aerodynamic drag, and a DC motor spins at constant speed under steady load torque. Those steady operating points where all time derivatives vanish ($\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{0}$) are **equilibrium states**.

Finding equilibria—and verifying when a system has one unique equilibrium, infinitely many, or none at all via Rouché-Capelli—prepares you to shift coordinates to the perturbation $\mathbf{z}(t) = \mathbf{x}(t) - \mathbf{x}_e$. That single coordinate shift lets every origin-based stability theorem in the rest of Part IV apply directly to any real operating point.

<Divider label="1 · Example" />

Consider a coupled two-state system driven by a constant external input $u(t) \equiv u_0 = 3$:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}(t) + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix} u(t)
$$

Here $x_2(t)$ is driven directly by the input $u(t)$ and decays at rate $4$, while $x_1(t)$ is coupled to $x_2(t)$ and decays at rate $2$. At a stationary **equilibrium state** $\mathbf{x}_e$, every state component stops changing, so the velocity vector vanishes identically ($\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) \equiv \mathbf{0}$). Setting $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{0}$ and $u(t) = 3$ turns the differential equation into the linear algebraic system $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e = -\mathbf{B} u_0$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_{e,1} \\ x_{e,2} \end{bmatrix} = -\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}(3) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -24 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Writing out the two scalar equations gives $-2x_{e,1} + x_{e,2} = 0$ and $-4x_{e,2} = -24$. Because the matrix is upper-triangular with $\det(\mathbf{A}) = (-2)(-4) = 8 \neq 0$, the two equilibrium lines intersect at a single point: the second equation gives $x_{e,2} = 6$, and substituting that into the first equation gives $-2x_{e,1} + 6 = 0 \implies x_{e,1} = 3$. Thus $\mathbf{x}_e = [3, \; 6]^T$ is the **unique** equilibrium state for $u_0 = 3$.

By contrast, suppose the top-left entry were $0$ instead of $-2$, so $\mathbf{A}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$ has $\det(\mathbf{A}_0) = 0$. Under zero input ($u_0 = 0$), the equilibrium condition becomes $x_{e,2} = 0$ with $x_{e,1}$ completely unconstrained—meaning every point along the horizontal axis $\ker(\mathbf{A}_0) = \operatorname{span}\{[1, 0]^T\}$ is an equilibrium state. Physically, $x_1$ behaves like a frictionless position coordinate that can rest at any value once velocity $x_2$ settles to zero.

### Equilibrium State Definition

In physical systems, an **equilibrium state** (also called an *operating point*, *quiescent state*, or *trim condition*) is a permanent stationary configuration: a mass-spring system where gravity balances spring tension, a DC motor where electromagnetic torque matches viscous drag, or a hovering drone where propeller thrust equals vehicle weight.

Formalizing this for the continuous-time LTI state-space model:

$$\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t), \qquad \mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}(t)$$

with $\mathbf{x}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $\mathbf{u}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^m$, and $\mathbf{y}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^p$, a vector $\mathbf{x}_e \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is an **equilibrium point** corresponding to constant input $\mathbf{u}(t) \equiv \mathbf{u}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^m$ if initializing the system at $\mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{x}_e$ keeps $\mathbf{x}(t) \equiv \mathbf{x}_e$ for all $t \ge 0$. Because $\mathbf{x}(t)$ is constant in time, its derivative vanishes identically ($\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{0}$), which turns the differential equation into the **operational LTI equilibrium criterion**:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e = -\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0}, \qquad \mathbf{y}_e = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}_e + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}_0$$

The first equation states that internal restoring dynamics $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e$ must exactly cancel the external forcing $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0$, while the second equation gives the resulting constant trim output $\mathbf{y}_e$. Conversely, by the Picard-Lindelöf uniqueness theorem for linear ODEs, if $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{0}$ at $(\mathbf{x}_e, \mathbf{u}_0)$, then the constant function $\mathbf{x}(t) \equiv \mathbf{x}_e$ is the unique trajectory starting from $\mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{x}_e$. For unforced systems ($\mathbf{u}_0 = \mathbf{0}$), the condition reduces to $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e = \mathbf{0}$, so the origin $\mathbf{x}_e = \mathbf{0}$ is always an equilibrium point.

### Solvability via Rouché-Capelli

The equilibrium equation $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e = -\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0$ is a system of $n$ linear equations in $n$ unknowns. Physically, the column space $\operatorname{Im}(\mathbf{A})$ represents every state-rate vector that the system's internal dynamics can produce to oppose an external input. By the **Rouché-Capelli theorem**, existence and uniqueness of $\mathbf{x}_e$ depend on comparing $\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})$ against the rank of the augmented matrix $[\mathbf{A} \mid -\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0]$:

1. **Non-Singular System Matrix ($\det(\mathbf{A}) \neq 0$):**
   Here $\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = n$, so $\operatorname{Im}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathbb{R}^n$ and zero is not an eigenvalue of $\mathbf{A}$. Because $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$ exists, every constant input $\mathbf{u}_0$ can be balanced at a **unique** isolated equilibrium state with steady-state output:
   $$\boxed{\mathbf{x}_e = -\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0}, \qquad \mathbf{y}_e = (-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D})\mathbf{u}_0 = \mathbf{W}(0)\mathbf{u}_0$$
   The matrix $\mathbf{W}(0) = -\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D}$ is the system's **DC gain matrix** (obtained by setting $s = 0$ in $\mathbf{W}(s) = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D}$). When $\mathbf{u}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, the origin $\mathbf{x}_e = \mathbf{0}$ is the strictly unique equilibrium.

2. **Singular System Matrix ($\det(\mathbf{A}) = 0$):**
   Here $\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = r < n$, so $\lambda = 0$ is an eigenvalue with geometric multiplicity $m_g(0) = n - r \ge 1$ and non-trivial null space $\ker(\mathbf{A}) = \{\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n \mid \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\}$. Any displacement along $\ker(\mathbf{A})$ generates zero internal restoring rate, splitting the behavior into two subcases:
   * **Incompatible Input ($-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0 \notin \operatorname{Im}(\mathbf{A})$):** Augmenting $\mathbf{A}$ with $-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0$ adds a linearly independent column, raising the rank to $\operatorname{rank}([\mathbf{A} \mid -\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0]) = r + 1 > r$. **Zero equilibria exist.** The constant input pushes along an unconstrained integrator direction with no internal restoring force to balance it, causing unbounded drift (like applying constant net force to a frictionless mass).
   * **Compatible Input ($-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0 \in \operatorname{Im}(\mathbf{A})$):** Augmenting does not increase the rank, so $\operatorname{rank}([\mathbf{A} \mid -\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0]) = r$. **Infinitely many equilibria exist**, forming an $(n - r)$-dimensional affine manifold:
     $$\boxed{\mathbf{x}_e = \mathbf{x}_{e,\text{part}} + \ker(\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{x}_{e,\text{part}} + \sum_{k=1}^{n-r} \alpha_k \mathbf{v}_k, \quad \alpha_k \in \mathbb{R}}$$
     where $\mathbf{x}_{e,\text{part}}$ is any particular solution and $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_{n-r}\}$ is a basis of $\ker(\mathbf{A})$. Moving from $\mathbf{x}_{e,\text{part}}$ along any $\mathbf{v}_k$ leaves $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e$ unchanged, so every point on the manifold balances $-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0$. For $\mathbf{u}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, compatibility always holds and the equilibrium set is $\ker(\mathbf{A})$.

### Perturbation Coordinate Translation

To study stability or design controllers around a non-zero operating trim $(\mathbf{x}_e, \mathbf{u}_0)$, we measure deviations relative to the equilibrium by defining the **perturbation state**, **perturbation input**, and **perturbation output**:

$$\mathbf{z}(t) = \mathbf{x}(t) - \mathbf{x}_e, \qquad \mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{u}(t) - \mathbf{u}_0, \qquad \mathbf{w}(t) = \mathbf{y}(t) - \mathbf{y}_e$$

Because $\mathbf{x}_e$ is constant in time, $\dot{\mathbf{x}}_e = \mathbf{0}$ and therefore $\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t) = \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)$. Substituting $\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{x}_e + \mathbf{z}(t)$ and $\mathbf{u}(t) = \mathbf{u}_0 + \mathbf{v}(t)$ into the state and output equations cancels the equilibrium terms $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}_e + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}_0 = \mathbf{y}_e$:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t) = \dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_e + \mathbf{z}(t)) + \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{u}_0 + \mathbf{v}(t)) = \underbrace{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0)}_{=\,\mathbf{0}} + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}(t) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}(t)
$$

$$
\mathbf{w}(t) = \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{x}_e + \mathbf{z}(t)) + \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{u}_0 + \mathbf{v}(t)) - \mathbf{y}_e = \underbrace{(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}_e + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}_0 - \mathbf{y}_e)}_{=\,\mathbf{0}} + \mathbf{C}\mathbf{z}(t) + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}(t)
$$

$$\boxed{\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}(t) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}(t)}, \qquad \mathbf{w}(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{z}(t) + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}(t)$$

Because this deviation model shares the exact same matrices $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{D})$ and places the equilibrium at the origin ($\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$), analyzing stability around **any** operating point $\mathbf{x}_e$ reduces completely to analyzing the origin of $\dot{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}$, as developed in [Lyapunov Internal State Stability](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/04-stability-routh/02-lyapunov-spectral-internal-stability/).

<Callout type="bridge" title="Robotics Application: Mobile Robots and Drone Hover Trim">

1. **Differential-Drive Heading:** A mobile robot with heading angle $\theta$ and angular velocity $\omega$ satisfies $\dot{\theta} = \omega$ and $\dot{\omega} = -\frac{d_m}{J}\omega + \frac{k_t}{J}u$. With $\mathbf{x} = [\theta, \; \omega]^T$, $\mathbf{A}$ is singular ($\det(\mathbf{A}) = 0$). For zero torque $u_0 = 0$, the equilibrium set is the line $\mathbf{x}_e = [\theta_e, \; 0]^T$ for any heading $\theta_e \in [-\pi, \pi]$.
2. **Quadrotor Vertical Hover:** A quadrotor of mass $m$ has vertical dynamics $\dot{z} = v_z$ and $\dot{v}_z = -g + \frac{1}{m}T(t)$. Hover equilibrium requires $T_0 = mg$, at which any altitude $z_e$ forms a valid equilibrium state.

</Callout>

<Divider label="3 · Summary" />

| Case | Rank Condition | Number of Equilibria | Solution / Behavior |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Non-Singular** ($\det(\mathbf{A}) \neq 0$) | $\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = n$ | Unique ($1$) | $\mathbf{x}_e = -\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0$ |
| **Singular, Compatible** | $\operatorname{rank}([\mathbf{A} \mid -\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0]) = r < n$ | Infinite ($\infty^{n-r}$) | $\mathbf{x}_{e,\text{part}} + \ker(\mathbf{A})$ |
| **Singular, Incompatible** | $\operatorname{rank}([\mathbf{A} \mid -\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0]) = r + 1$ | None ($0$) | Unbounded integrator drift |

### Calculation Procedure

1. Set $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{0}$ and write the algebraic equilibrium system $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e = -\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0$.
2. Evaluate $\det(\mathbf{A})$: if non-zero, compute the unique $\mathbf{x}_e = -\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0$.
3. If $\det(\mathbf{A}) = 0$, compare $\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})$ and $\operatorname{rank}([\mathbf{A} \mid -\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0])$; when compatible, find a particular solution plus a basis for $\ker(\mathbf{A})$.
4. Translate coordinates via $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_e$ to obtain $\dot{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}$ around the origin.

### Key Proofs

- **Rouché-Capelli Classification:** $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e = -\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0$ has solutions iff $-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0 \in \operatorname{Im}(\mathbf{A})$, with affine solution dimension $n - \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})$.
- **Translation Invariance:** Differentiating $\mathbf{z}(t) = \mathbf{x}(t) - \mathbf{x}_e$ cancels $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, leaving the dynamics matrix $\mathbf{A}$ unchanged.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistakes">

1. **Missing Minus Sign:** Always solve $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e = -\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0$, never $+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0$.
2. **Misreading $\det(\mathbf{A}) = 0$:** A singular $\mathbf{A}$ does *not* automatically mean no equilibrium exists; you must check compatibility $-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0 \in \operatorname{Im}(\mathbf{A})$.
3. **Confusing Trim Output with DC Gain When Singular:** The DC-gain formula $\mathbf{y}_e = (-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D})\mathbf{u}_0$ applies only when $\det(\mathbf{A}) \neq 0$.

</Callout>

Given the continuous-time LTI system:

$$\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B}u(t)$$

with parameters:

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad u_0 = 5$$

1. Compute the determinant $\det(\mathbf{A})$ and show that $\mathbf{A}$ is non-singular.
2. Determine the matrix inverse $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$.
3. Calculate the unique equilibrium state vector $\mathbf{x}_e = -\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B} u_0$.
4. Verify by direct matrix multiplication that $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e + \mathbf{B} u_0 = \mathbf{0}$.

2. Matrix inversion:
   $$\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(\mathbf{A})} \begin{bmatrix} -3 & -1 \\ -1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} -3 & -1 \\ -1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$$

   $$\mathbf{B} u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \cdot 5 = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 20 \end{bmatrix}$$

4. Algebraic verification:
   $$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e + \mathbf{B} u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 20 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (-2)(7) + (1)(9) \\ (1)(7) + (-3)(9) \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 20 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -5 \\ -20 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 20 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad \checkmark$$

<Exercise client:load id="ex-11-2" title="Exercise 11.2 — Singular Rouché-Capelli Analysis" level="Exam Standard">

Consider a system with a singular state matrix:

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -4 \\ -1 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad u_0 = 1$$

1. Compute $\det(\mathbf{A})$ and determine the rank of $\mathbf{A}$. Compute a basis vector $\mathbf{v}$ spanning the null space $\ker(\mathbf{A})$.
2. Apply the Rouché-Capelli theorem to test solvability for input vector $\mathbf{B}_1 u_0$. Find a particular solution $\mathbf{x}_{e,\text{part}}$ and write the complete parametric family of equilibrium states $\mathbf{x}_e(\alpha)$.
3. Apply the Rouché-Capelli theorem to input vector $\mathbf{B}_2 u_0$. Does an equilibrium state exist? Explain why by inspecting the column space $\operatorname{Im}(\mathbf{A})$.
4. Explain the physical dynamics of the system when excited by a constant input $\mathbf{B}_2 u_0 \notin \operatorname{Im}(\mathbf{A})$.

<StepCheck
  client:load
  prompt="What is the rank of the singular matrix $\mathbf{A}$?"
  expected={2}
  tolerance={0.01}
  unit=""
  explanation="$\det(\mathbf{A}) = 0$ (Row 3 $= -$Row 1 $-$ Row 2), while the top-left $2\times 2$ minor equals $2 \neq 0$, so $\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = 2$."
/>

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Determinant, rank, and null space:
   $$\det(\mathbf{A}) = 1(6 - 0) - (-2)(0 - 4) + 1(0 - (-2)) = 6 - 8 + 2 = 0$$
   Inspecting the top-left $2 \times 2$ minor:
   $$\det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = 2 \neq 0 \implies \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = 2$$
   Solving $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$:
   $$
   \begin{cases}
   v_1 - 2v_2 + v_3 = 0 \\
   2v_2 - 4v_3 = 0 \implies v_2 = 2v_3 \\
   -v_1 + 3v_3 = 0 \implies v_1 = 3v_3
   \end{cases}
   $$
   Choosing $v_3 = 1$ yields the null space basis vector:
   $$\ker(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{span}\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$$
2. Rouché-Capelli compatibility for $\mathbf{B}_1 u_0$:
   The linear equilibrium system is $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e = -\mathbf{B}_1 u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
   Forming the augmented matrix:
   $$[\mathbf{A} \mid -\mathbf{B}_1 u_0] = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & -4 & -2 \\ -1 & 0 & 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$
   Adding Row 1 to Row 3 produces $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -2 & 4 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = -1 \times \text{Row 2}$, so $\operatorname{rank}[\mathbf{A} \mid -\mathbf{B}_1 u_0] = 2 = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})$.
   By Rouché-Capelli, the system is compatible with $\infty^{3-2} = \infty^1$ solutions. Setting $x_{e3} = 0$ gives $2x_{e2} = -2 \implies x_{e2} = -1$ and $x_{e1} - 2(-1) = 0 \implies x_{e1} = -2$:
   $$\mathbf{x}_e(\alpha) = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \alpha \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \alpha \in \mathbb{R}$$
3. Rouché-Capelli compatibility for $\mathbf{B}_2 u_0$:
   The equilibrium system is $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_e = -\mathbf{B}_2 u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$:
   $$[\mathbf{A} \mid -\mathbf{B}_2 u_0] = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 & -4 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 3 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
   Adding Row 1 to Row 3 produces $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -2 & 4 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$, and adding Row 2 yields $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \implies 0 = -1$.
   Consequently, $\operatorname{rank}[\mathbf{A} \mid -\mathbf{B}_2 u_0] = 3 \neq \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = 2$, so $-\mathbf{B}_2 u_0 \notin \operatorname{Im}(\mathbf{A})$ and no equilibrium state exists.
4. Physical consequence:
   The input vector projects onto the unconstrained integrator mode; internal states can never produce a balancing counter-rate, causing continuous unbounded drift.

</details>

</Exercise>
