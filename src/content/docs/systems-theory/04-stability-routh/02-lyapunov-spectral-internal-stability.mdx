## Introduction

Once a machine sits at an equilibrium point, the immediate safety question is what happens after a nudge: if a wind gust tilts a drone or a load bump perturbs a generator, does the internal state snap back to rest, wander indefinitely near equilibrium, or run away exponentially? That intrinsic self-recovery behavior under zero external input is **Lyapunov internal stability**.

For linear time-invariant systems, stability reduces completely to the geometry of the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$ in the complex plane—with one subtle, critical edge case when repeated eigenvalues sit right on the imaginary axis ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) = 0$), where algebraic versus geometric multiplicity separates safe sustained oscillation from polynomial blow-up.

<Divider label="1 · Example" />

Compare three $2 \times 2$ autonomous systems $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t)$, all of whose eigenvalues satisfy $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) \le 0$:

$$
\mathbf{A}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{A}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ -2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{J} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Evaluating each system's trajectory $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}(0)$ from an initial perturbation $\mathbf{x}(0) = [x_1(0), x_2(0)]^T$ reveals three sharply different behaviors:

1. **Damped Decay ($\mathbf{A}_1$):** The diagonal entries give eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = -1$ and $\lambda_2 = -3$, both strictly in the open left-half plane ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) < 0$). Because $e^{\mathbf{A}_1 t} = \operatorname{diag}(e^{-t}, e^{-3t})$, the free response is:

   $$
   \mathbf{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} e^{-t} x_1(0) \\ e^{-3t} x_2(0) \end{bmatrix}
   $$

   Every initial displacement shrinks monotonically to zero as $t \to \infty$, so the origin is **asymptotically stable**.

2. **Harmonic Oscillator ($\mathbf{A}_2$):** Solving $\det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_2) = \lambda^2 + 4 = 0$ gives distinct purely imaginary eigenvalues $\lambda_{1,2} = \pm j2$ ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) = 0$, $m_a = m_g = 1$). Its state transition matrix is a pure rotation:

   $$
   e^{\mathbf{A}_2 t} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(2t) & \sin(2t) \\ -\sin(2t) & \cos(2t) \end{bmatrix}
   $$

   Because $\cos^2(2t) + \sin^2(2t) = 1$, the Euclidean norm satisfies $\|\mathbf{x}(t)\|_2 = \|\mathbf{x}(0)\|_2$ for all $t \ge 0$. Trajectories trace closed circles around the origin—staying permanently bounded without decaying—so $\mathbf{A}_2$ is **simply stable**.

3. **Defective Integrator Chain ($\mathbf{J}$):** Here $\det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{J}) = \lambda^2 = 0$, giving a repeated zero eigenvalue $\lambda_{1,2} = 0$ ($m_a(0) = 2$) that also satisfies $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) \le 0$. However, $\operatorname{rank}(0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{J}) = 1$, so the geometric multiplicity is $m_g(0) = 2 - 1 = 1 < m_a(0)$. Because $\mathbf{J}^2 = \mathbf{0}$, the matrix exponential series truncates at $t^1$:

   $$
   e^{\mathbf{J}t} = \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{J}t = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \implies \mathbf{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1(0) + t x_2(0) \\ x_2(0) \end{bmatrix}
   $$

   The off-diagonal coupling produces a growing secular term $t x_2(0)$: even an arbitrarily small initial velocity $x_2(0) \neq 0$ drives $\|\mathbf{x}(t)\| \to \infty$, making $\mathbf{J}$ strictly **unstable**.

### Perturbation Coordinates and Origin

Internal stability evaluates how a plant recovers from an initial energy displacement (such as a wind gust, mechanical tap, or stray capacitor charge) under **zero external forcing**. In [Equilibrium States in Linear Dynamic Systems](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/04-stability-routh/01-linear-time-invariant-equilibrium-points/), we proved that for any operating trim $(\mathbf{x}_e, \mathbf{u}_0)$, the deviation vector $\mathbf{z}(t) = \mathbf{x}(t) - \mathbf{x}_e$ satisfies $\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}(t) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}(t)$. Holding the input at its nominal trim ($\mathbf{v}(t) \equiv \mathbf{0}$) removes external forcing and leaves:

$$\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}(t)$$

Analyzing stability around any equilibrium $\mathbf{x}_e$ is therefore identical to analyzing the origin $\mathbf{x}_e = \mathbf{0}$ of the autonomous system $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t)$, whose free trajectory is $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}(0)$.

### Formal Lyapunov Definitions

Lyapunov formalized stability by comparing the size of the initial perturbation $\|\mathbf{x}(0)\|$ against the size of the resulting trajectory $\|\mathbf{x}(t)\|$ for all future time $t \ge 0$:

1. **Stability in the Sense of Lyapunov (Simple Stability):** The origin $\mathbf{x}_e = \mathbf{0}$ is *simply stable* if for every tolerance radius $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a smaller launch radius $\delta(\varepsilon) > 0$ such that:
   $$\|\mathbf{x}(0)\| < \delta \implies \|\mathbf{x}(t)\| < \varepsilon \quad \forall t \ge 0$$
   In words, any trajectory that starts close enough to equilibrium stays inside the chosen $\varepsilon$-ball forever, though it need not return to zero (as seen with $\mathbf{A}_2$ above).
2. **Asymptotic Stability:** The origin is *asymptotically stable* if it is simply stable **and** attractive: there exists $\delta_a > 0$ such that $\|\mathbf{x}(0)\| < \delta_a \implies \lim_{t \to \infty} \|\mathbf{x}(t)\| = \mathbf{0}$. Internal dissipation drains all perturbation energy so the state returns to rest.
3. **Instability:** The origin is *unstable* if it is not simply stable: there exists some threshold $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ such that even arbitrarily tiny perturbations ($\|\mathbf{x}(0)\| < \delta$ for any $\delta > 0$) eventually breach $\|\mathbf{x}(t_1)\| \ge \varepsilon_0$ at some finite time $t_1 > 0$.

Unlike nonlinear systems—where stability can hold only inside a small basin around equilibrium—asymptotic stability in continuous-time LTI systems is **strictly global** and **strictly exponential**. By linearity, if a trajectory from a unit ball decays to zero, scaling $\mathbf{x}(0)$ by any factor $R$ scales $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}(0)$ by $R$, so every initial state $\mathbf{x}(0) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ converges to zero. Because stability depends solely on $\mathbf{A}$, the matrix $\mathbf{A}$ itself is classified as asymptotically stable, simply stable, or unstable.

### Master Spectral Theorem

Every state transition matrix $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ can be decomposed via the Jordan canonical form $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ into $e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathbf{T}e^{\mathbf{J}t}\mathbf{T}^{-1}$, where each Jordan block of size $m$ for an eigenvalue $\lambda_i = \sigma_i + j\omega_i$ contributes modes of the form $\frac{t^k}{k!} e^{\sigma_i t} e^{j\omega_i t}$ ($0 \le k \le m - 1$). Internal stability is therefore governed completely by the eigenvalue spectrum $\sigma(\mathbf{A}) = \{\lambda \in \mathbb{C} \mid \det(\lambda\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = 0\}$, together with the algebraic multiplicity $m_a(\lambda_i)$ and geometric multiplicity $m_g(\lambda_i) = \dim(\ker(\lambda_i\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})) = n - \operatorname{rank}(\lambda_i\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$:

1. **Asymptotically Stable (Hurwitz Matrix):** All eigenvalues lie strictly in the open left-half plane:
   $$\boxed{\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0 \quad \forall i = 1, 2, \dots, n}$$
   Because every exponential factor $e^{-|\sigma_i|t}$ decays faster than any polynomial factor $t^k$ grows ($\lim_{t \to \infty} t^k e^{-|\sigma_i|t} = 0$), even repeated or defective eigenvalues inside the open LHP decay to zero.

2. **Simply (Marginally) Stable:** All eigenvalues satisfy $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) \le 0$, at least one lies on the imaginary axis ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_k) = 0$), and every imaginary-axis eigenvalue is **non-defective** (all its Jordan blocks have size $1 \times 1$, so $k = 0$ only):
   $$\boxed{m_a(\lambda_k) = m_g(\lambda_k) \quad \forall \lambda_k \text{ with } \operatorname{Re}(\lambda_k) = 0}$$
   With no decaying exponential on the $j\omega$-axis ($e^{0\cdot t} = 1$), size-$1$ blocks produce bounded constants or pure sinusoids $e^{j\omega_k t}$ that neither grow nor decay.

3. **Unstable:** Either at least one eigenvalue has strictly positive real part ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) > 0$, causing exponential blow-up $e^{\sigma_i t} \to \infty$), **or** at least one imaginary-axis eigenvalue ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_k) = 0$) is defective ($m_g(\lambda_k) < m_a(\lambda_k)$). A defective boundary eigenvalue creates at least one Jordan block of size $\ge 2$, introducing an untamed polynomial factor $t^k$ ($k \ge 1$) whose magnitude grows to infinity.

### Stability Margin and Envelope

When $\mathbf{A}$ is Hurwitz ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$), the slowest exponential decay rate among all eigenvalues defines the **stability margin** (or *degree of stability*) $\alpha$:

$$\alpha = \min_{i=1,\dots,n} |\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i)| > 0, \qquad \|\mathbf{x}(t)\| \le M \|\mathbf{x}(0)\| e^{-\alpha t}$$

Here $\alpha$ sets the asymptotic decay rate of the dominant pole closest to the imaginary axis, while the constant $M \ge 1$ accounts for any finite transient peaking caused by non-orthogonal eigenvectors or polynomial factors $t^k$ in defective LHP blocks.

<Callout type="bridge" title="Robotics Bridge: Structural Damping and Vibration Suppression">

In industrial robotic manipulators with elastic harmonic-drive joints, mechanical flexure introduces lightly damped poles $\lambda_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_n \pm j\omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$ close to the imaginary axis. For $\zeta = 0.005$ and $\omega_n = 200\;\text{rad/s}$, $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{1,2}) = -1.0\;\text{s}^{-1}$ gives a $1.0\;\text{s}$ time constant (over $4\;\text{s}$ settling). State feedback shifts these poles left to $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) = -20\;\text{s}^{-1}$, suppressing vibration in under $200\;\text{ms}$.

<Divider label="3 · Summary" />

| Classification | Eigenvalue Real Parts | Imaginary-Axis Multiplicity | Trajectory as $t \to \infty$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Asymptotically Stable** | All $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$ | None on $j\omega$-axis | $\mathbf{x}(t) \to \mathbf{0}$ exponentially |
| **Simply Stable** | $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) \le 0$, some $= 0$ | $m_g(\lambda_k) = m_a(\lambda_k)$ for all $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_k)=0$ | Bounded ($\lVert\mathbf{x}(t)\rVert \le M\lVert\mathbf{x}_0\rVert$) |
| **Unstable** | Any $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) > 0$, or $= 0$ | $m_g(\lambda_k) < m_a(\lambda_k)$ on $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_k)=0$ | Unbounded ($\lVert\mathbf{x}(t)\rVert \to \infty$) |

### Classification Procedure

1. Solve $\det(\lambda\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = 0$ for all eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ and their algebraic multiplicities $m_a(\lambda_i)$.
2. If any $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) > 0$, classify immediately as **unstable**.
3. If all $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$, classify as **asymptotically stable** and read $\alpha = \min_i |\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i)|$.
4. If $\max \operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) = 0$, compute $m_g(\lambda_k) = n - \operatorname{rank}(\lambda_k\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$ for every boundary root: **simply stable** iff $m_g = m_a$ on every $j\omega$-axis root, otherwise **unstable**.

### Key Proofs

- **Global Exponential LTI Bound:** Linearity scales any convergent $\delta_a$-neighborhood trajectory to all of $\mathbb{R}^n$, bounded by $M e^{-\alpha t}$ from the slowest eigenvalue real part.
- **Jordan Defect Secular Growth:** A $2 \times 2$ Jordan block for $\lambda_k = j\omega$ gives off-diagonal terms $t e^{j\omega t}$, whose magnitude grows linearly to infinity.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistakes">

1. **The Defective Imaginary Trap:** Never conclude stability solely from $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) \le 0$; a repeated eigenvalue on the imaginary axis (including $\lambda = 0$) is unstable whenever $m_g(\lambda_k) < m_a(\lambda_k)$.
2. **Repeated Poles in the Open LHP:** A defective eigenvalue with $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) < 0$ still yields $t^k e^{-|\sigma|t} \to 0$, so multiplicity defects inside the strict LHP do **not** break asymptotic stability.
3. **Mixing Simple and Asymptotic Stability:** Purely imaginary simple eigenvalues yield bounded oscillations, never convergence to zero.

</Callout>

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Matrix $\mathbf{A}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 0 \\ 4 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$ is lower triangular; its eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = -3, \lambda_2 = -2$.
   * Since $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_1) = -3 < 0$ and $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_2) = -2 < 0$, both natural modes decay exponentially.
   * **Classification:** **Asymptotically stable** ($\lim_{t \to \infty} \mathbf{x}_{\ell}(t) = \mathbf{0}$).
2. For $\mathbf{A}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 5 \\ -5 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$:
   $$\det(\lambda\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_2) = \det\begin{bmatrix} \lambda & -5 \\ 5 & \lambda \end{bmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 25 = 0 \implies \lambda_{1,2} = \pm j5$$
   * Both roots are purely imaginary and distinct ($m_a = m_g = 1$).
   * **Classification:** **Simply stable** in the sense of Lyapunov ($e^{\mathbf{A}_2 t} = \cos(5t)\mathbf{I} + \frac{\sin(5t)}{5}\mathbf{A}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(5t) & \sin(5t) \\ -\sin(5t) & \cos(5t) \end{bmatrix}$ yields bounded perpetual orbits).
3. Matrix $\mathbf{A}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$ is upper triangular; its eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = +1, \lambda_2 = -4$.
   * Because $\lambda_1 = +1 > 0$, mode $e^{+t}$ diverges exponentially.
   * **Classification:** **Unstable**.

<Exercise client:load id="ex-12-2" title="Exercise 12.2 — Defective vs. Diagonalizable Zero Modes" level="Exam Standard">

Consider two $3 \times 3$ systems both possessing a repeated zero eigenvalue on the imaginary axis:

$$\mathbf{A}_A = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{A}_B = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Compute the eigenvalues and state the algebraic multiplicity $m_a(\lambda)$ of each mode for both systems.
2. Evaluate $\operatorname{rank}(0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$ and compute the geometric multiplicity $m_g(0) = n - \operatorname{rank}(0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$ for both matrices.
3. Compute the matrix exponential $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ for both systems.
4. Classify the internal Lyapunov stability of $\mathbf{A}_A$ and $\mathbf{A}_B$. Explain why the presence of the secular term $t$ renders $\mathbf{A}_A$ strictly unstable despite having $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) \le 0$.

<StepCheck
  client:load
  prompt="Compute the geometric multiplicity $m_g(0) = 3 - \operatorname{rank}(0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_A)$ for system $\mathbf{A}_A$:"
  expected={1}
  tolerance={0.01}
  unit=""
  explanation="Since $0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_A = -\mathbf{A}_A$ has two linearly independent rows, $\operatorname{rank}(-\mathbf{A}_A) = 2$, so $m_g(0) = 3 - 2 = 1 < m_a(0) = 2$ (unstable)."
/>

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Characteristic polynomial and eigenvalues:
   For both $\mathbf{A}_A$ and $\mathbf{A}_B$:
   $$p(\lambda) = (\lambda + 2)\lambda^2 = 0$$
   * Mode $\lambda_1 = -2$: $m_a(-2) = 1$.
   * Mode $\lambda_{2,3} = 0$: $m_a(0) = 2$.
2. Rank evaluation and geometric multiplicity $m_g(0)$:
   * For $\mathbf{A}_A$:
     $$0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies \operatorname{rank}(0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_A) = 2$$
     $$m_g(0) = 3 - 2 = 1 < m_a(0) = 2$$
     The zero eigenvalue is **defective** with a $2 \times 2$ Jordan block.
   * For $\mathbf{A}_B$:
     $$0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies \operatorname{rank}(0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_B) = 1$$
     $$m_g(0) = 3 - 1 = 2 = m_a(0) = 2$$
     The zero eigenvalue is **non-defective** (diagonalizable into two $1 \times 1$ blocks).
3. Matrix exponentials:
   For $\mathbf{A}_A$, the upper-left $1 \times 1$ block gives $e^{-2t}$ and the lower-right $2 \times 2$ Jordan block $\mathbf{J}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ satisfies $\mathbf{J}_0^2 = \mathbf{0}$, so $e^{\mathbf{J}_0 t} = \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{J}_0 t = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$:
   $$e^{\mathbf{A}_A t} = \begin{bmatrix} e^{-2t} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & t \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad e^{\mathbf{A}_B t} = \begin{bmatrix} e^{-2t} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
4. Stability classification:
   * System $\mathbf{A}_B$ is **simply stable** in the sense of Lyapunov ($\|e^{\mathbf{A}_B t}\| \le 1$ for all $t \ge 0$).
   * System $\mathbf{A}_A$ is strictly **unstable**: for $\mathbf{x}(0) = [0, 0, 1]^T$, $\mathbf{x}_{\ell}(t) = [0, t, 1]^T$ has norm $\sqrt{t^2 + 1} \to \infty$ as $t \to \infty$.

</details>

</Exercise>
