## Concept and Definition of Internal Stability
### Physical Intuition: The Autonomous Perturbation Response
In engineering systems, stability answers a fundamental question:
> If a system in equilibrium is perturbed by an initial impulse or disturbance, will its internal state return to equilibrium, remain bounded near equilibrium, or violently diverge toward infinity?

Notice that stability is evaluated in the **absence of continuous external excitation**:
* When an aircraft hits a gust of wind, the gust imparts an initial angular velocity and attitude displacement. Once the gust passes, do the aircraft's aerodynamic restoring moments return it to level flight without pilot intervention?
* When a pendulum hanging downward is tapped, friction eventually dissipates the kinetic energy, returning it to the vertical resting position. If inverted upside down, the slightest disturbance causes it to accelerate away and topple.
* In an electrical RLC circuit with non-zero initial capacitor charge and inductor current, do the voltages and currents decay asymptotically to zero through resistive dissipation?

Because stability investigates the intrinsic dissipative or energetic properties of the physical plant itself, we evaluate the system under **zero external forcing** ($\mathbf{u}(t) \equiv \mathbf{0}$). This intrinsic property is called **internal state stability**.

### Bridging Perturbation Coordinates to the Origin
In [Equilibrium States in Linear Dynamic Systems](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/04-stability-routh/01-linear-time-invariant-equilibrium-points/), we proved that for any nominal operating equilibrium $(\mathbf{x}_e, \mathbf{u}_0)$, the deviation vector $\mathbf{z}(t) = \mathbf{x}(t) - \mathbf{x}_e$ satisfies:
$$\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}(t) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}(t)$$
When the external perturbation input is zero ($\mathbf{v}(t) \equiv \mathbf{0}$), the deviation dynamics reduce to:
$$\dot{\mathbf{z}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}(t)$$
Notice that when $\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{x}_e$, the deviation is at the origin $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$. Therefore, **analyzing the stability of any arbitrary physical equilibrium $\mathbf{x}_e$ is mathematically identical to analyzing the stability of the origin $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$ of the autonomous linear system**.

Without loss of generality, in all subsequent stability definitions, we analyze the unforced continuous-time system:
$$\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t), \quad \mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{x}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$$
whose dynamic trajectory is governed by the state transition matrix:
$$\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}_0$$

### Formal Mathematical Definitions

#### 1. Stability in the Sense of Lyapunov (Simple Stability)
The equilibrium point $\mathbf{x}_e = \mathbf{0}$ is **stable in the sense of Lyapunov** (or simply stable) if, for every chosen positive bound $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a corresponding initial perturbation radius $\delta(\varepsilon) > 0$ such that:
$$\|\mathbf{x}(0)\| < \delta \implies \|\mathbf{x}(t)\| < \varepsilon \quad \forall t \ge 0$$

*Physical Meaning:* If the initial disturbance is sufficiently small ($\|\mathbf{x}(0)\| < \delta$), the trajectory is guaranteed to remain permanently trapped within an $\varepsilon$-neighborhood of the origin. Trajectories cannot blow up, but they are not required to decay back to zero (for example, undamped harmonic oscillators that oscillate indefinitely with constant amplitude).

#### 2. Asymptotic Stability
The equilibrium point $\mathbf{x}_e = \mathbf{0}$ is **asymptotically stable** if:
1. It is stable in the sense of Lyapunov (bounded excursions), and
2. It is **attractive**: there exists an attractive radius $\delta_a > 0$ such that:
   $$\|\mathbf{x}(0)\| < \delta_a \implies \lim_{t \to \infty} \|\mathbf{x}(t)\| = \mathbf{0}$$

*Physical Meaning:* Not only do perturbations remain bounded, but internal physical dissipation (such as electrical resistance or mechanical friction) drains all stored dynamic energy, forcing all state variables to return asymptotically to the equilibrium point.

#### 3. Instability
An equilibrium point that is not stable in the sense of Lyapunov is defined as **unstable**. In this case, there exists at least one $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ such that, no matter how small the initial displacement $\delta > 0$ is chosen, at least one state trajectory starting within $\|\mathbf{x}(0)\| < \delta$ eventually breaches the boundary $\|\mathbf{x}(t_1)\| \ge \varepsilon_0$ at some future time $t_1 > 0$.

### Global and Exponential Properties of Linear Systems
In general non-linear systems, Lyapunov stability is strictly local (holding only within small neighborhoods $\delta$). 

In continuous-time **linear time-invariant systems**, however:
* Asymptotic stability is **strictly global**: if trajectories converge to zero from within some $\delta_a$, then by linearity, $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}_0$ converges to zero for **every** initial state $\mathbf{x}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$, no matter how large $\|\mathbf{x}_0\|$ is.
* Asymptotic stability is **strictly exponential**: there always exist finite positive constants $M \ge 1$ and $\alpha > 0$ such that:
  $$\|\mathbf{x}(t)\| \le M \|\mathbf{x}(0)\| e^{-\alpha t} \quad \forall t \ge 0$$

Because stability in LTI systems does not depend on the initial state $\mathbf{x}_0$ or the operating point $\mathbf{x}_e$, we say that **the system matrix $\mathbf{A}$ itself is asymptotically stable, simply stable, or unstable**.

## The Master Spectral Theorem
Because the state transition matrix $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ is composed of linear combinations of natural modes of the form $t^k e^{\lambda_i t}$, internal stability is governed entirely by the spectrum of eigenvalues of the system matrix $\mathbf{A}$:
$$\sigma(\mathbf{A}) = \{ \lambda \in \mathbb{C} \;:\; \det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = 0 \}$$

Let $\lambda_i = \sigma_i + j\omega_i$ denote the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$, where $m_a(\lambda_i)$ is the algebraic multiplicity and $m_g(\lambda_i) = \operatorname{dim}(\ker(\lambda_i \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}))$ is the geometric multiplicity.

### Operational Classification Criteria

1. **Asymptotically Stable:**
   The system is asymptotically stable if and only if **all eigenvalues have strictly negative real parts**:
   $$\boxed{\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0 \quad \forall i = 1, 2, \dots, n}$$
   A matrix $\mathbf{A}$ satisfying this property is called a **Hurwitz matrix**.

2. **Simply (Marginally) Stable:**
   The system is stable in the sense of Lyapunov, but not asymptotically stable, if and only if:
   * All eigenvalues satisfy $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) \le 0$,
   * At least one eigenvalue lies directly on the imaginary axis ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_k) = 0$), and
   * **Every eigenvalue on the imaginary axis is non-defective (simple Jordan blocks):**
     $$\boxed{m_a(\lambda_k) = m_g(\lambda_k) \quad \forall \lambda_k \text{ with } \operatorname{Re}(\lambda_k) = 0}$$
     Equivalently, all Jordan blocks corresponding to purely imaginary or zero eigenvalues have dimension $1 \times 1$.

3. **Unstable:**
   The system is unstable if and only if:
   * **Condition A:** At least one eigenvalue has a strictly positive real part:
     $$\exists \lambda_i \;:\; \operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) > 0$$
     or
   * **Condition B (Defective Imaginary Singularity):** At least one eigenvalue on the imaginary axis ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_k) = 0$) has geometric multiplicity strictly less than algebraic multiplicity:
     $$m_g(\lambda_k) < m_a(\lambda_k) \quad (\text{Jordan block size } \ge 2)$$

<Callout type="warning" title="Exam Trap: The Defective Imaginary Singularity Trap">

Many students assume that if all eigenvalues satisfy $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) \le 0$, the system is guaranteed to be stable. **This is a dangerous trap.**
If a repeated eigenvalue on the imaginary axis is defective ($m_g(\lambda) < m_a(\lambda)$), its Jordan form contains an off-diagonal 1:
$$\mathbf{J} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies e^{\mathbf{J}t} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
The secular term $t$ grows linearly without bound as $t \to \infty$. Consequently, $\|\mathbf{x}(t)\| \to \infty$, rendering the system strictly **unstable**!

</Callout>

## Stability Margin and Dominant Decay Envelope
When all eigenvalues satisfy $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$, the exponential rate of decay of the slowest mode is defined as the **stability margin** (or degree of stability) $\alpha$:
$$\alpha = \min_{i=1,\dots,n} |\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i)| > 0$$

The dominant pole closest to the imaginary axis establishes the upper bound envelope:
$$\|\mathbf{x}(t)\| \le M \|\mathbf{x}(0)\| e^{-\alpha t}$$

<Callout type="bridge" title="Robotics Bridge: Structural Damping and Vibration Suppression">

In industrial robotic manipulators with elastic harmonic drive joints, mechanical flexure introduces complex conjugate poles close to the imaginary axis:
$$\lambda_{1,2} = -\zeta \omega_n \pm j\omega_n \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$$
For joints with low damping ($\zeta = 0.005, \omega_n = 200\text{ rad/s}$):
$$\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{1,2}) = -0.005 \times 200 = -1.0\text{ s}^{-1}$$
The time constant of the decaying oscillation is $\tau = 1/\alpha = 1.0\text{ s}$, requiring over 4 seconds for vibration to subside. State feedback is synthesized to shift these poles to $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) = -20\text{ s}^{-1}$, increasing the stability margin twenty-fold and extinguishing vibration in under 200 ms.

</Callout>

## Chapter Exercises

<Exercise client:load id="ex-12-1" title="Exercise 12.1" level="LEVEL 1: WARM-UP DRILL">

Analyze the spectral properties and determine the internal stability classification (asymptotically stable, simply stable, or unstable) for each of the following autonomous systems:
1. $\mathbf{A}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 0 \\ 4 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$
2. $\mathbf{A}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 5 \\ -5 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
3. $\mathbf{A}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$
For each system, state the qualitative time-domain behavior of the free response trajectory $\mathbf{x}(t)$ as $t \to \infty$.

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Matrix $\mathbf{A}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 0 \\ 4 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$ is lower triangular; its eigenvalues are its diagonal entries: $\lambda_1 = -3, \lambda_2 = -2$.
   * Since $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_1) = -3 < 0$ and $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_2) = -2 < 0$, both natural modes decay exponentially.
   * **Classification:** **Asymptotically stable**.
   * **Time-domain behavior:** All unforced trajectories converge exponentially to the origin: $\lim_{t \to \infty} \mathbf{x}_{\ell}(t) = \mathbf{0}$.
2. For $\mathbf{A}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 5 \\ -5 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$:
   $$\det(\lambda\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_2) = \lambda^2 + 25 = 0 \implies \lambda_{1,2} = \pm j5$$
   * The eigenvalues are purely imaginary with $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) = 0$.
   * Both roots are distinct ($m_a(j5) = 1, m_a(-j5) = 1 \implies m_g = m_a = 1$).
   * **Classification:** **Simply stable (marginally stable)** in the sense of Lyapunov.
   * **Time-domain behavior:** The unforced trajectory forms a bounded, closed elliptical orbit oscillating perpetually at $\omega = 5\text{ rad/s}$ ($e^{\mathbf{A}_2 t} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(5t) & \sin(5t) \\ -\sin(5t) & \cos(5t) \end{bmatrix}$); trajectories neither decay to zero nor diverge to infinity.
3. Matrix $\mathbf{A}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$ is upper triangular; its eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = +1, \lambda_2 = -4$.
   * Because $\lambda_1 = +1 > 0$, the system contains an unstable natural mode $e^{+t}$.
   * **Classification:** **Unstable**.
   * **Time-domain behavior:** Any unforced state with non-zero projection onto the eigenspace of $\lambda_1$ diverges exponentially: $\lim_{t \to \infty} \|\mathbf{x}_{\ell}(t)\| = \infty$.

</details>

</Exercise>

<Exercise client:load id="ex-12-2" title="Exercise 12.2" level="LEVEL 2: EXAM BENCHMARK">

In a university written examination, consider two $3 \times 3$ systems both possessing a repeated zero eigenvalue on the imaginary axis:
$$\mathbf{A}_A = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{A}_B = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
1. Compute the eigenvalues and state the algebraic multiplicity $m_a(\lambda)$ of each mode for both systems.
2. Evaluate $\operatorname{rank}(0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$ and compute the geometric multiplicity $m_g(0) = n - \operatorname{rank}(0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$ for both matrices.
3. Compute the matrix exponential $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ for both systems.
4. Classify the internal Lyapunov stability of $\mathbf{A}_A$ and $\mathbf{A}_B$. Explain why the presence of the secular term $t$ renders $\mathbf{A}_A$ strictly unstable despite having no positive real parts ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) \le 0$).

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Characteristic polynomial and eigenvalues:
   For both $\mathbf{A}_A$ and $\mathbf{A}_B$, the block triangular/diagonal form yields:
   $$p(\lambda) = (\lambda + 2)\lambda^2 = 0$$
   * Mode $\lambda_1 = -2$: algebraic multiplicity $m_a(-2) = 1$.
   * Mode $\lambda_{2,3} = 0$: algebraic multiplicity $m_a(0) = 2$.
2. Rank evaluation and geometric multiplicity $m_g(0)$:
   * For $\mathbf{A}_A$:
     $$0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies \operatorname{rank}(0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_A) = 2$$
     $$m_g(0) = n - \operatorname{rank}(0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_A) = 3 - 2 = 1 < m_a(0) = 2$$
     The zero eigenvalue is **defective** with a non-trivial $2 \times 2$ Jordan block.
   * For $\mathbf{A}_B$:
     $$0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies \operatorname{rank}(0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_B) = 1$$
     $$m_g(0) = n - \operatorname{rank}(0\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}_B) = 3 - 1 = 2 = m_a(0) = 2$$
     The zero eigenvalue is **non-defective** (diagonalizable into two $1 \times 1$ Jordan blocks).
3. Matrix exponentials:
   * For $\mathbf{A}_A$:
     $$e^{\mathbf{A}_A t} = \begin{bmatrix} e^{-2t} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & t \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
   * For $\mathbf{A}_B$:
     $$e^{\mathbf{A}_B t} = \begin{bmatrix} e^{-2t} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
4. Lyapunov stability classification and secular term:
   * System $\mathbf{A}_B$ is **simply stable (marginally stable)** in the sense of Lyapunov. The state transition matrix is uniformly bounded ($\|e^{\mathbf{A}_B t}\| \le 1$ for all $t \ge 0$).
   * System $\mathbf{A}_A$ is strictly **UNSTABLE**. Even though all eigenvalues satisfy $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) \le 0$, the $(2,3)$ entry of $e^{\mathbf{A}_A t}$ is the secular polynomial term $t$.
     For initial state $\mathbf{x}(0) = [0, 0, 1]^T$:
     $$\mathbf{x}_{\ell}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}_A t}\mathbf{x}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ t \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \implies \|\mathbf{x}_{\ell}(t)\| = \sqrt{t^2 + 1} \to \infty \quad \text{as } t \to \infty$$
     The secular term drives the state to infinity, causing unbounded linear drift.

</details>

</Exercise>
