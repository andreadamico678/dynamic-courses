## Introduction

In ordinary arithmetic, hitting a zero in a denominator stops the calculation dead. Inside a Routh table, hitting a zero in the first column does something far more informative: it warns you that the system sits on a razor's edge of **spectral symmetry** or cancellation.

Rather than guessing or aborting, this chapter equips you with two clean algebraic rescues—the **$\varepsilon$-perturbation** for an isolated zero pivot and the **auxiliary polynomial** $A(s)$ for an all-zero row—allowing you to diagnose pure imaginary oscillations ($\pm j\omega$) and symmetric unstable roots ($\pm \sigma$) accurately.

<Divider label="1 · Example" />

Classify all roots of the fourth-order characteristic polynomial:

$$p(s) = s^4 + 3s^3 + 5s^2 + 9s + 6$$

1. **Build the Table Down to Row $s^1$:** Place alternating coefficients into rows $s^4$ ($[1, 5, 6]$) and $s^3$ ($[3, 9, 0]$). Dividing row $s^3$ by $3$ gives $[1, 3, 0]$. Computing row $s^2$ by cross-multiplication yields:

   $$
   b_1 = \frac{(1)(5) - (1)(3)}{1} = 2, \qquad b_2 = \frac{(1)(6) - (1)(0)}{1} = 6
   $$

   Scaling row $s^2$ ($[2, 6, 0]$) by $1/2$ gives $[1, 3, 0]$. Notice that row $s^2$ is now identical to the scaled row $s^3$, so the next row $s^1$ vanishes completely:

   $$
   c_1 = \frac{(1)(3) - (1)(3)}{1} = 0, \qquad c_2 = 0
   $$

   $$
   \begin{array}{c|lll}
   s^4 & 1 & 5 & 6 \\
   s^3 & 3 & 9 & 0 \quad (\div 3 \to [1,\, 3,\, 0]) \\
   s^2 & 2 & 6 & 0 \quad (\div 2 \to [1,\, 3,\, 0]) \\
   s^1 & 0 & 0 &
   \end{array}
   $$

2. **Resolve the Vanishing Row via $A(s)$:** An entire row of zeros signals that $p(s)$ contains an even factor $A(s)$ with origin-symmetric roots. Reading the scaled row $s^2$ ($[1, 3]$) as coefficients of $s^2$ and $s^0$ gives the **auxiliary polynomial**:

   $$A(s) = s^2 + 3 = 0 \implies s_{1,2} = \pm j\sqrt{3}$$

   To continue the Routh table past row $s^1$, differentiate $A(s)$ to obtain $\frac{dA}{ds} = 2s$, replace row $s^1$ with its coefficients $[2, 0]$, and compute row $s^0 = \frac{2(3) - 1(0)}{2} = 3$.
3. **Read the Completed Column:** The completed first column $[+1, +1, +1, +2, +3]^T$ has zero sign changes, proving $n_{\text{RHP}} = 0$. Dividing $p(s)$ by $A(s) = s^2 + 3$ leaves the quotient $Q(s) = s^2 + 3s + 2 = (s + 1)(s + 2)$, whose two roots lie in the open LHP. Thus $n_{\text{LHP}} = 2$, $n_{j\omega} = 2$, and $n_{\text{RHP}} = 0$: the system is **simply stable** with undamped oscillation at $\omega_0 = \sqrt{3}\;\text{rad/s}$.

### Classification of Singular Rows

The standard Routh algorithm in [Routh-Hurwitz Stability Criterion](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/04-stability-routh/03-routh-hurwitz-tabular-array-construction/) divides each cross-product by the leading pivot of the row immediately above. Whenever a pivot evaluates to zero, division by zero halts the ordinary table and signals one of two distinct algebraic phenomena:

1. **Singular Case 1 (Isolated First-Column Zero):** The leading entry of row $s^k$ is zero ($a_{k,1} = 0$), while at least one subsequent entry in that row is non-zero ($a_{k,j} \neq 0$ for some $j > 1$). This occurs because of an accidental numerical cancellation in the table, not because of origin-symmetric roots.
2. **Singular Case 2 (Complete Row of Zeros):** Every entry in row $s^k$ vanishes identically ($a_{k,j} = 0$ for all $j \ge 1$). This occurs **if and only if** $p(s)$ contains an even polynomial factor whose roots are symmetric about the origin.

### Isolated Zero Perturbation

When row $s^k$ begins with $0$ followed by at least one non-zero entry (for example, $[0, \; 5]$), apply the **$\varepsilon$-perturbation method**:

1. Replace the isolated zero in the first column with an infinitesimal positive parameter $\varepsilon > 0$.
2. Complete all remaining rows symbolically as rational functions of $\varepsilon$.
3. Evaluate the limit $\varepsilon \to 0^+$ in the first column to determine the sign of every entry and count sign changes.

To see why this works, consider a row $s^2 = [\varepsilon, \; b_2]$ below row $s^3 = [a, \; c]$ with $a > 0$ and $b_2 > 0$. The next first-column entry is:

$$c_1(\varepsilon) = \frac{\varepsilon c - a b_2}{\varepsilon} = c - \frac{a b_2}{\varepsilon}$$

As $\varepsilon \to 0^+$, the $-a b_2 / \varepsilon$ term dominates and drives $c_1(\varepsilon) \to -\infty$, while row $s^0$ evaluates to $b_2 > 0$. The resulting sign sequence $[+, \; +\varepsilon, \; -, \; +]$ exhibits two sign changes ($+\varepsilon \to -$ and $- \to +$). By continuity of polynomial roots, a tiny perturbation $\varepsilon > 0$ cannot push any root across the $j\omega$-axis when no roots sit on it; moreover, choosing $-\varepsilon$ instead of $+\varepsilon$ simply flips the sign sequence to $[+, \; -\varepsilon, \; +, \; +]$, which still yields the exact same two sign changes.

### Auxiliary Polynomial Symmetry

An entire row of zeros occurs **if and only if** $p(s)$ contains an even factor $A(s)$ (satisfying $A(s) = A(-s)$). Because $A(-s) = 0$ whenever $A(s) = 0$, and complex roots of real polynomials appear in conjugate pairs, every root $s_0$ of $A(s)$ forces $-s_0$, $\overline{s_0}$, and $-\overline{s_0}$ to be roots as well. This produces **quadrantal symmetry** in three possible geometries:

1. Purely imaginary conjugate pairs $\pm j\omega_0$ (marginal oscillation, $n_{j\omega}$).
2. Equal-and-opposite real pairs $\pm \sigma_0$ (one LHP root and one RHP root).
3. Complex quadruplets $\pm \sigma \pm j\omega$ (two LHP roots and two RHP roots).

Because $A(s)$ contains only even or odd powers separated by $2$, row $s^{k+1}$ immediately above the vanishing row $s^k$ holds the exact coefficients of $A(s)$ (stepping down by powers of $2$: $s^{k+1}, s^{k-1}, \dots$), and $A(s)$ divides $p(s)$ exactly as $p(s) = A(s)Q(s)$. To resolve Singular Case 2:

1. Form $A(s)$ from row $s^{k+1}$ immediately above the vanishing row $s^k$.
2. Differentiate to obtain $\frac{dA}{ds}$ and substitute its coefficients into the vanishing row $s^k$. Because $A(s)$ and $\frac{dA}{ds}$ share no roots unless $A(s)$ has repeated symmetric roots, replacing row $s^k$ with $\frac{dA}{ds}$ preserves the RHP root count of both $A(s)$ and the quotient $Q(s)$.
3. Resume the standard Routh algorithm down to $s^0$: the total number of sign changes in Column 1 equals $n_{\text{RHP}}$ across the entire polynomial $p(s)$.
4. Solve $A(s) = 0$ explicitly to separate any $j\omega$-axis roots ($n_{j\omega}$) from the remaining LHP roots ($n_{\text{LHP}} = n - n_{\text{RHP}} - n_{j\omega}$). In particular, when row $s^1$ vanishes below row $s^2 = [\alpha_1, \alpha_2]$, the auxiliary equation $A(s) = \alpha_1 s^2 + \alpha_2 = 0$ gives $s = \pm j\sqrt{\alpha_2/\alpha_1}$ whenever $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ share the same sign.

<Divider label="3 · Summary" />

| Singular Type | Table Symptom | Physical Root Geometry | Algebraic Remedy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Case 1: Isolated Zero** | $a_{k,1} = 0$, some $a_{k,j} \neq 0$ | Asymmetrical cancellation in table | Replace $0$ with $\varepsilon > 0$; take $\varepsilon \to 0^+$ |
| **Case 2: Full Zero Row** | $a_{k,j} = 0$ for all $j$ | Symmetric pairs ($\pm j\omega_0, \pm\sigma_0, \pm\sigma\pm j\omega$) | Build $A(s)$ from row $s^{k+1}$; replace row $s^k$ with $\frac{dA}{ds}$ |

### Resolution Procedure

1. Build rows normally until hitting either an isolated zero pivot (Case 1) or a full zero row (Case 2).
2. For Case 1, substitute $\varepsilon > 0$, complete all remaining rows in $\varepsilon$, and evaluate $\lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+}$ signs.
3. For Case 2, build $A(s)$ from row $s^{k+1}$, replace row $s^k$ with $\frac{dA}{ds}$, finish the table, and solve $A(s) = 0$ to separate $n_{j\omega}$ from $n_{\text{LHP}}$ and $n_{\text{RHP}}$.

### Key Proofs

- **Quadrantal Symmetry:** An even polynomial $A(s)$ satisfies $A(s) = A(-s)$, forcing roots to appear in origin-symmetric pairs ($\pm\sigma_0$, $\pm j\omega_0$) or quadruplets ($\pm\sigma \pm j\omega$); because $p(s) = A(s)Q(s)$ divides evenly, row $s^k$ vanishes.
- **Root-Sign Preservation via $\frac{dA}{ds}$:** Replacing row $s^k$ with $\frac{dA}{ds}$ preserves the RHP root count of $A(s)$ because $A(s)$ and $dA/ds$ share no roots unless $A(s)$ itself has repeated symmetric roots.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistakes">

1. **Ignoring $j\omega$ Roots Below $A(s)$:** Zero sign changes in Column 1 after using $dA/ds$ means $n_{\text{RHP}} = 0$, **not** asymptotic stability; always solve $A(s) = 0$ to identify $j\omega$-axis roots.
2. **Forgetting to Differentiate $A(s)$:** Never copy row $s^{k+1}$ directly into row $s^k$; you must compute $\frac{dA}{ds}$ first.
3. **Confusing Case 1 and Case 2:** Check the entire row $s^k$ before choosing a method; only use $A(s)$ when **every** entry in row $s^k$ is zero.

</Callout>

$$p(s) = s^4 + 2s^3 + 2s^2 + 4s + 5$$

2. Replace the zero entry with $\varepsilon > 0$ and express subsequent row entries as functions of $\varepsilon$.
3. Evaluate the signs of the first-column entries as $\varepsilon \to 0^+$.
4. Count sign variations, determine $n_{\text{RHP}}$ and $n_{\text{LHP}}$, and classify stability.

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Constructing the initial rows:
   * Row $s^4$: $1, \quad 2, \quad 5$
   * Row $s^3$: $2, \quad 4, \quad 0 \xrightarrow{\div 2} [1, \; 2, \; 0]$
   * Row $s^2$: $b_1 = \frac{1(2) - 1(2)}{1} = 0, \quad b_2 = \frac{1(5) - 1(0)}{1} = 5 \implies [0, \; 5]$
   An isolated zero appears at $b_1 = 0$ while $b_2 = 5 \neq 0$ (Singular Case 1).
2. $\varepsilon$-substitution:
   Replacing $0$ by $\varepsilon > 0$ gives row $s^2 = [\varepsilon, \; 5]$:
   * Row $s^1$: $c_1 = \frac{\varepsilon(2) - 1(5)}{\varepsilon} = \frac{2\varepsilon - 5}{\varepsilon}$
   * Row $s^0$: $d_1 = \frac{c_1(5) - \varepsilon(0)}{c_1} = 5$
3. Limit signs as $\varepsilon \to 0^+$:
   In row $s^1$, $c_1(\varepsilon) = \frac{2\varepsilon - 5}{\varepsilon} = 2 - \frac{5}{\varepsilon} \to -\infty$ as $\varepsilon \to 0^+$ (strictly negative), while $d_1 = 5 > 0$:
   $$\text{Column 1} = \left[ +1, \; +1, \; +\varepsilon, \; \frac{2\varepsilon - 5}{\varepsilon}, \; +5 \right]^T \xrightarrow{\varepsilon \to 0^+} [+, \; +, \; +, \; -, \; +]^T$$
4. Sign variations and stability:
   Two sign changes ($+\varepsilon \to -\infty$ and $-\infty \to +5$) yield $n_{\text{RHP}} = 2$ and $n_{\text{LHP}} = 4 - 2 = 2$; the system is **unstable**.

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Building the Routh array down to row $s^1$:
   * Row $s^4$: $1, \quad 5, \quad 6$
   * Row $s^3$: $3, \quad 9, \quad 0 \xrightarrow{\div 3} [1, \; 3, \; 0]$
   * Row $s^2$: $b_1 = \frac{1(5) - 1(3)}{1} = 2, \quad b_2 = 6 \implies [2, \; 6] \xrightarrow{\div 2} [1, \; 3]$
   * Row $s^1$: $c_1 = \frac{1(3) - 1(3)}{1} = 0 \implies [0, \; 0]$
2. Auxiliary polynomial resolution:
   From row $s^2$ (unscaled $[2, \; 6]$), $A(s) = 2s^2 + 6 = 2(s^2 + 3) = 0$ and $\frac{dA}{ds} = 4s$.
   Replacing row $s^1$ by $[4, \; 0] \xrightarrow{\div 4} [1, \; 0]$ gives row $s^0$: $d_1 = \frac{1(3) - 1(0)}{1} = 3$, with Column 1 $= [+1, \; +1, \; +1, \; +1, \; +3]^T$.
3. Roots of $A(s)$:
   $$s^2 + 3 = 0 \implies s_{1,2} = \pm j\sqrt{3}$$
4. Stability and complete root distribution:
   Dividing $p(s)$ by $s^2 + 3$ gives $Q(s) = s^2 + 3s + 2 = (s + 1)(s + 2)$, so $n_{j\omega} = 2$, $n_{\text{LHP}} = 2$, and $n_{\text{RHP}} = 0$. The system is **simply stable** (sustaining oscillations at $\omega = \sqrt{3}\;\text{rad/s}$).

</details>
