## Introduction

Physical systems rarely evolve in isolation—motors receive PWM voltage commands, aircraft wings feel continuous wind loads, and thermal chambers track setpoint profiles. When a system already holds initial stored energy ($\mathbf{x}_0$) at the moment an external input $\mathbf{u}(t)$ turns on, how do the two effects combine into a single trajectory?

Because the state-space equations are linear, the two contributions simply add together (**superposition**). **Lagrange's formula** expresses the forced component as a convolution integral that weights every past slice of input effort $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(\tau)$ by the state transition $e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)}$ remaining since instant $\tau$—and lets us separate fast decaying **transients** from long-term **steady-state** behavior.

<Divider label="1 · Example" />

Consider a first-order thermal chamber starting at initial temperature $x(0) = 5$ above ambient, driven at $t = 0$ by a constant unit-step heater command $u(t) = 1(t)$:

$$\dot{x}(t) = -2 x(t) + 4 u(t), \qquad y(t) = x(t)$$

Because the differential equation is linear, we can solve for the effect of $x(0)$ and the effect of $u(t)$ separately and add them together:

1. **Free Response:** Without the heater ($u(t) \equiv 0$, $x(0) = 5$), initial thermal energy decays exponentially at rate $A = -2$:
   $$x_l(t) = e^{-2t} \cdot 5 = 5e^{-2t}, \qquad y_l(t) = 5e^{-2t}$$
2. **Forced Response:** Starting from zero initial temperature ($x(0) = 0$, $u(t) = 1(t)$), Lagrange's convolution integral sums the heater effort $B u(\tau) = 4$ over all past instants $\tau \in [0, t]$:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   y_f(t) &= \int_0^t e^{-2(t-\tau)} \cdot 4 \, d\tau = 4e^{-2t} \int_0^t e^{2\tau} \, d\tau \\
   &= 4e^{-2t} \left[ \frac{e^{2\tau}}{2} \right]_0^t = 2e^{-2t}\left(e^{2t} - 1\right) = 2\left(1 - e^{-2t}\right)
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   In the Laplace domain, the exact same result follows from $W(s) = \frac{4}{s+2}$ and $U(s) = \frac{1}{s}$: $Y_f(s) = \frac{4}{s(s+2)} = \frac{2}{s} - \frac{2}{s+2}$, whose inverse transform is $2(1 - e^{-2t})$.

Adding both parts yields the complete trajectory, which regroups cleanly into **transient** and **steady-state** components:

$$y(t) = \underbrace{5e^{-2t}}_{y_l(t)} + \underbrace{2\left(1 - e^{-2t}\right)}_{y_f(t)} = \underbrace{3e^{-2t}}_{y_{\text{trans}}(t)} + \underbrace{2}_{y_{\text{ss}}}$$

Physically, the heater's upward buildup $2(1 - e^{-2t})$ partially offsets the initial $5e^{-2t}$ cooling, leaving a net $3e^{-2t}$ transient envelope. As $t \to \infty$, that transient vanishes and the chamber settles onto $y_{\text{ss}} = 2 = W(0)$, matching the DC gain $-C A^{-1} B = -(1)(-2)^{-1}(4) = 2$.

Solving the non-homogeneous state equation adds a forced convolution term to the homogeneous transition matrix. This section derives Lagrange's formula, relates its zero-state integral to the impulse response matrix, and separates transient modes from steady-state behavior.

### Non-Homogeneous State Evolution

While the free response analyzed in [Free State Response](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/03-time-response/02-free-state-response-evaluation/) tracks the release of initial stored energy under $\mathbf{u}(t) \equiv \mathbf{0}$, practical control systems are driven by active inputs: actuator commands $\mathbf{u}(t)$, reference trajectories, and external disturbances. As established in [First Order Differential State Equations](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/01-state-models/01-first-order-differential-state-equations/), complete state evolution is governed by the non-homogeneous LTI system:

$$\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}(t), \quad \mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{x}_0, \qquad \mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{C} \mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{D} \mathbf{u}(t)$$

Because $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t)$ continuously injects new state velocity at every instant, $e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}_0$ alone no longer satisfies the differential equation. We must find a closed-form solution that accounts for both $\mathbf{x}_0$ and the entire history of $\mathbf{u}(\tau)$ for $0 \le \tau \le t$.

### Lagrange Formula and Superposition

To solve $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(\tau) - \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(\tau) = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(\tau)$, we use the matrix integrating factor $e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}$. Multiplying both sides on the left by $e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}$ gives:

$$e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}\dot{\mathbf{x}}(\tau) - e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(\tau) = e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(\tau)$$

From [Matrix Exponential State Transition](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/03-time-response/01-matrix-exponential-state-transition/), $\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}\right) = -e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}\mathbf{A}$, so by the product rule the left-hand side collapses into a single exact derivative:

$$\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}\mathbf{x}(\tau)\right) = e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(\tau)$$

Integrating both sides from $\tau = 0$ to $\tau = t$ and using $e^{\mathbf{A}\cdot 0} = \mathbf{I}$ yields:

$$e^{-\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}(t) - \mathbf{x}(0) = \int_0^t e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(\tau) \, d\tau$$

Finally, multiply both sides on the left by $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ (the inverse of $e^{-\mathbf{A}t}$) and apply the semigroup composition law $e^{\mathbf{A}t}e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau} = e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)}$ to obtain **Lagrange's formula**:

$$\mathbf{x}(t) = \underbrace{e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}_0}_{\mathbf{x}_l(t) \text{ (Free State)}} + \underbrace{\int_0^t e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}(\tau) \, d\tau}_{\mathbf{x}_f(t) \text{ (Forced State)}}$$

Physically, the integral breaks the continuous input into infinitesimal impulses $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(\tau)\,d\tau$ delivered at each past instant $\tau$, propagates each impulse forward over the remaining elapsed duration $t - \tau$ via $e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)}$, and sums all contributions at time $t$.

Substituting $\mathbf{x}(t)$ into $\mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}(t)$ gives the complete output trajectory:

$$\mathbf{y}(t) = \underbrace{\mathbf{C} e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}_0}_{\mathbf{y}_l(t) \text{ (Free Output)}} + \underbrace{\int_0^t \mathbf{C} e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}(\tau) \, d\tau + \mathbf{D} \mathbf{u}(t)}_{\mathbf{y}_f(t) \text{ (Forced Output)}}$$

This decomposition expresses the **Superposition Principle**: every LTI trajectory is the exact sum of the **free response** (due solely to $\mathbf{x}_0$ with $\mathbf{u}(t) \equiv \mathbf{0}$) and the **forced response** (due solely to $\mathbf{u}(t)$ starting from rest $\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{0}$).

In the Laplace domain—linking back to [Transfer Function Matrix](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/04-transfer-function-matrix-invariants/)—the exact same split appears algebraically as:

$$\mathbf{X}(s) = (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_0 + (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{U}(s), \qquad \mathbf{Y}(s) = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_0 + \mathbf{W}(s)\mathbf{U}(s)$$

where $\mathbf{W}(s) = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D}$ is the transfer function matrix. For hand calculations, inverting $\mathbf{W}(s)\mathbf{U}(s)$ via partial fractions is usually far faster than evaluating the matrix-exponential integral directly.

### Impulse Response Matrix

Setting $\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ isolates the forced output $\mathbf{y}_f(t) = \int_0^t \mathbf{C} e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}(\tau) \, d\tau + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}(t)$. To express this mapping as a single operator independent of any particular input $\mathbf{u}(t)$, we test the system with a unit Dirac impulse on each input channel and define the **impulse response matrix** $\mathbf{W}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times m}$:

$$\mathbf{W}(t) \triangleq \mathbf{C} e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{B} \cdot 1(t) + \mathbf{D} \delta(t)$$

where $1(t)$ is the Heaviside unit step and $\delta(t)$ is the Dirac delta. Using the sifting property $\int_0^t \mathbf{D}\delta(t - \tau)\mathbf{u}(\tau)\,d\tau = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}(t)$, the forced output simplifies to the continuous **convolution integral**:

$$\mathbf{y}_f(t) = \int_0^t \mathbf{W}(t-\tau) \mathbf{u}(\tau) \, d\tau = (\mathbf{W} * \mathbf{u})(t)$$

Because convolution in time corresponds to multiplication in the $s$-domain, $\mathcal{L}\{\mathbf{W}(t)\} = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D} = \mathbf{W}(s)$. Thus, for a SISO system ($m = 1, p = 1$), the impulse response $w(t) = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{W(s)\}$ is the exact time-domain signature of the plant.

### Transient and Steady-State Partitioning

While splitting $\mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{y}_l(t) + \mathbf{y}_f(t)$ separates initial conditions from external inputs, control engineers often care more about *how fast the startup behavior fades* versus *where the system settles*. When $\mathbf{A}$ is asymptotically stable ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$ for all eigenvalues), $\lim_{t \to \infty} e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathbf{0}$, so all natural modes decay to zero. Under any bounded input $\mathbf{u}(t)$, the total response regroups into:

$$\mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{y}_{\text{trans}}(t) + \mathbf{y}_{\text{ss}}(t)$$

- **Transient Response $\mathbf{y}_{\text{trans}}(t)$:** Collects every decaying exponential term that vanishes as $t \to \infty$; it combines the free response $\mathbf{y}_l(t)$ with the decaying natural modes excited inside $\mathbf{y}_f(t)$ by $\mathbf{u}(t)$.
- **Steady-State Response $\mathbf{y}_{\text{ss}}(t)$:** The permanent regime surviving as $t \to \infty$.

For a constant step input $\mathbf{u}(t) = \mathbf{u}_0 \cdot 1(t)$, an asymptotically stable system has $\mathbf{A}$ invertible (no eigenvalue at $0$) and reaches equilibrium $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{0}$ as $t \to \infty$. Setting $\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{\text{ss}} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0$ gives $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ss}} = -\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_0$, and substituting into the output equation gives:

$$\mathbf{x}_{\text{ss}} = \lim_{t \to \infty} \mathbf{x}(t) = -\mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}_0, \qquad \mathbf{y}_{\text{ss}} = \lim_{t \to \infty} \mathbf{y}(t) = \left( -\mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D} \right) \mathbf{u}_0 = \mathbf{W}(0) \mathbf{u}_0$$

Notice that the exact same result follows from the Final Value Theorem in [Unilateral Laplace Transform](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/01-unilateral-laplace-transformation-primitives/): $\lim_{t \to \infty}\mathbf{y}(t) = \lim_{s \to 0} s\mathbf{W}(s)\frac{\mathbf{u}_0}{s} = \mathbf{W}(0)\mathbf{u}_0$, provided all eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$ lie strictly in the open left-half plane.

<Callout type="bridge" title="Manipulator Joint Trajectory Tracking">

In robotic manipulators (such as Universal Robots or Franka Emika arms), joint positions $\mathbf{q}(t)$ are driven by actuator torque commands $\boldsymbol{\tau}(t)$. Because gravity acts as a constant external disturbance $\mathbf{u}_{\text{dist}} = -\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q})$, Lagrange's formula proves that proportional-derivative (PD) control without an integrator leaves a persistent steady-state error:

$$\mathbf{e}_{\text{ss}} = -(\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B}\mathbf{K})^{-1} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{g} \neq \mathbf{0}$$

This explains why integral action (PID) or feedforward gravity compensation $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ff}} = \hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{q})$ is required to eliminate steady-state droop.

</Callout>

| **Free State** $\mathbf{x}_l(t)$ | $e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}_0$ | $\mathbf{x}_0$ | $\mathbf{0}$ | $\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{(s\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_0\}$ |
| **Forced State** $\mathbf{x}_f(t)$ | $\int_0^t e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(\tau)\,d\tau$ | $\mathbf{0}$ | $\mathbf{u}(t)$ | $\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{(s\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{U}(s)\}$ |
| **Forced Output** $\mathbf{y}_f(t)$ | $(\mathbf{W} * \mathbf{u})(t)$ | $\mathbf{0}$ | $\mathbf{u}(t)$ | $\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{\mathbf{W}(s)\mathbf{U}(s)\}$ |
| **Step Steady State** $\mathbf{y}_{\text{ss}}$ | $(-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D})\mathbf{u}_0$ | Any (if stable) | $\mathbf{u}_0 \cdot 1(t)$ | $\mathbf{W}(0)\mathbf{u}_0$ |

### Evaluation Procedure

1. Compute the free response $\mathbf{x}_l(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}(0)$ and $\mathbf{y}_l(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}_l(t)$ with $\mathbf{u}(t) \equiv \mathbf{0}$.
2. Compute the forced response in the $s$-domain via $\mathbf{Y}_f(s) = \mathbf{W}(s)\mathbf{U}(s)$ (or $\mathbf{X}_f(s) = (s\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{U}(s)$) and invert via partial fractions.
3. Add both terms by superposition: $\mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{y}_l(t) + \mathbf{y}_f(t)$, and regroup decaying vs. persistent terms into $\mathbf{y}_{\text{trans}}(t) + \mathbf{y}_{\text{ss}}(t)$.
4. When $\mathbf{A}$ is Hurwitz, cross-check step steady state with $\mathbf{y}_{\text{ss}} = \mathbf{W}(0)\mathbf{u}_0$.

### Key Proofs

- **Lagrange Integral:** Integrating $\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}\mathbf{x}(\tau)\right) = e^{-\mathbf{A}\tau}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(\tau)$ from $0$ to $t$ and multiplying by $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ yields $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}_0 + \int_0^t e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(\tau)\,d\tau$.
- **Impulse Kernel:** Setting $\mathbf{u}(t) = \delta(t)$ and $\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ in $\mathbf{y}_f(t)$ collapses the integral to $\mathbf{W}(t) = \mathbf{C}e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{B}\cdot 1(t) + \mathbf{D}\delta(t)$.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistakes">

1. **Free vs. Transient Confusion:** $\mathbf{y}_l(t)$ comes only from $\mathbf{x}_0$, whereas $\mathbf{y}_{\text{trans}}(t)$ collects all decaying terms from *both* $\mathbf{y}_l(t)$ and $\mathbf{y}_f(t)$.
2. **Invalid DC Gain Formula:** $\mathbf{y}_{\text{ss}} = (-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D})\mathbf{u}_0$ holds **only** when $\mathbf{A}$ is asymptotically stable ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$); if $\mathbf{A}$ has RHP or $j\omega$ eigenvalues, the limit does not converge to $\mathbf{W}(0)\mathbf{u}_0$.
3. **Manual Matrix Convolution:** For timed hand calculations, convert $\mathbf{x}_f(t)$ or $y_f(t)$ to the Laplace domain instead of evaluating $\int_0^t e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(\tau)\,d\tau$ directly.

</Callout>

$$\dot{x}(t) = -3 x(t) + 6 u(t), \quad y(t) = 2 x(t), \quad x(0) = 4$$

3. Apply the Superposition Principle to obtain the complete output trajectory $y(t) = y_l(t) + y_f(t)$.
4. Compute the steady-state value $y_{\text{ss}} = \lim_{t \to \infty} y(t)$ and verify using the static gain formula $y_{\text{ss}} = -C A^{-1} B = W(0)$.

1. For scalar system $\dot{x}(t) = -3 x(t) + 6 u(t)$ with $x(0) = 4$:
   $$x_l(t) = e^{At} x(0) = 4 e^{-3t} \implies y_l(t) = C x_l(t) = 2(4e^{-3t}) = 8 e^{-3t}$$
2. Transfer function and forced step response via Laplace inversion:
   $$W(s) = C(sI - A)^{-1}B = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{s + 3} \cdot 6 = \frac{12}{s + 3}$$
   $$Y_f(s) = W(s) \cdot U(s) = \frac{12}{s(s + 3)} = \frac{R_1}{s} + \frac{R_2}{s + 3}$$
   $$R_1 = \lim_{s \to 0} s Y_f(s) = \frac{12}{3} = 4, \qquad R_2 = \lim_{s \to -3} (s + 3) Y_f(s) = \frac{12}{-3} = -4$$

3. Complete output response via Superposition:
   $$y(t) = y_l(t) + y_f(t) = 8 e^{-3t} + 4(1 - e^{-3t}) = 4 + 4 e^{-3t}$$
   Boundary check: $y(0) = 4 + 4 = 8 = C x(0)$. $\checkmark$
4. Steady-state output:
   $$y_{\text{ss}} = \lim_{t \to \infty} (4 + 4 e^{-3t}) = 4$$
   Verification using $y_{\text{ss}} = -C A^{-1} B u_0 = W(0) u_0$:
   $$y_{\text{ss}} = -(2)(-3)^{-1}(6)(1) = 4 = W(0) \quad \checkmark$$

<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. From [Matrix Exponential State Transition](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/03-time-response/01-matrix-exponential-state-transition/), multiplying $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ by $\mathbf{x}(0) = [1, \; 0]^T$ gives:
   $$\mathbf{x}_l(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 2e^{-t} - e^{-2t} \\ -2e^{-t} + 2e^{-2t} \end{bmatrix} \implies y_l(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_l(t) = 4e^{-t} - 2e^{-2t}$$
2. Using the resolvent from Chapter 8:
   $$(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} = \frac{1}{(s+1)(s+2)}\begin{bmatrix} s+3 & 1 \\ -2 & s \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{(s+1)(s+2)}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ s \end{bmatrix}$$
   $$W(s) = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} = \frac{2}{(s+1)(s+2)}$$
   With $U(s) = \frac{3}{s}$, the forced output transform is:
   $$Y_f(s) = \frac{6}{s(s+1)(s+2)} = \frac{R_0}{s} + \frac{R_1}{s+1} + \frac{R_2}{s+2}$$
   Evaluating residues via Heaviside cover-up:
   $$R_0 = \left.\frac{6}{(s+1)(s+2)}\right|_{s=0} = \frac{6}{2} = 3, \quad R_1 = \left.\frac{6}{s(s+2)}\right|_{s=-1} = \frac{6}{-1} = -6, \quad R_2 = \left.\frac{6}{s(s+1)}\right|_{s=-2} = \frac{6}{2} = 3$$
   Inverting term by term gives:
   $$y_f(t) = 3 - 6e^{-t} + 3e^{-2t}$$
3. Total output trajectory via Superposition:
   $$y(t) = y_l(t) + y_f(t) = (4e^{-t} - 2e^{-2t}) + (3 - 6e^{-t} + 3e^{-2t}) = \underbrace{-2e^{-t} + e^{-2t}}_{y_{\text{trans}}(t)} + \underbrace{3}_{y_{\text{ss}}}$$
   Boundary check: $y(0) = -2 + 1 + 3 = 2 = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}(0)$. $\checkmark$
4. Static gain verification:
   $$\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \frac{1}{0 - (-2)}\begin{bmatrix} -3 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} -3 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
   $$y_{\text{ss}} = -\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}u_0 = -\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} -3 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}(3) = -\begin{bmatrix} -3 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}(3) = 3 \quad \checkmark$$
