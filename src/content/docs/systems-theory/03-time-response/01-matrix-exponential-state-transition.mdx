## Introduction

A scalar differential equation $\dot{x} = a x$ is solved immediately by $x(t) = e^{at} x(0)$. When $n$ state variables interact through a coupling matrix $\mathbf{A}$ in the state-space model $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ introduced in [First Order Differential State Equations](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/01-state-models/01-first-order-differential-state-equations/), the same exponential structure governs the homogeneous evolution, with the scalar $a$ replaced by the matrix operator $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$.

This operator underpins both continuous-time stability proofs and digital flight software, where autopilot firmware computes $\mathbf{A}_d = e^{\mathbf{A}\Delta t}$ to propagate vehicle states across each sample period $\Delta t$. Below, we define $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ via its convergent power series, prove the group properties of the state transition matrix, and apply three exact pencil-on-paper computation routes.

<Divider label="1 · Example" />

Consider an unforced damped second-order mechanical system—such as a mass-spring-damper governed by $\ddot{q} + 3\dot{q} + 2q = 0$—written in companion state coordinates $x_1 = q$ (position) and $x_2 = \dot{q}$ (velocity):

$$\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t), \qquad \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{x}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Here the system starts displaced by $1\;\text{m}$ from equilibrium with zero initial velocity. Because $x_1$ and $x_2$ are coupled through $\mathbf{A}$, we cannot simply exponentiate each entry of $\mathbf{A}t$ individually. Instead, we find the **state transition matrix** $\mathbf{\Phi}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ using the [State Resolvent Matrix](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/03-state-resolvent-matrix-inversion/) $(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}$.

First, form $s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}$ and compute its determinant:

$$s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & s \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} s & -1 \\ 2 & s + 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = s(s + 3) - (-1)(2) = s^2 + 3s + 2 = (s + 1)(s + 2)$$

Next, apply the $2 \times 2$ adjugate formula—swapping the diagonal entries and negating the off-diagonal entries—to invert $s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}$:

$$(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} = \frac{1}{(s+1)(s+2)} \begin{bmatrix} s + 3 & 1 \\ -2 & s \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{s+3}{(s+1)(s+2)} & \frac{1}{(s+1)(s+2)} \\ \frac{-2}{(s+1)(s+2)} & \frac{s}{(s+1)(s+2)} \end{bmatrix}$$

Now expand each of the four rational entries using [Heaviside partial fractions](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/02-partial-fraction-expansion-heaviside-residues/) at the simple poles $s = -1$ and $s = -2$:

- Entry $(1,1)$: $R_1 = \left.\frac{s+3}{s+2}\right|_{s=-1} = \frac{2}{1} = 2$, and $R_2 = \left.\frac{s+3}{s+1}\right|_{s=-2} = \frac{1}{-1} = -1$.
- Entry $(1,2)$: $R_1 = \left.\frac{1}{s+2}\right|_{s=-1} = 1$, and $R_2 = \left.\frac{1}{s+1}\right|_{s=-2} = -1$.
- Entry $(2,1)$: $R_1 = \left.\frac{-2}{s+2}\right|_{s=-1} = -2$, and $R_2 = \left.\frac{-2}{s+1}\right|_{s=-2} = 2$.
- Entry $(2,2)$: $R_1 = \left.\frac{s}{s+2}\right|_{s=-1} = -1$, and $R_2 = \left.\frac{s}{s+1}\right|_{s=-2} = 2$.

Assembling these residues gives the expanded resolvent matrix:

$$
(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{2}{s+1} - \frac{1}{s+2} & \frac{1}{s+1} - \frac{1}{s+2} \\ \frac{-2}{s+1} + \frac{2}{s+2} & \frac{-1}{s+1} + \frac{2}{s+2} \end{bmatrix}
$$

Taking the inverse Laplace transform $\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{s+a}\right\} = e^{-at}$ entry by entry yields the **state transition matrix**:

$$
e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \begin{bmatrix} 2e^{-t} - e^{-2t} & e^{-t} - e^{-2t} \\ -2e^{-t} + 2e^{-2t} & -e^{-t} + 2e^{-2t} \end{bmatrix}
$$

Each column of $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ is the trajectory produced by a unit basis initial condition: the first column gives the response from $[1,\; 0]^T$, and the second column gives the response from $[0,\; 1]^T$. Multiplying $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ by $\mathbf{x}(0) = [1, \; 0]^T$ selects the first column, giving $\mathbf{x}(t) = [2e^{-t} - e^{-2t}, \; -2e^{-t} + 2e^{-2t}]^T$, which starts at $[1, \; 0]^T$ at $t = 0$ and decays smoothly back to the origin as $t \to \infty$.

Generalizing the scalar exponential to $n \times n$ matrices requires a convergent series that preserves differentiation with respect to time. This section establishes the power series definition, proves the transition group laws, and develops the resolvent, diagonalization, and Cayley-Hamilton evaluation methods.

### Power Series Definition

In scalar calculus, $e^{at}$ is defined by its Maclaurin series $\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(at)^k}{k!}$. Because powers $\mathbf{A}^k$ and scalar multiplication are well-defined for any square matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, the exact same series defines the **matrix exponential**—canonically called the **state transition matrix** $\mathbf{\Phi}(t) \triangleq e^{\mathbf{A}t} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$:

$$
e^{\mathbf{A}t} \triangleq \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(\mathbf{A}t)^k}{k!} = \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{A}t + \frac{1}{2!} \mathbf{A}^2 t^2 + \frac{1}{3!} \mathbf{A}^3 t^3 + \dots
$$

Geometrically, $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ is the linear operator that takes any initial state $\mathbf{x}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$ at $t = 0$ and transports it forward in time to $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}_0$ along the system's natural flow.

To prove that $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}_0$ solves the homogeneous differential equation $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t)$ with $\mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{x}_0$, note that the series converges absolutely and uniformly on every finite interval $[0, T]$ for any finite matrix $\mathbf{A}$. We can therefore differentiate the series term by term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dt} e^{\mathbf{A}t} &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{A}t + \frac{1}{2!}\mathbf{A}^2 t^2 + \frac{1}{3!}\mathbf{A}^3 t^3 + \dots\right) \\
&= \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{A} + \frac{2}{2!}\mathbf{A}^2 t + \frac{3}{3!}\mathbf{A}^3 t^2 + \dots \\
&= \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\mathbf{A}^k t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}
\end{aligned}
$$

Re-indexing the sum with $m = k - 1$ (so $m$ runs from $0$ to $\infty$) and factoring $\mathbf{A}$ out on the left—or on the right, since $\mathbf{A}$ commutes with all its own powers—gives:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathbf{A} \sum_{m=0}^\infty \frac{(\mathbf{A}t)^m}{m!} = \mathbf{A} e^{\mathbf{A}t} = e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{A}
$$

Multiplying on the right by $\mathbf{x}_0$ confirms $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t)$, while substituting $t = 0$ leaves only the $k = 0$ term, giving $\mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{I}\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{x}_0$.

### Algebraic Group Properties

Before computing $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ for specific systems, we need to know how transition operators compose across time intervals and whether a state transition can ever lose information. Algebraically, $\mathbf{\Phi}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ forms a one-parameter group under matrix multiplication governed by four properties:

1. **Identity at Origin:** Setting $t = 0$ in the power series annihilates all terms with $k \ge 1$, leaving $e^{\mathbf{A}\cdot 0} = \mathbf{I}$. At zero elapsed time, no state has moved.
2. **Infinitesimal Generator:** Evaluating the derivative identity at $t = 0$ yields $\left.\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mathbf{A}t}\right|_{t=0} = \mathbf{A}e^{\mathbf{0}} = \mathbf{A}$. The matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is the instantaneous velocity field of the transition operator at $t = 0$.
3. **Semigroup Composition:** For any two durations $t_1, t_2 \in \mathbb{R}$, evolving for $t_1$ and then for $t_2$ equals evolving directly over $t_1 + t_2$:

$$
e^{\mathbf{A}(t_1 + t_2)} = e^{\mathbf{A}t_1} e^{\mathbf{A}t_2} = e^{\mathbf{A}t_2} e^{\mathbf{A}t_1}
$$

This follows because $\mathbf{A}t_1$ and $\mathbf{A}t_2$ commute, so the Cauchy product of their two power series matches the binomial expansion of $\frac{\mathbf{A}^k(t_1 + t_2)^k}{k!}$.

4. **Unconditional Invertibility:** Setting $t_1 = t$ and $t_2 = -t$ in the composition law gives $e^{\mathbf{A}t} e^{-\mathbf{A}t} = e^{\mathbf{A}(t - t)} = e^{\mathbf{0}} = \mathbf{I}$. Therefore, $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ is strictly non-singular for every finite $t$—even when $\mathbf{A}$ itself is singular—and its inverse is simply backward time evolution:

$$
\left( e^{\mathbf{A}t} \right)^{-1} = e^{-\mathbf{A}t}, \qquad \det\left(e^{\mathbf{A}t}\right) = e^{\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A})t} \neq 0
$$

Geometrically, this means trajectories of a linear system never merge or cross in finite time: knowing $\mathbf{x}(t)$ uniquely determines $\mathbf{x}(0) = e^{-\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}(t)$.

A common trap arises when combining two *different* matrices $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. Expanding both sides up to $t^2$ reveals why:

$$
e^{(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})t} = \mathbf{I} + (\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})t + \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{A}^2 + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}^2\right)t^2 + \dots
$$

$$
e^{\mathbf{A}t} e^{\mathbf{B}t} = \left(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{A}t + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{A}^2 t^2 + \dots\right)\left(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{B}t + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{B}^2 t^2 + \dots\right) = \mathbf{I} + (\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})t + \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{A}^2 + 2\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{B}^2\right)t^2 + \dots
$$

Subtracting the two expansions leaves a discrepancy of $\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} - \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})t^2$ at second order. Thus, $e^{(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})t} = e^{\mathbf{A}t}e^{\mathbf{B}t}$ holds for all $t$ **if and only if** $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ commute ($\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$).

### Resolvent Inverse Laplace

Summing the infinite power series directly is impractical by hand unless $\mathbf{A}$ is diagonal or nilpotent ($\mathbf{A}^k = \mathbf{0}$ for some $k$). To compute $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ in closed form for general $2 \times 2$ and $3 \times 3$ systems, we convert the differential equation $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t)$ into algebra using the [Unilateral Laplace Transform](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/01-unilateral-laplace-transformation-primitives/).

Applying $\mathcal{L}\{\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\} = s\mathbf{X}(s) - \mathbf{x}(0)$ to $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t)$ gives:

$$
s\mathbf{X}(s) - \mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}(s) \implies (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{X}(s) = \mathbf{x}(0) \implies \mathbf{X}(s) = (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{x}(0)
$$

Since we already know that the time-domain solution is $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}(0)$ for any initial vector $\mathbf{x}(0)$, taking the inverse Laplace transform identifies the matrix exponential with the inverse of the [State Resolvent Matrix](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/03-state-resolvent-matrix-inversion/):

$$
e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} \right\} = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ \frac{\operatorname{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})}{\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})} \right\}
$$

This identity turns matrix exponentiation into a systematic three-step algebraic routine: form $s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}$, invert it via the adjugate, and invert each scalar rational entry back to time $t$ via partial fractions.

### Spectral Eigenvalue Decomposition

When $\mathbf{A}$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\}$, we can understand $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ geometrically without computing adjugates. As shown in [Coordinate Basis Similarity Transformations](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/01-state-models/02-coordinate-basis-similarity-transformations/), assembling those eigenvectors into the modal matrix $\mathbf{T} = [\mathbf{v}_1 \; \dots \; \mathbf{v}_n]$ diagonalizes $\mathbf{A}$ into $\mathbf{\Lambda} = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ via $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{T}^{-1}$.

Every power of $\mathbf{A}$ inherits the same similarity factorization because the inner $\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{I}$ pairs cancel:

$$
\mathbf{A}^2 = (\mathbf{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{T}^{-1})(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{T}^{-1}) = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}^2\mathbf{T}^{-1}, \qquad \mathbf{A}^k = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}^k\mathbf{T}^{-1}
$$

Substituting $\mathbf{A}^k = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}^k\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ into the power series definition and factoring $\mathbf{T}$ on the left and $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ on the right yields:

$$
e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{t^k}{k!} \mathbf{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}^k\mathbf{T}^{-1} = \mathbf{T}\left(\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(\mathbf{\Lambda}t)^k}{k!}\right)\mathbf{T}^{-1} = \mathbf{T} e^{\mathbf{\Lambda}t} \mathbf{T}^{-1}
$$

Because $\mathbf{\Lambda}$ is diagonal, its exponential is simply the diagonal matrix of scalar exponentials, which expands into a sum of rank-one modal dyads:

$$
e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathbf{T} \begin{bmatrix} e^{\lambda_1 t} & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & e^{\lambda_2 t} & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & e^{\lambda_n t} \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{T}^{-1} = \sum_{i=1}^n e^{\lambda_i t} \mathbf{v}_i \mathbf{w}_i^T
$$

Here $\mathbf{w}_i^T$ denotes the $i$-th row of $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ (the $i$-th left eigenvector). Physically, $\mathbf{w}_i^T \mathbf{x}_0$ measures how much of mode $i$ is present in the initial state, $e^{\lambda_i t}$ scales that mode over time, and $\mathbf{v}_i$ places it back into physical state coordinates.

### Cayley-Hamilton Polynomial Expansion

What if $\mathbf{A}$ is *not* diagonalizable (defective), or what if we want an analytical formula without inverting $s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}$? The **Cayley-Hamilton theorem** provides a third exact route by collapsing the infinite power series into a finite polynomial of degree at most $n - 1$.

The theorem states that every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation $p(\lambda) = \det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = \lambda^n + a_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 \lambda + a_0 = 0$:

$$
p(\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{A}^n + a_{n-1}\mathbf{A}^{n-1} + \dots + a_1\mathbf{A} + a_0\mathbf{I} = \mathbf{0}
$$

Solving for $\mathbf{A}^n$ expresses the $n$-th power as a linear combination of $\{\mathbf{I}, \mathbf{A}, \dots, \mathbf{A}^{n-1}\}$. Multiplying that identity by $\mathbf{A}$ shows that $\mathbf{A}^{n+1}$ also reduces to $\{\mathbf{I}, \mathbf{A}, \dots, \mathbf{A}^{n-1}\}$, and by induction *every* higher power $\mathbf{A}^k$ ($k \ge n$) collapses into the same finite basis. Grouping all resulting coefficients into $n$ scalar functions $\alpha_0(t), \dots, \alpha_{n-1}(t)$ yields:

$$
e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \alpha_0(t)\mathbf{I} + \alpha_1(t)\mathbf{A} + \alpha_2(t)\mathbf{A}^2 + \dots + \alpha_{n-1}(t)\mathbf{A}^{n-1}
$$

To determine the functions $\alpha_k(t)$, divide the analytic function $f(\lambda) = e^{\lambda t}$ by the characteristic polynomial $p(\lambda)$: $e^{\lambda t} = q(\lambda)p(\lambda) + r(\lambda)$, where the remainder $r(\lambda) = \alpha_0(t) + \alpha_1(t)\lambda + \dots + \alpha_{n-1}(t)\lambda^{n-1}$ has degree at most $n - 1$. Because $p(\lambda_i) = 0$ at every eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, the quotient term vanishes and we obtain $n$ scalar equations:

$$
e^{\lambda_i t} = \alpha_0(t) + \alpha_1(t)\lambda_i + \alpha_2(t)\lambda_i^2 + \dots + \alpha_{n-1}(t)\lambda_i^{n-1}, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n
$$

When $\mathbf{A}$ has a repeated eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ of algebraic multiplicity $m > 1$, the first $m - 1$ derivatives of $p(\lambda)$ also vanish at $\lambda_i$. Differentiating $e^{\lambda t} = q(\lambda)p(\lambda) + r(\lambda)$ with respect to $\lambda$ up to order $m - 1$ therefore yields the remaining independent equations:

$$
\left.\frac{d^k}{d\lambda^k}\left(e^{\lambda t}\right)\right|_{\lambda = \lambda_i} = \left.\frac{d^k}{d\lambda^k}\left(\alpha_0(t) + \alpha_1(t)\lambda + \dots + \alpha_{n-1}(t)\lambda^{n-1}\right)\right|_{\lambda = \lambda_i}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, m - 1
$$

Notice that this method works uniformly whether $\mathbf{A}$ is diagonalizable or defective, because it depends only on the roots of $p(\lambda)$ and never requires computing eigenvectors.

<Callout type="bridge" title="Discretization in Flight Loops">

Physical microcontrollers in autonomous drones (such as the STM32H7 on a Pixhawk flight controller) cannot execute continuous-time differential equations $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}$. Instead, the continuous state-space model is discretized at a fixed sample period $T_s$ (e.g., $T_s = 2.5\;\text{ms}$ for a $400\;\text{Hz}$ inner rate loop):

$$\mathbf{x}[k+1] = \mathbf{A}_d \mathbf{x}[k] + \mathbf{B}_d \mathbf{u}[k]$$

Using exact zero-order hold (ZOH) integration, the discrete matrices are $\mathbf{A}_d = e^{\mathbf{A} T_s}$ and $\mathbf{B}_d = \left( \int_0^{T_s} e^{\mathbf{A}\tau}\,d\tau \right) \mathbf{B}$. While slow loops may approximate $\mathbf{A}_d \approx \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{A} T_s$, high-speed aerobatic maneuvers require higher-order matrix exponential terms $\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{A}^2 T_s^2$ to prevent attitude estimator divergence.

</Callout>

<Divider label="3 · Summary" />

| Method | Formula | When to Use |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Power Series** | $\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(\mathbf{A}t)^k}{k!}$ | Nilpotent or small diagonal matrices |
| **Resolvent Laplace** | $\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{(s\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}\}$ | General $2 \times 2$ and $3 \times 3$ matrices |
| **Spectral Decomposition** | $\mathbf{T} e^{\mathbf{\Lambda} t} \mathbf{T}^{-1}$ | Diagonalizable matrices with known eigenbasis |
| **Cayley-Hamilton** | $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \alpha_k(t) \mathbf{A}^k$ | Symbolic evaluation or repeated defective roots |

### Evaluation Procedure

1. Form $s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}$ and compute the characteristic polynomial $\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$.
2. Invert via $\operatorname{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) / \det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$ (or use Cayley-Hamilton when eigenvalues are already known).
3. Expand each rational entry into partial fractions and apply $\mathcal{L}^{-1}$ term by term.
4. Verify your matrix exponential by checking $e^{\mathbf{A}\cdot 0} = \mathbf{I}$ and $\left.\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mathbf{A}t}\right|_{t=0} = \mathbf{A}$.

### Key Proofs

- **Differentiation & Group Law:** Term-by-term differentiation of the uniformly convergent power series proves $\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathbf{A}e^{\mathbf{A}t} = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{A}$ and $(e^{\mathbf{A}t})^{-1} = e^{-\mathbf{A}t}$.
- **Resolvent Identity:** Taking the unilateral Laplace transform of $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ yields $\mathbf{X}(s) = (s\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_0$, identifying $e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{(s\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}\}$.
- **Cayley-Hamilton Reduction:** Because $p(\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{0}$, every power $\mathbf{A}^k$ ($k \ge n$) reduces to a combination of $\{\mathbf{I}, \mathbf{A}, \dots, \mathbf{A}^{n-1}\}$.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistakes">

1. **Element-Wise Exponential:** Never compute $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ by exponentiating each entry $[e^{a_{ij}t}]$; that only works when $\mathbf{A}$ is strictly diagonal.
2. **Assuming Commutativity:** Never factor $e^{(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})t}$ as $e^{\mathbf{A}t}e^{\mathbf{B}t}$ unless you have verified $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$.
3. **Adjugate Sign Errors:** In $2\times 2$ adjugates, swap the diagonal entries of $s\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}$ and negate only the off-diagonal entries.

</Callout>

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. For upper-triangular matrix $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$:
   $$s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} s + 1 & -2 \\ 0 & s + 3 \end{bmatrix}$$
   The determinant of an upper-triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal elements:
   $$\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = (s + 1)(s + 3)$$
2. The adjugate matrix swaps diagonal elements and negates off-diagonal elements:
   $$\operatorname{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = \begin{bmatrix} s + 3 & 2 \\ 0 & s + 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
   The resolvent matrix is:
   $$(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} = \frac{1}{(s+1)(s+3)}\begin{bmatrix} s + 3 & 2 \\ 0 & s + 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{s+1} & \frac{2}{(s+1)(s+3)} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{s+3} \end{bmatrix}$$
3. Expanding entry $(1,2)$ via Heaviside partial fractions:
   $$\frac{2}{(s+1)(s+3)} = \frac{R_1}{s+1} + \frac{R_2}{s+3}$$
   $$R_1 = \left.\frac{2}{s+3}\right|_{s=-1} = \frac{2}{-1+3} = 1, \qquad R_2 = \left.\frac{2}{s+1}\right|_{s=-3} = \frac{2}{-3+1} = -1$$
   $$\frac{2}{(s+1)(s+3)} = \frac{1}{s+1} - \frac{1}{s+3}$$
4. Taking the inverse Laplace transform element by element:
   $$e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \begin{bmatrix} e^{-t} & e^{-t} - e^{-3t} \\ 0 & e^{-3t} \end{bmatrix}$$
   Evaluating at $t = 0$:
   $$e^{\mathbf{A}\cdot 0} = \begin{bmatrix} e^0 & e^0 - e^0 \\ 0 & e^0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{I} \quad \checkmark$$

Given the standard coupled companion state matrix:

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -6 & -5 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Formulate the resolvent matrix $(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}$ using the adjugate method.
2. Perform partial fraction expansion on all four scalar rational entries of $(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}$.
3. Take the inverse Laplace transform to obtain the explicit analytical transition matrix $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$.
4. Verify that $e^{\mathbf{A}\cdot 0} = \mathbf{I}$ and $\left.\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mathbf{A}t}\right|_{t=0} = \mathbf{A}$.

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Setting up $s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}$:
   $$s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} s & -1 \\ 6 & s + 5 \end{bmatrix}$$
   Determinant:
   $$\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = s(s + 5) - (-1)(6) = s^2 + 5s + 6 = (s + 2)(s + 3)$$
   The adjugate matrix is:
   $$\operatorname{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = \begin{bmatrix} s + 5 & 1 \\ -6 & s \end{bmatrix}$$
   The resolvent matrix is:
   $$(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} = \frac{1}{(s+2)(s+3)}\begin{bmatrix} s + 5 & 1 \\ -6 & s \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{s+5}{(s+2)(s+3)} & \frac{1}{(s+2)(s+3)} \\ \frac{-6}{(s+2)(s+3)} & \frac{s}{(s+2)(s+3)} \end{bmatrix}$$
2. Decomposing each entry into partial fractions via Heaviside cover-up at $s = -2$ and $s = -3$:
   - Entry $(1,1)$: $\left.\frac{s+5}{s+3}\right|_{s=-2} = \frac{3}{1} = 3$ and $\left.\frac{s+5}{s+2}\right|_{s=-3} = \frac{2}{-1} = -2$, so $\frac{s+5}{(s+2)(s+3)} = \frac{3}{s+2} - \frac{2}{s+3}$.
   - Entry $(1,2)$: $\left.\frac{1}{s+3}\right|_{s=-2} = 1$ and $\left.\frac{1}{s+2}\right|_{s=-3} = -1$, so $\frac{1}{(s+2)(s+3)} = \frac{1}{s+2} - \frac{1}{s+3}$.
   - Entry $(2,1)$: $\left.\frac{-6}{s+3}\right|_{s=-2} = -6$ and $\left.\frac{-6}{s+2}\right|_{s=-3} = 6$, so $\frac{-6}{(s+2)(s+3)} = \frac{-6}{s+2} + \frac{6}{s+3}$.
   - Entry $(2,2)$: $\left.\frac{s}{s+3}\right|_{s=-2} = -2$ and $\left.\frac{s}{s+2}\right|_{s=-3} = 3$, so $\frac{s}{(s+2)(s+3)} = \frac{-2}{s+2} + \frac{3}{s+3}$.
3. Taking the inverse Laplace transform element by element:
   $$e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \begin{bmatrix} 3e^{-2t} - 2e^{-3t} & e^{-2t} - e^{-3t} \\ -6e^{-2t} + 6e^{-3t} & -2e^{-2t} + 3e^{-3t} \end{bmatrix}$$
4. Evaluating at $t = 0$:
   $$e^{\mathbf{A}\cdot 0} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 - 2 & 1 - 1 \\ -6 + 6 & -2 + 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{I} \quad \checkmark$$

   $$\left.\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mathbf{A}t}\right|_{t=0} = \begin{bmatrix} -6 + 6 & -2 + 3 \\ 12 - 18 & 4 - 9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -6 & -5 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{A} \quad \checkmark$$

<Exercise client:load id="ex-8-3" title="Exercise 8.3 — Defective Matrix via Cayley-Hamilton" level="Exam Standard">

Consider the non-diagonalizable (defective) matrix:

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Compute the characteristic polynomial $p(\lambda) = \det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$ and determine the algebraic multiplicity of its eigenvalue.
2. Using the Cayley-Hamilton expansion $e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \alpha_0(t)\mathbf{I} + \alpha_1(t)\mathbf{A}$ and the derivative condition for repeated roots, solve for $\alpha_0(t)$ and $\alpha_1(t)$.
3. Compute $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ explicitly and cross-check your result against $\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\}$.

<StepCheck
  client:load
  prompt="Evaluate the $(1,2)$ entry of $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ at $t = 1\;\mathrm{s}$ (3 decimal places, using $e^{-2} \approx 0.1353$):"
  expected={0.135}
  tolerance={0.005}
  unit=""
  explanation="The $(1,2)$ entry is $t e^{-2t}$. At $t = 1\;\mathrm{s}$, $1 \cdot e^{-2} \approx 0.1353 \approx 0.135$."
/>

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Characteristic polynomial:
   $$p(\lambda) = \det\begin{bmatrix} \lambda + 2 & -1 \\ 0 & \lambda + 2 \end{bmatrix} = (\lambda + 2)^2 = 0$$
   The single eigenvalue is $\lambda_1 = -2$ with algebraic multiplicity $m_a = 2$.
2. Write the Cayley-Hamilton remainder polynomial $r(\lambda) = \alpha_0(t) + \alpha_1(t)\lambda$. Evaluating $e^{\lambda t} = r(\lambda)$ and its first derivative with respect to $\lambda$ at $\lambda = -2$:
   - Zero-order ($k = 0$): $e^{-2t} = \alpha_0(t) - 2\alpha_1(t)$
   - First derivative ($k = 1$): $\left.\frac{d}{d\lambda}(e^{\lambda t})\right|_{\lambda=-2} = t e^{-2t} = \alpha_1(t)$
   Substituting $\alpha_1(t) = t e^{-2t}$ into the first equation:
   $$\alpha_0(t) = e^{-2t} + 2t e^{-2t} = (1 + 2t)e^{-2t}$$
3. Substituting $\alpha_0(t)$ and $\alpha_1(t)$ into $e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \alpha_0(t)\mathbf{I} + \alpha_1(t)\mathbf{A}$:
   $$e^{\mathbf{A}t} = (1 + 2t)e^{-2t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + t e^{-2t}\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (1 + 2t - 2t)e^{-2t} & t e^{-2t} \\ 0 & (1 + 2t - 2t)e^{-2t} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} e^{-2t} & t e^{-2t} \\ 0 & e^{-2t} \end{bmatrix}$$
   Cross-check via the resolvent:
   $$(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} s+2 & -1 \\ 0 & s+2 \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{(s+2)^2}\begin{bmatrix} s+2 & 1 \\ 0 & s+2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{s+2} & \frac{1}{(s+2)^2} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{s+2} \end{bmatrix}$$
   Since $\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{(s+2)^2}\right\} = t e^{-2t}$, both methods give the exact same matrix. $\checkmark$

</details>

</Exercise>
