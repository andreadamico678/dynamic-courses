## Power Series Definition and Analytical Foundations

The scalar differential equation $\dot{x}(t) = a x(t)$ with initial condition $x(0) = x_0$ has the classical exponential solution $x(t) = e^{at} x_0$. For a continuous-time multivariable linear system governed by the homogeneous vector equation:

$$\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}(t), \quad \mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{x}_0$$

the closed-form solution requires defining the exponential of a square matrix operator $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$.

<Callout type="note" title="Definition: The Matrix Exponential">

The **matrix exponential** (or state transition operator) $e^{\mathbf{A}t} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is defined by the globally convergent Taylor power series:
$$e^{\mathbf{A}t} \triangleq \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(\mathbf{A}t)^k}{k!} = \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{A}t + \frac{1}{2!} \mathbf{A}^2 t^2 + \frac{1}{3!} \mathbf{A}^3 t^3 + \dots$$

</Callout>

Because the series the equation above converges absolutely and uniformly on every compact time interval $[0, T]$ for any matrix $\mathbf{A}$ of finite norm, it can be differentiated term-by-term with respect to time $t$:

$$\frac{d}{dt} e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{k \mathbf{A}^k t^{k-1}}{k!} = \mathbf{A} \left( \sum_{m=0}^\infty \frac{\mathbf{A}^m t^m}{m!} \right) = \mathbf{A} e^{\mathbf{A}t} = e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{A}$$

Consequently, the trajectory $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}_0$ satisfies the dynamic equation the equation above with exact initial condition:

$$\mathbf{x}(0) = e^{\mathbf{A} \cdot 0} \mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{I} \mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{x}_0$$

The matrix function $\mathbf{\Phi}(t) \triangleq e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ is canonically termed the **State Transition Matrix**.

## Fundamental Group Properties of the State Transition Matrix

The operator $\mathbf{\Phi}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ forms a continuous one-parameter Lie group under matrix multiplication, satisfying four core algebraic axioms:

1. **Identity Action at Origin:**
   $$e^{\mathbf{A} \cdot 0} = \mathbf{I}$$
2. **Infinitesimal Generator:**
   $$\left. \frac{d}{dt} e^{\mathbf{A}t} \right|_{t=0} = \mathbf{A}$$
3. **Semigroup Composition (Time Additivity):**
   $$e^{\mathbf{A}(t_1 + t_2)} = e^{\mathbf{A}t_1} e^{\mathbf{A}t_2} = e^{\mathbf{A}t_2} e^{\mathbf{A}t_1}$$
   *Proof:* Moving from $t=0$ to $t_1$, and then evolving by an additional duration $t_2$, is equivalent to evolving directly over duration $t_1 + t_2$.
4. **Unconditional Non-Singular Invertibility:**
   For any matrix $\mathbf{A}$ and finite time $t$, $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ is strictly non-singular ($\det(e^{\mathbf{A}t}) = e^{\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A})t} \neq 0$). Its inverse is simply the time-reversed transition:
   $$\left( e^{\mathbf{A}t} \right)^{-1} = e^{-\mathbf{A}t}$$

<Callout type="warning" title="Non-Commutativity of Exponentials">

For two matrices $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$, $e^{(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B})t} = e^{\mathbf{A}t} e^{\mathbf{B}t}$ holds **if and only if** $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ commute: $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$. In general robotic kinematic formulations, matrix operators do not commute, requiring the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff series expansion.

</Callout>

## Analytical Evaluation Methods for $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$

In hand calculations and exam problems, evaluating the infinite series the equation above directly is impossible unless $\mathbf{A}$ is nilpotent ($\mathbf{A}^k = \mathbf{0}$). Three closed-form algebraic methods exist:

### Method 1: Resolvent Matrix Inverse Laplace Transform
Taking the Laplace transform of the homogeneous state equation $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t)$:
$$s \mathbf{X}(s) - \mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{X}(s) \implies (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) \mathbf{X}(s) = \mathbf{x}(0)$$
$$\mathbf{X}(s) = (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} \mathbf{x}(0)$$
Taking the inverse Laplace transform on both sides yields the foundational identity:

$$e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathcal{L}^{-1} \left\{ (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} \right\} = \mathcal{L}^{-1} \left\{ \frac{\operatorname{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})}{\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})} \right\}$$

This is the most systematic method for $2 \times 2$ and $3 \times 3$ matrices in written university examinations.

### Method 2: Spectral Eigenvalue Decomposition
If $\mathbf{A}$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors assembled into modal matrix $\mathbf{T} = [\mathbf{v}_1 \; \dots \; \mathbf{v}_n]$ such that $\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{\Lambda} = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$:
$$e^{\mathbf{A}t} = e^{\mathbf{T} \mathbf{\Lambda} \mathbf{T}^{-1} t} = \mathbf{T} e^{\mathbf{\Lambda}t} \mathbf{T}^{-1}$$
Since the exponential of a diagonal matrix is simply the exponential of its diagonal scalar elements:

$$e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathbf{T} \begin{bmatrix} e^{\lambda_1 t} & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & e^{\lambda_2 t} & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & e^{\lambda_n t} \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{T}^{-1} = \sum_{i=1}^n e^{\lambda_i t} \mathbf{v}_i \mathbf{w}_i^T$$

where $\mathbf{w}_i^T$ is the $i$-th row of $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ (the $i$-th left eigenvector).

### Method 3: The Cayley-Hamilton Polynomial Expansion
The **Cayley-Hamilton Theorem** states that every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation:
$$p(\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{A}^n + a_{n-1} \mathbf{A}^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 \mathbf{A} + a_0 \mathbf{I} = \mathbf{0}$$
Consequently, all powers $\mathbf{A}^k$ for $k \ge n$ can be written as linear combinations of $\{\mathbf{I}, \mathbf{A}, \mathbf{A}^2, \dots, \mathbf{A}^{n-1}\}$.
Thus, the infinite series $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ collapses into a finite polynomial in $\mathbf{A}$ of degree at most $n-1$:

$$e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \alpha_0(t) \mathbf{I} + \alpha_1(t) \mathbf{A} + \alpha_2(t) \mathbf{A}^2 + \dots + \alpha_{n-1}(t) \mathbf{A}^{n-1}$$

The scalar functions $\alpha_k(t)$ are determined by solving the scalar system obtained by substituting each eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ into the equation above:
$$e^{\lambda_i t} = \alpha_0(t) + \alpha_1(t) \lambda_i + \alpha_2(t) \lambda_i^2 + \dots + \alpha_{n-1}(t) \lambda_i^{n-1}, \quad i = 1, \dots, n$$
(For repeated eigenvalues of multiplicity $m$, derivatives $\frac{d^k}{d\lambda^k} e^{\lambda t}$ are taken).

<Callout type="bridge" title="SDU Robotics Bridge: Discretization in PX4 Flight Control Loops">

Physical microcontrollers in autonomous drones (e.g. STM32H7 on a Pixhawk flight controller) cannot execute continuous-time differential equations $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}$. Instead, the continuous state-space model is discretized at a fixed sample period $T_s$ (e.g., $T_s = 2.5\text{ ms}$ for a 400 Hz inner rate loop):
$$\mathbf{x}[k+1] = \mathbf{A}_d \mathbf{x}[k] + \mathbf{B}_d \mathbf{u}[k]$$
Using exact zero-order hold (ZOH) integration, the discrete matrices are given by:
$$\mathbf{A}_d = e^{\mathbf{A} T_s}, \quad \mathbf{B}_d = \left( \int_0^{T_s} e^{\mathbf{A}\tau} d\tau \right) \mathbf{B}$$
For very small sampling intervals $T_s$, microcontrollers approximate $\mathbf{A}_d \approx \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{A} T_s$ and $\mathbf{B}_d \approx \mathbf{B} T_s$. When executing high-speed aerobatic maneuvers, however, higher-order matrix exponential terms $\frac{1}{2} \mathbf{A}^2 T_s^2$ must be computed to prevent numerical divergence of the attitude estimator.

</Callout>

## Chapter Exercises

<Exercise client:load id="ex-8-1" title="Exercise 8.1: Upper-Triangular $2\times 2$ Matrix Exponential" level="LEVEL 1: WARM-UP DRILL">

Consider the upper-triangular matrix:
$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Formulate $s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}$ and determine $\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$ by inspection from the diagonal elements.
2. Compute the resolvent matrix $(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}$ using the $2\times 2$ adjugate matrix formula:
   $$(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})} \operatorname{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$$
3. Expand entry $(1,2)$ into partial fractions.
4. Apply the inverse Laplace transform to compute $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ and verify that $e^{\mathbf{A}\cdot 0} = \mathbf{I}$.

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. For upper-triangular matrix $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$:
   $$s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} s + 1 & -2 \\ 0 & s + 3 \end{bmatrix}$$
   The determinant of an upper-triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal elements:
   $$\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = (s + 1)(s + 3)$$
2. The adjugate matrix swaps diagonal elements and negates off-diagonal elements:
   $$\operatorname{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = \begin{bmatrix} s + 3 & 2 \\ 0 & s + 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
   The resolvent matrix is:
   $$(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} = \frac{1}{(s+1)(s+3)}\begin{bmatrix} s + 3 & 2 \\ 0 & s + 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{s+1} & \frac{2}{(s+1)(s+3)} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{s+3} \end{bmatrix}$$
3. Expanding entry $(1,2)$ via Heaviside partial fractions:
   $$\frac{2}{(s+1)(s+3)} = \frac{R_1}{s+1} + \frac{R_2}{s+3}$$
   $$R_1 = \left.\frac{2}{s+3}\right|_{s=-1} = \frac{2}{2} = 1, \quad R_2 = \left.\frac{2}{s+1}\right|_{s=-3} = \frac{2}{-2} = -1$$
   $$\frac{2}{(s+1)(s+3)} = \frac{1}{s+1} - \frac{1}{s+3}$$
4. Taking the inverse Laplace transform element-by-element:
   $$e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \begin{bmatrix} e^{-t} & e^{-t} - e^{-3t} \\ 0 & e^{-3t} \end{bmatrix}$$
   Evaluating at $t = 0$:
   $$e^{\mathbf{A}\cdot 0} = \begin{bmatrix} e^0 & e^0 - e^0 \\ 0 & e^0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{I}$$

</details>

</Exercise>

<Exercise client:load id="ex-8-2" title="Exercise 8.2: Coupled $2\times 2$ Companion Matrix Exponential" level="LEVEL 2: EXAM BENCHMARK">

Given the standard coupled companion state matrix:
$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -6 & -5 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Formulate the resolvent matrix $(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}$ using the adjugate method.
2. Perform partial fraction expansion on all four scalar rational entries of $(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}$.
3. Take the inverse Laplace transform to obtain the explicit analytical transition matrix $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$.
4. Verify that $e^{\mathbf{A}\cdot 0} = \mathbf{I}$.

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Setting up $(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$:
   $$s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} s & -1 \\ 6 & s + 5 \end{bmatrix}$$
   Determinant:
   $$\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = s(s + 5) - (-6) = s^2 + 5s + 6 = (s + 2)(s + 3)$$
   The adjugate matrix is:
   $$\operatorname{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = \begin{bmatrix} s + 5 & 1 \\ -6 & s \end{bmatrix}$$
   The resolvent matrix is:
   $$(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} = \frac{1}{(s+2)(s+3)}\begin{bmatrix} s + 5 & 1 \\ -6 & s \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{s+5}{(s+2)(s+3)} & \frac{1}{(s+2)(s+3)} \\ \frac{-6}{(s+2)(s+3)} & \frac{s}{(s+2)(s+3)} \end{bmatrix}$$
2. Decomposing each entry into partial fractions:
   * Entry $(1,1)$: $\frac{s+5}{(s+2)(s+3)} = \frac{3}{s+2} - \frac{2}{s+3}$
   * Entry $(1,2)$: $\frac{1}{(s+2)(s+3)} = \frac{1}{s+2} - \frac{1}{s+3}$
   * Entry $(2,1)$: $\frac{-6}{(s+2)(s+3)} = \frac{-6}{s+2} + \frac{6}{s+3}$
   * Entry $(2,2)$: $\frac{s}{(s+2)(s+3)} = \frac{-2}{s+2} + \frac{3}{s+3}$
3. Taking the inverse Laplace transform element-by-element:
   $$e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \begin{bmatrix} 3e^{-2t} - 2e^{-3t} & e^{-2t} - e^{-3t} \\ -6e^{-2t} + 6e^{-3t} & -2e^{-2t} + 3e^{-3t} \end{bmatrix}$$
4. Evaluating at $t = 0$:
   $$e^{\mathbf{A}\cdot 0} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 - 2 & 1 - 1 \\ -6 + 6 & -2 + 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{I}$$

</details>

</Exercise>
