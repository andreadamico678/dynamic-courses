## Introduction

Before predicting how a motor or aircraft responds to external pilot commands, we must understand what the system does on its own when all inputs are turned off ($\mathbf{u}(t) \equiv \mathbf{0}$) and it is released from an initial stored-energy state $\mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{x}_0$. That autonomous trajectory—the **free state response** $\mathbf{x}_l(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}_0$—reveals the plant's pure natural personality: whether initial energy bleeds away smoothly, rings in decaying oscillations, or diverges outward.

Think of a quadrotor knocked sideways by a brief wind gust or an RLC circuit left with initial capacitor charge: every resulting curve is a weighted mixture of the system's **natural modes**, dictated by its eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Understanding how initial states project onto eigenvectors prepares you directly for internal stability analysis in Part IV.

<Divider label="1 · Example" />

Consider the coupled second-order autonomous system initialized at $\mathbf{x}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ a \end{bmatrix}$ ($a \neq 0$):

$$\dot{x}_1(t) = x_2(t), \qquad \dot{x}_2(t) = -6 x_1(t) - 5 x_2(t)$$

1. **Dynamic Matrix & Eigenvalues:** Writing $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ gives:

   $$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -6 & -5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad p(\lambda) = \det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = \det\begin{bmatrix} \lambda & -1 \\ 6 & \lambda + 5 \end{bmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 5\lambda + 6 = (\lambda + 2)(\lambda + 3) = 0$$

   The eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = -2$ and $\lambda_2 = -3$. Because both are strictly negative, every initial state decays back to the origin.

2. **Eigenvectors & Modal Decomposition:** Solve $(\mathbf{A} - \lambda_i\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}$ for each eigenvalue:
   - For $\lambda_1 = -2$: $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -6 & -3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_{11} \\ v_{12} \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{0} \implies 2v_{11} + v_{12} = 0$, so $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$.
   - For $\lambda_2 = -3$: $\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ -6 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_{21} \\ v_{22} \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{0} \implies 3v_{21} + v_{22} = 0$, so $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$.

   Decomposing the initial vector $\mathbf{x}(0) = [a, \; a]^T$ in this eigenbasis gives $\mathbf{x}(0) = 4a\mathbf{v}_1 - 3a\mathbf{v}_2$ (check: $4a[1, -2]^T - 3a[1, -3]^T = [a, a]^T$).

3. **State Transition Matrix & Free Trajectory:** From [Matrix Exponential State Transition](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/03-time-response/01-matrix-exponential-state-transition/), the state transition matrix is:

   $$e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \begin{bmatrix} 3e^{-2t} - 2e^{-3t} & e^{-2t} - e^{-3t} \\ -6e^{-2t} + 6e^{-3t} & -2e^{-2t} + 3e^{-3t} \end{bmatrix}$$

   Multiplying $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ by $\mathbf{x}(0) = [a, \; a]^T$ adds the two columns scaled by $a$:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \mathbf{x}_l(t) &= e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}(0) = a \begin{bmatrix} (3e^{-2t} - 2e^{-3t}) + (e^{-2t} - e^{-3t}) \\ (-6e^{-2t} + 6e^{-3t}) + (-2e^{-2t} + 3e^{-3t}) \end{bmatrix} \\
   &= \begin{bmatrix} 4a e^{-2t} - 3a e^{-3t} \\ -8a e^{-2t} + 9a e^{-3t} \end{bmatrix} = 4a e^{-2t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} - 3a e^{-3t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Notice that the matrix multiplication produces the exact same modal decomposition $4a e^{-2t}\mathbf{v}_1 - 3a e^{-3t}\mathbf{v}_2$: the initial state splits into two independent exponential waves, one along $\mathbf{v}_1$ decaying at rate $e^{-2t}$ and one along $\mathbf{v}_2$ decaying faster at rate $e^{-3t}$.

4. **Boundary Check ($t = 0$):** Evaluating at $t = 0$ confirms $\mathbf{x}_l(0) = [4a - 3a, \; -8a + 9a]^T = [a, \; a]^T = \mathbf{x}(0)$. As $t$ grows, the faster mode $e^{-3t}$ dies out first, leaving $\mathbf{x}_l(t)$ tangent to the slower eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_1 = [1, \; -2]^T$ as it approaches the origin.

### Autonomous Dynamics and Modes

When a continuous-time linear system starts with initial stored energy $\mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{x}_0$ under zero external input ($\mathbf{u}(t) \equiv \mathbf{0}$ for all $t \ge 0$), the input term $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t)$ and feedthrough term $\mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}(t)$ vanish. From [Matrix Exponential State Transition](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/03-time-response/01-matrix-exponential-state-transition/), the **free state response** $\mathbf{x}_l(t)$ and **free output response** $\mathbf{y}_l(t)$ are:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}(t), \quad \mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{x}_0 \implies \mathbf{x}_l(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}_0, \qquad \mathbf{y}_l(t) = \mathbf{C} e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}_0
$$

Physically, $\mathbf{x}_l(t)$ tracks how the system's internal energy (stored in springs, masses, capacitors, or inductors) redistributes and dissipates on its own.

Because $e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\}$ and every entry of $(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}$ has denominator dividing $p(s) = \det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$, every component of $\mathbf{x}_l(t)$ is a linear combination of elementary waveforms called the **natural modes** of the system. By [Partial Fraction Expansion](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/02-partial-fraction-expansion-heaviside-residues/), each root of $p(\lambda) = 0$ generates one of three mode families:

1. **Real Simple Eigenvalue ($\lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}$):** A simple pole at $s = \lambda_i$ produces $\frac{1}{s - \lambda_i} \xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}^{-1}} e^{\lambda_i t}$. The mode decays monotonically to $0$ if $\lambda_i < 0$, stays constant if $\lambda_i = 0$, and diverges if $\lambda_i > 0$.
2. **Real Repeated Eigenvalue with Defect ($m_g < m_a$):** When a Jordan block of size $m \ge 2$ occurs (so the geometric multiplicity $m_g = n - \operatorname{rank}(\lambda_i\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$ is strictly less than the algebraic multiplicity $m_a$), $(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}$ contains higher-order poles $\frac{1}{(s - \lambda_i)^{k+1}}$ ($0 \le k \le m - 1$), generating secular polynomial-exponential modes $e^{\lambda_i t}, \; t e^{\lambda_i t}, \; \dots, \; t^{m-1} e^{\lambda_i t}$. Note that if $\mathbf{A}$ is diagonalizable ($m_g = m_a$), all higher-order poles cancel and no $t^k$ terms appear.
3. **Complex Conjugate Pair ($\lambda_{1,2} = \sigma \pm j\omega$):** Since $\mathbf{A}$ has real entries, complex eigenvalues always appear in conjugate pairs. By Euler's formula, $e^{(\sigma \pm j\omega)t} = e^{\sigma t}(\cos\omega t \pm j\sin\omega t)$, so combining conjugate residues yields real oscillatory modes $e^{\sigma t}\cos(\omega t)$ and $e^{\sigma t}\sin(\omega t)$—damped if $\sigma < 0$, sustained harmonic if $\sigma = 0$, and growing if $\sigma > 0$.

### Modal Projection

To see *why* $\mathbf{x}_l(t)$ behaves geometrically as a sum of straight-line rays in state space, suppose $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues $\{\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n\}$ with linearly independent right eigenvectors $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\}$. As in [Coordinate Basis Similarity Transformations](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/01-state-models/02-coordinate-basis-similarity-transformations/), any initial vector $\mathbf{x}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$ expands uniquely in the eigenbasis:

$$
\mathbf{x}_0 = c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_n \mathbf{v}_n = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \mathbf{v}_i
$$

where the modal coordinate vector $\mathbf{c} = [c_1, \dots, c_n]^T$ is given by $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_0$ for $\mathbf{T} = [\mathbf{v}_1 \; \dots \; \mathbf{v}_n]$.

Now examine how $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ acts on a single eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_i$. From $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{v}_i$, multiplying repeatedly by $\mathbf{A}$ gives $\mathbf{A}^2 \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{A}(\lambda_i \mathbf{v}_i) = \lambda_i^2 \mathbf{v}_i$, and by induction $\mathbf{A}^k \mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i^k \mathbf{v}_i$ for every $k \ge 0$. Substituting this into the matrix exponential power series yields:

$$
e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{v}_i = \left(\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{t^k}{k!} \mathbf{A}^k\right) \mathbf{v}_i = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{t^k}{k!} \left(\mathbf{A}^k \mathbf{v}_i\right) = \left(\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(\lambda_i t)^k}{k!}\right) \mathbf{v}_i = e^{\lambda_i t} \mathbf{v}_i
$$

Applying linearity of $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ to the expansion $\mathbf{x}_0 = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \mathbf{v}_i$ immediately decomposes the free response into $n$ independent modal waves:

$$
\mathbf{x}_l(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}_0 = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{v}_i = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i e^{\lambda_i t} \mathbf{v}_i, \qquad \mathbf{y}_l(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i e^{\lambda_i t} (\mathbf{C}\mathbf{v}_i)
$$

Each scalar $c_i$ is the initial excitation amplitude of mode $i$; if $\mathbf{x}_0$ happens to be orthogonal to the $i$-th left eigenvector $\mathbf{w}_i^T$, then $c_i = \mathbf{w}_i^T \mathbf{x}_0 = 0$ and mode $i$ remains completely silent.

### Invariance of Eigenspaces

Suppose we choose the initial state $\mathbf{x}_0$ to lie strictly along a single eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_k$, so that $\mathbf{x}_0 = c_k \mathbf{v}_k$ and $c_i = 0$ for all $i \neq k$. Because $e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{v}_k = e^{\lambda_k t}\mathbf{v}_k$, the entire trajectory collapses to:

$$
\mathbf{x}_l(t) = c_k e^{\lambda_k t} \mathbf{v}_k
$$

Geometrically, $\mathbf{x}_l(t)$ only changes length by the positive scalar factor $e^{\lambda_k t}$ and never leaves the one-dimensional ray $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_k\}$ for any $t \ge 0$. Thus, **eigenspaces are invariant** under $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$, and **only the single mode** $e^{\lambda_k t}$ is excited.

Conversely, for a generic off-axis initial state where $c_1, \dots, c_n$ are all nonzero and the eigenvalues are real and ordered $0 > \lambda_1 > \lambda_2 > \dots > \lambda_n$, factoring out the slowest exponential $e^{\lambda_1 t}$ gives:

$$
\mathbf{x}_l(t) = e^{\lambda_1 t}\left(c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + \sum_{i=2}^n c_i e^{(\lambda_i - \lambda_1)t}\mathbf{v}_i\right)
$$

Because $\lambda_i - \lambda_1 < 0$ for all $i \ge 2$, every fast term $e^{(\lambda_i - \lambda_1)t}$ vanishes relative to $c_1 \mathbf{v}_1$ as $t \to \infty$. That is why off-axis trajectories in a stable node always bend away from the fast eigenvector and enter the origin tangent to the slowest (dominant) eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_1$.

<Divider label="3 · Summary" />

| Eigenvalue Type | Natural Modes | Decay / Growth Condition |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Real simple** $\lambda_i$ | $e^{\lambda_i t}$ | Decays if $\lambda_i < 0$; grows if $\lambda_i > 0$ |
| **Defective** ($m_g < m_a$) | $e^{\lambda_i t}, \; t e^{\lambda_i t}, \; \dots, \; t^{m-1} e^{\lambda_i t}$ | Decays if $\lambda_i < 0$; grows if $\lambda_i \ge 0$ |
| **Complex pair** $\sigma \pm j\omega$ | $e^{\sigma t}\cos(\omega t), \; e^{\sigma t}\sin(\omega t)$ | Damped if $\sigma < 0$; sustained if $\sigma = 0$; grows if $\sigma > 0$ |

### Evaluation Procedure

1. Assemble $\mathbf{A}$ and solve $\det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = 0$ to identify all natural modes.
2. Compute $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ via $\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\}$ or spectral modal decomposition $\sum c_i e^{\lambda_i t}\mathbf{v}_i$.
3. Multiply $\mathbf{x}_l(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}(0)$ and compute output $\mathbf{y}_l(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}_l(t)$ if required.
4. Check the boundary condition $\mathbf{x}_l(0) = \mathbf{x}(0)$ to catch sign or partial-fraction errors.

### Key Proofs

- **Eigenvector Propagation:** Expanding $e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{v}_i = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{t^k}{k!}\mathbf{A}^k\mathbf{v}_i = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(\lambda_i t)^k}{k!}\mathbf{v}_i = e^{\lambda_i t}\mathbf{v}_i$ proves that each eigenvector stays invariant and scales purely by $e^{\lambda_i t}$.
- **Modal Decomposition:** Expanding $\mathbf{x}_0$ in the eigenbasis $\mathbf{T} = [\mathbf{v}_1 \; \dots \; \mathbf{v}_n]$ yields $\mathbf{x}_l(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i e^{\lambda_i t}\mathbf{v}_i$.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistakes">

1. **Skipping the $t = 0$ Check:** Always plug $t = 0$ into your final $\mathbf{x}_l(t)$ to confirm it equals $\mathbf{x}(0)$.
2. **Slow vs. Fast Alignment:** Off-axis trajectories approach the origin tangent to the **slowest** decaying eigenvector (smallest $|\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)|$), not the fastest.
3. **Secular Terms on Repeated Roots:** Repeated eigenvalues produce $t^k e^{\lambda t}$ modes only when a Jordan block has size $\ge 2$ ($m_g < m_a$), not when $\mathbf{A}$ is diagonalizable.

</Callout>

Consider the continuous-time system:

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ -5/2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{x}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Determine system eigenvalues $\det(\lambda\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = 0$ and classify internal stability.
2. Compute the state resolvent matrix $(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}$ and its inverse Laplace transform $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$.
3. Calculate the analytical free state trajectory $\mathbf{x}_l(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}(0)$.
4. Compute the corresponding free output trajectory $y_l(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}_l(t)$.

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Characteristic polynomial:
   $$p(\lambda) = \det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = \det\begin{bmatrix} \lambda & -2 \\ 5/2 & \lambda + 2 \end{bmatrix} = \lambda(\lambda + 2) + 5 = \lambda^2 + 2\lambda + 5 = 0$$
   Eigenvalues: $\lambda_{1,2} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 20}}{2} = -1 \pm j2$.
   Since $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{1,2}) = -1 < 0$, the system is **asymptotically stable** with underdamped oscillatory modes.
2. Resolvent matrix:
   $$(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} = \frac{1}{s^2 + 2s + 5}\begin{bmatrix} s + 2 & 2 \\ -5/2 & s \end{bmatrix}$$
   Completing the square on the denominator gives $(s+1)^2 + 2^2$. Taking inverse Laplace transforms entry by entry:
   - Entry $(1,1)$: $\frac{s+2}{(s+1)^2 + 4} = \frac{s+1}{(s+1)^2 + 4} + \frac{1}{2}\frac{2}{(s+1)^2 + 4} \xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}^{-1}} e^{-t}\cos(2t) + \frac{1}{2}e^{-t}\sin(2t)$
   - Entry $(1,2)$: $\frac{2}{(s+1)^2 + 4} \xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}^{-1}} e^{-t}\sin(2t)$
   - Entry $(2,1)$: $\frac{-5/2}{(s+1)^2 + 4} = -\frac{5}{4}\frac{2}{(s+1)^2 + 4} \xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}^{-1}} -\frac{5}{4}e^{-t}\sin(2t)$
   - Entry $(2,2)$: $\frac{s}{(s+1)^2 + 4} = \frac{s+1}{(s+1)^2 + 4} - \frac{1}{2}\frac{2}{(s+1)^2 + 4} \xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}^{-1}} e^{-t}\cos(2t) - \frac{1}{2}e^{-t}\sin(2t)$
   $$e^{\mathbf{A}t} = e^{-t}\begin{bmatrix} \cos(2t) + \frac{1}{2}\sin(2t) & \sin(2t) \\ -\frac{5}{4}\sin(2t) & \cos(2t) - \frac{1}{2}\sin(2t) \end{bmatrix}$$
3. Free state trajectory with $\mathbf{x}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$:
   $$\mathbf{x}_l(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = e^{-t}\begin{bmatrix} \left(\cos(2t) + \frac{1}{2}\sin(2t)\right) + \sin(2t) \\ -\frac{5}{4}\sin(2t) + \left(\cos(2t) - \frac{1}{2}\sin(2t)\right) \end{bmatrix} = e^{-t}\begin{bmatrix} \cos(2t) + \frac{3}{2}\sin(2t) \\ \cos(2t) - \frac{7}{4}\sin(2t) \end{bmatrix}$$

4. Free output response for $\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$:
   $$y_l(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}_l(t) = 2 x_{l1}(t) = e^{-t}\left( 2\cos(2t) + 3\sin(2t) \right)$$

<Exercise client:load id="ex-9-3" title="Exercise 9.3 — Modal Projection and Suppression" level="Exam Standard">

Consider the upper-triangular system:

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{x}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Find the eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ and corresponding right eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2$ of $\mathbf{A}$.
2. Form the modal matrix $\mathbf{T} = [\mathbf{v}_1 \; \mathbf{v}_2]$, compute $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$, and express $\mathbf{x}(0) = c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{v}_2$.
3. Write the free state response $\mathbf{x}_l(t)$ and free output response $y_l(t)$ directly using modal projection.
4. State a non-zero initial state $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}(0)$ for which the slow mode $e^{-t}$ is completely absent from $\mathbf{x}_l(t)$.

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Reveal Worked Solution</summary>

1. Because $\mathbf{A}$ is upper-triangular, its eigenvalues are the diagonal entries $\lambda_1 = -1$ and $\lambda_2 = -4$.
   - For $\lambda_1 = -1$: $(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{0} \implies v_{12} = 0$, so $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
   - For $\lambda_2 = -4$: $(\mathbf{A} + 4\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{0} \implies 3v_{21} + v_{22} = 0$, so $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$.
2. Modal matrix and its inverse:
   $$\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{T}^{-1} = \frac{1}{-3}\begin{bmatrix} -3 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1/3 \\ 0 & -1/3 \end{bmatrix}$$
   Computing modal coordinates $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{x}(0)$:
   $$\mathbf{c} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1/3 \\ 0 & -1/3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$$
   So $\mathbf{x}(0) = 3\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2$.
3. Applying $\mathbf{x}_l(t) = c_1 e^{\lambda_1 t}\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t}\mathbf{v}_2$:
   $$\mathbf{x}_l(t) = 3 e^{-t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - e^{-4t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3e^{-t} - e^{-4t} \\ 3e^{-4t} \end{bmatrix}$$
   Boundary check: $\mathbf{x}_l(0) = [3 - 1, \; 3]^T = [2, \; 3]^T = \mathbf{x}(0)$. $\checkmark$
   Free output:
   $$y_l(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}_l(t) = (3e^{-t} - e^{-4t}) + 3e^{-4t} = 3e^{-t} + 2e^{-4t}$$
4. To suppress the slow mode $e^{-t}$ ($c_1 = 0$), initialize the state strictly along the fast eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_2$: any $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}(0) = \alpha \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$ ($\alpha \neq 0$) gives $\mathbf{x}_l(t) = \alpha e^{-4t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$.
