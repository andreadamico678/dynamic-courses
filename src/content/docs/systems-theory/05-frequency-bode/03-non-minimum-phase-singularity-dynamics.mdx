## Introduction

In [Chapter 17](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/05-frequency-bode/01-asymptotic-bode-magnitude-diagrams/) and [Chapter 18](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/05-frequency-bode/02-exact-bode-phase-diagrams/), you learned to read gain crossover $\omega_{gc}$ and **phase margin** $\varphi_m$ directly from open-loop Bode diagrams. A natural question follows: if you already know the exact magnitude curve $|L(j\omega)|$ across all frequencies, do you automatically know how the system will behave when you close a feedback loop around it?

For most standard plants, the answer is yes—Hendrik Bode proved that when all poles and zeros lie in the left half-plane and no time delay exists, the slope of the magnitude curve uniquely fixes the phase curve. However, as soon as a plant contains a **right-half-plane (RHP) zero** or a **pure transport delay** $e^{-sT_d}$, that coupling breaks down completely. Two open-loop systems can trace the **exact same Bode magnitude diagram** at every frequency, yet when placed inside the same unity-feedback loop, one settles smoothly while the other explodes into growing oscillations.

### Mirror Zeros in a Feedback Loop

To see why identical magnitude curves can produce opposite closed-loop stability, place a plant $G(s)$ driven by a proportional controller $C(s) = K = 6$ inside the standard unity negative-feedback loop from [Chapter 3](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/01-state-models/03-block-diagram-algebra-reduction/) and [Chapter 18](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/05-frequency-bode/02-exact-bode-phase-diagrams/):

  <figcaption><strong>Loop Signals:</strong> Bode diagrams plot the open-loop chain $L(s) = C(s)G(s)$ before closing feedback; closed-loop stability belongs to $W_{\text{CL}}(s) = L(s)/(1 + L(s))$.</figcaption>

Recall the fundamental distinction between open-loop and closed-loop transfer functions:
- The **open-loop transfer function** $L(s) = C(s)G(s)$ is the forward product you plot on a Bode diagram.
- The **closed-loop transfer function** $W_{\text{CL}}(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{R(s)} = \frac{L(s)}{1 + L(s)}$ governs the actual feedback loop. Its poles are the roots of $1 + L(s) = 0$.

Now compare two open-loop candidates that share the exact same gain $K = 6$, the same integrator $1/s$, the same real pole at $\omega_p = 2\text{ rad/s}$ ($s = -2$), and the same zero corner frequency $\omega_z = 4\text{ rad/s}$, differing **only by the algebraic sign** in the numerator:

$$L_{\text{MP}}(s) = K \frac{1 + s/4}{s(1 + s/2)}, \qquad L_{\text{NMP}}(s) = K \frac{1 - s/4}{s(1 + s/2)}$$

In $L_{\text{MP}}(s)$, setting $1 + s/4 = 0$ places the zero at $s = -4$ in the open left half-plane (LHP). In $L_{\text{NMP}}(s)$, setting $1 - s/4 = 0$ places the zero at $s = +4$ in the open right half-plane (RHP), which is the mirror image of $s = -4$ across the imaginary axis.

Let us compute the sinusoidal magnitude $|L(j\omega)|$ of both open-loop systems by substituting $s = j\omega$. For any real frequency $\omega$, the numerator complex numbers $1 + j(\omega/4)$ and $1 - j(\omega/4)$ are complex conjugates of each other. Because a complex number and its conjugate have identical Euclidean length,

$$|1 + j\omega/4| = \sqrt{1^2 + (\omega/4)^2} = \sqrt{1 + (\omega/4)^2}, \qquad |1 - j\omega/4| = \sqrt{1^2 + (-\omega/4)^2} = \sqrt{1 + (\omega/4)^2}$$

Dividing by the denominator modulus $|j\omega|\cdot|1 + j\omega/2| = \omega\sqrt{1 + (\omega/2)^2}$ shows that both loops share the **exact same magnitude curve** at every frequency:

$$|L_{\text{MP}}(j\omega)| = |L_{\text{NMP}}(j\omega)| = \frac{K}{\omega}\sqrt{\frac{1 + (\omega/4)^2}{1 + (\omega/2)^2}}$$

Because their magnitude curves are identical, both systems cross $0\text{ dB}$ ($|L(j\omega_{gc})| = 1$) at the exact same **gain crossover frequency** $\omega_{gc}$. Let us solve for $\omega_{gc}$ explicitly when $K = 6$ by squaring both sides of $|L(j\omega_{gc})| = 1$:

$$\frac{36\left(1 + \frac{\omega_{gc}^2}{16}\right)}{\omega_{gc}^2\left(1 + \frac{\omega_{gc}^2}{4}\right)} = 1 \implies 36 + \frac{36}{16}\omega_{gc}^2 = \omega_{gc}^2 + \frac{1}{4}\omega_{gc}^4$$

Simplifying $\frac{36}{16} = 2.25$ and rearranging all terms onto one side yields a quadratic equation in $u = \omega_{gc}^2$:

$$0.25\,u^2 - 1.25\,u - 36 = 0 \implies u^2 - 5u - 144 = 0$$

Applying the quadratic formula and keeping the positive root $u > 0$ gives:

$$u = \omega_{gc}^2 = \frac{5 + \sqrt{25 - 4(1)(-144)}}{2} = \frac{5 + \sqrt{601}}{2} \approx \frac{5 + 24.515}{2} \approx 14.758 \implies \omega_{gc} = \sqrt{14.758} \approx 3.84\text{ rad/s}$$

Both loops have $\omega_{gc} \approx 3.84\text{ rad/s}$. Now evaluate the open-loop phase $\angle L(j\omega)$ at $\omega_{gc} = 3.84\text{ rad/s}$. The integrator $1/(j\omega)$ contributes $-90^\circ$, and the denominator pole $(1 + j\,3.84/2)$ subtracts $\arctan(1.92) \approx 62.5^\circ$ in both systems. However, look at the numerator zero factor:
- In **$L_{\text{MP}}(j\,3.84)$**, $1 + j(3.84/4) = 1 + j\,0.96$ lies in the **first quadrant** ($\operatorname{Re} > 0, \operatorname{Im} > 0$), so its angle is **positive** (**phase lead**):
  $$\arg(1 + j\,0.96) = +\arctan(0.96) \approx +43.8^\circ$$
  $$\angle L_{\text{MP}}(j\,3.84) = -90^\circ - 62.5^\circ + 43.8^\circ = -108.7^\circ \implies \varphi_m = 180^\circ - 108.7^\circ = +71.3^\circ > 0^\circ$$
  With $+71.3^\circ$ of phase margin, the closed-loop system $W_{\text{CL,MP}}(s)$ is well damped and asymptotically stable.
- In **$L_{\text{NMP}}(j\,3.84)$**, $1 - j(3.84/4) = 1 - j\,0.96$ lies in the **fourth quadrant** ($\operatorname{Re} > 0, \operatorname{Im} < 0$), so its angle is **negative** (**phase lag**):
  $$\arg(1 - j\,0.96) = -\arctan(0.96) \approx -43.8^\circ$$
  $$\angle L_{\text{NMP}}(j\,3.84) = -90^\circ - 62.5^\circ - 43.8^\circ = -196.3^\circ \implies \varphi_m = 180^\circ - 196.3^\circ = -16.3^\circ < 0^\circ$$
  Because the RHP zero pulled phase *down* by $-43.8^\circ$ instead of lifting it by $+43.8^\circ$, total open-loop phase at crossover has already dropped past $-180^\circ$, making $\varphi_m$ negative and driving the closed-loop system unstable.

We can verify this frequency-domain diagnosis directly in the $s$-domain using the **Routh-Hurwitz criterion** from [Chapter 15](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/04-stability-routh/05-parametric-feedback-gain-stability-bounds/). Forming the closed-loop characteristic equation $1 + L(s) = 0$ by clearing the denominator $s(1 + 0.5s)$:
- For the **LHP zero ($L_{\text{MP}}$)**:
  $$s(1 + 0.5s) + K(1 + 0.25s) = 0 \implies 0.5s^2 + (1 + 0.25K)s + K = 0$$
  For every positive gain $K > 0$, all three coefficients are strictly positive, so the closed-loop poles remain in the LHP for all $K > 0$.
- For the **RHP zero ($L_{\text{NMP}}$)**:
  $$s(1 + 0.5s) + K(1 - 0.25s) = 0 \implies 0.5s^2 + (1 - 0.25K)s + K = 0$$
  Here the $-0.25Ks$ term from the RHP zero directly fights the natural damping $+1s$. As soon as $K > 4$, the $s^1$ coefficient $1 - 0.25K$ becomes negative (at $K = 6$, we get $0.5s^2 - 0.5s + 6 = 0$, with RHP roots $s = 0.5 \pm j\sqrt{11.75}$).

### Minimum vs. Non-Minimum Phase

Why do we call well-behaved systems *minimum phase*? Suppose an engineer specifies only the gain profile $|L(j\omega)|$ across all frequencies $0 \le \omega < \infty$. Because replacing any factor $(1 + s/\omega_0)$ by $(1 - s/\omega_0)$ or multiplying by $e^{-sT_d}$ leaves $|L(j\omega)|$ unchanged, there are infinitely many different transfer functions that share the exact same Bode magnitude plot.

Among all causal transfer functions sharing a given magnitude curve $|L(j\omega)|$, there is one unique transfer function whose total phase lag $-\angle L(j\omega)$ is as small as physically possible across frequency: the one that has **no RHP zeros, no RHP poles, and no time delay**. We formalize this classification as follows:
- An open-loop transfer function $L(s)$ is **minimum phase** if all of its poles lie in the closed left half-plane (allowing integrators at $s = 0$), all of its finite zeros lie strictly in the open left half-plane ($\operatorname{Re}(z_i) < 0$), and its transport delay is zero ($T_d = 0$).
- If $L(s)$ contains at least one **RHP zero** ($\operatorname{Re}(z_i) > 0$), one **RHP pole**, or a **pure time delay** $e^{-sT_d}$ ($T_d > 0$), it is **non-minimum phase (NMP)**.

Bode's **Gain-Phase Integral Theorem** proves that for any minimum phase system, the phase $\angle L(j\omega_0)$ at frequency $\omega_0$ is uniquely determined by the weighted integral of the logarithmic magnitude slope $\frac{d|L(j\omega)|_{\text{dB}}}{d(\log_{10}\omega)}$ across all frequencies (roughly $-90^\circ$ of phase for every $-20\text{ dB/dec}$ of local slope). For non-minimum phase systems, that relationship fails: actual phase equals the minimum-phase baseline **minus additional phase lag** contributed by every RHP singularity and delay.

### All-Pass Factorization

To quantify the exact phase penalty caused by an RHP zero at $s = +\omega_z$ ($\omega_z > 0$), we can multiply and divide $L_{\text{NMP}}(s)$ by the mirrored LHP zero factor $(1 + s/\omega_z)$. This splits any RHP-zero plant into a cascade of two blocks:

$$L_{\text{NMP}}(s) = \underbrace{\left[L_{\text{NMP}}(s)\frac{1 + s/\omega_z}{1 - s/\omega_z}\right]}_{L_{\text{MP}}(s)} \cdot \underbrace{\left(\frac{1 - s/\omega_z}{1 + s/\omega_z}\right)}_{A(s)} = L_{\text{MP}}(s) \cdot A(s)$$

Here $L_{\text{MP}}(s)$ is the minimum-phase twin (where the zero at $s = +\omega_z$ has been reflected to $s = -\omega_z$ in the LHP), and $A(s) = \frac{1 - s/\omega_z}{1 + s/\omega_z}$ contains a pole at $s = -\omega_z$ and a mirror zero at $s = +\omega_z$.

Let us evaluate the magnitude and phase of $A(s)$ along the imaginary axis $s = j\omega$:

$$|A(j\omega)| = \frac{|1 - j(\omega/\omega_z)|}{|1 + j(\omega/\omega_z)|} = \frac{\sqrt{1 + (\omega/\omega_z)^2}}{\sqrt{1 + (\omega/\omega_z)^2}} = 1 \quad \forall \omega \ge 0$$

$$\arg A(j\omega) = \arg\!\left(1 - j\frac{\omega}{\omega_z}\right) - \arg\!\left(1 + j\frac{\omega}{\omega_z}\right) = -\arctan\!\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_z}\right) - \arctan\!\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_z}\right) = -2\arctan\!\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_z}\right)$$

Because $|A(j\omega)| = 1$ ($0\text{ dB}$) at every frequency, $A(s)$ is called an **all-pass filter**: it passes all sinusoidal amplitudes without attenuation or amplification. In phase, however, $\arg A(j\omega) = -2\arctan(\omega/\omega_z)$ starts at $0^\circ$ for $\omega \ll \omega_z$, drops through $-90^\circ$ at $\omega = \omega_z$, and reaches $-180^\circ$ for $\omega \gg \omega_z$. In other words, flipping a single zero from $s = -\omega_z$ to $s = +\omega_z$ injects up to $180^\circ$ of extra phase lag while leaving the Bode magnitude diagram completely unchanged.

On a hand-drawn Bode diagram, how do we sketch a single RHP zero factor $(1 - s/\omega_z)$ directly without factoring out $A(s)$?
- **Magnitude:** $(1 - s/\omega_z)$ behaves **identically** to an LHP zero $(1 + s/\omega_z)$: its asymptotic slope breaks **upward by $+20\text{ dB/dec}$** at $\omega = \omega_z$, and the exact curve sits $+3.01\text{ dB}$ above the corner vertex.
- **Phase:** Because $\arg(1 - j\omega/\omega_z) = -\arctan(\omega/\omega_z)$, an RHP zero behaves **like a pole in phase**: its 3-segment asymptotic ramp breaks **downward at $-45^\circ/\text{dec}$** across $[0.1\omega_z, 10\omega_z]$, reaching $-45^\circ$ at $\omega_z$ and $-90^\circ$ for $\omega \ge 10\omega_z$.

### Initial Undershoot

How does a right-half-plane zero manifest in the time domain when you apply a unit step command? In physical hardware, RHP zeros arise whenever two competing pathways connect input to output with opposite signs and different speeds—a fast direct path with the *wrong* sign and a slower dominant path with the *correct* steady-state sign:
- **Bicycle / Motorcycle Steering:** To turn right, a rider must first briefly steer the front wheel *left* (counter-steering) so the tire tracks out to the left and gravity leans the frame to the right.
- **Aircraft Altitude Control:** Pulling back the elevator of a tail-controlled aircraft pushes the tail downward to pitch the nose up; before the wings build additional lift, that downward tail force momentarily pushes the center of mass *downward*.
- **Hydroelectric & Boiler Drums:** Opening a steam valve drops drum pressure immediately, causing steam bubbles to expand and water level to swell upward before the depleted mass lowers the level.

We can prove mathematically that any stable system with a single real RHP zero exhibits this initial reverse motion—called **initial undershoot**—using the **Final Value Theorem (FVT)** and **Initial Value Theorem (IVT)** from [Chapter 4](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/01-unilateral-laplace-transformation-primitives/). Consider a stable, strictly proper second-order transfer function with unit DC gain ($W(0) = 1$), two LHP poles at $-p_1, -p_2 < 0$ ($p_1, p_2 > 0$), and one RHP zero at $s = +z_0$ ($z_0 > 0$):

$$W(s) = \frac{1 - s/z_0}{(1 + s/p_1)(1 + s/p_2)}$$

When excited by a unit step input $u(t) = \mathbb{1}(t)$ ($U(s) = 1/s$), the Laplace output is $Y(s) = W(s)/s$. Let us inspect the trajectory $y(t)$ at $t \to \infty$ and at $t = 0^+$:

1. **Steady-State Value ($t \to \infty$):** Since all poles of $sY(s) = W(s)$ lie strictly in the LHP ($-p_1, -p_2 < 0$), the Final Value Theorem applies:
   $$y(\infty) = \lim_{s \to 0} s Y(s) = \lim_{s \to 0} W(s) = W(0) = +1$$
   The output eventually settles to $+1$.
2. **Initial Value ($t = 0^+$):** Applying the Initial Value Theorem to $Y(s)$ gives:
   $$y(0^+) = \lim_{s \to \infty} s Y(s) = \lim_{s \to \infty} \frac{1 - s/z_0}{(1 + s/p_1)(1 + s/p_2)} = 0$$
   Because the denominator has degree $2$ and the numerator has degree $1$, the output starts continuously at $y(0^+) = 0$.
3. **Initial Velocity $\dot{y}(0^+)$:** Recall from [Chapter 4](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/01-unilateral-laplace-transformation-primitives/) that the Laplace transform of the derivative $\dot{y}(t)$ is $\mathcal{L}\{\dot{y}(t)\} = s Y(s) - y(0^+) = W(s) - 0 = W(s)$. Applying the Initial Value Theorem to $\mathcal{L}\{\dot{y}(t)\}$ yields the initial slope at $t = 0^+$:
   $$\dot{y}(0^+) = \lim_{s \to \infty} s\,\mathcal{L}\{\dot{y}(t)\} = \lim_{s \to \infty} s\,W(s) = \lim_{s \to \infty} \frac{s(1 - s/z_0)}{(1 + s/p_1)(1 + s/p_2)}$$
   At high $s \to \infty$, keeping the leading $s^2$ terms in numerator and denominator gives:
   $$\dot{y}(0^+) = \lim_{s \to \infty} \frac{-s^2 / z_0}{s^2 / (p_1 p_2)} = -\frac{p_1 p_2}{z_0} < 0$$

Because $p_1, p_2, z_0 > 0$, the initial slope $\dot{y}(0^+)$ is strictly **negative**. Starting from $y(0^+) = 0$ with negative velocity $\dot{y}(0^+) < 0$, the step response is forced to dip below zero ($y(t) < 0$) before reversing direction and climbing to $y(\infty) = +1$. Moreover, notice the denominator $z_0$ in $-\frac{p_1 p_2}{z_0}$: the closer the RHP zero $z_0$ sits to the origin (small $z_0$), the larger the ratio $p_1 p_2 / z_0$ becomes and the deeper the initial negative undershoot grows.

### Why You Cannot Cancel an RHP Zero

Seeing an undesirable RHP zero $(1 - s/z_0)$ in the plant $G(s)$, beginners often propose cancelling it algebraically inside the controller by placing an unstable pole at $s = +z_0$:

$$C(s) = \frac{C_0(s)}{1 - s/z_0}$$

In the paper product $L(s) = C(s)G(s)$, the $(1 - s/z_0)$ factors appear to cancel out. Why is this strictly forbidden in real engineering systems?
1. **Loss of Internal Stability:** In a feedback loop, input-output stability of $W_{\text{CL}}(s) = \frac{C(s)G(s)}{1 + C(s)G(s)}$ is not enough; every internal signal must remain bounded under sensor noise and load disturbances. If a load disturbance $d(t)$ enters at the plant input (between $C(s)$ and $G(s)$), the transfer function from $d(t)$ to the controller state or plant output contains the uncanceled unstable pole $(1 - s/z_0)$ in its denominator. As proved in [Chapter 16](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/04-stability-routh/06-bounded-input-bounded-output-stability/) and [Chapter 23](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/06-kalman-structure/04-uncontrollable-mode-stabilizability-verification/), cancelling an RHP singularity creates a hidden unstable mode $e^{+z_0 t}$ that saturates the actuator and destroys the hardware.
2. **Hard Bandwidth Limit ($\omega_{gc} \lesssim z_0 / 2$):** Because an RHP zero at $s = +z_0$ cannot be cancelled, it remains in $L(s)$ and in $W_{\text{CL}}(s)$. By $\omega = z_0$, the factor $(1 - j\omega/z_0)$ already subtracts $45^\circ$ of phase margin (and $90^\circ$ by $\omega \gg z_0$). If you try to push closed-loop bandwidth $\omega_{gc}$ past $z_0$, the RHP zero strips away your phase margin and forces closed-loop poles into the RHP. In practice, an RHP zero at $s = +z_0$ imposes an inescapable physical speed limit: the gain crossover frequency must be kept below roughly $\omega_{gc} \le z_0 / 2$.

### Pure Time Delay

A second major source of non-minimum phase behavior is a **pure transport delay** (dead time) $T_d > 0$: fluid traveling down a pipe of length $L$ at speed $v$ ($T_d = L/v$), material riding a conveyor belt, or digital camera / network packet latency in a robotic control loop. If a delay element shifts a time signal by $y(t) = u(t - T_d)\,\mathbb{1}(t - T_d)$, the Laplace time-shift theorem from [Chapter 4](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/01-unilateral-laplace-transformation-primitives/) gives the irrational transfer function:

$$W_d(s) = e^{-sT_d}$$

Substituting $s = j\omega$ and applying Euler's identity $e^{-j\omega T_d} = \cos(\omega T_d) - j\sin(\omega T_d)$ gives its exact magnitude and phase at frequency $\omega$:

$$|e^{-j\omega T_d}| = \sqrt{\cos^2(\omega T_d) + \sin^2(\omega T_d)} = 1 \implies |e^{-j\omega T_d}|_{\text{dB}} = 20\log_{10}(1) = 0\text{ dB}$$

$$\arg(e^{-j\omega T_d}) = -\omega T_d\;\text{rad} = -\omega T_d \cdot \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx -57.3^\circ\,(\omega T_d)$$

Look closely at what these two equations say:
1. **Zero Magnitude Effect:** Pure delay never attenuates or amplifies any frequency ($0\text{ dB}$ everywhere). Placing a transport delay in series with an open-loop plant $L(s) = L_0(s)e^{-sT_d}$ leaves $|L(j\omega)|$ and the gain crossover frequency $\omega_{gc}$ **completely unchanged**.
2. **Unbounded Linear Phase Drop:** Unlike a rational pole or RHP zero—whose phase lag levels off at $-90^\circ$—the phase lag of a pure delay grows **linearly with $\omega$ without bound** ($\to -\infty$ as $\omega \to \infty$). On a semi-logarithmic Bode plot (where the horizontal axis is $x = \log_{10}\omega$, so $\omega = 10^x$), the curve $-\omega T_d \frac{180^\circ}{\pi}$ plunges downward exponentially fast as you move to higher decades.

To see the link between pure delay and RHP zeros, approximate $e^{-sT_d}$ at low frequencies ($\omega T_d \ll 1$) using its first-order **Padé rational approximation**:

$$e^{-sT_d} = \frac{e^{-sT_d / 2}}{e^{+sT_d / 2}} \approx \frac{1 - s(T_d / 2)}{1 + s(T_d / 2)}$$

The first-order Padé approximation is literally an **all-pass filter** $A(s)$ with an LHP pole at $s = -2/T_d$ and an RHP zero at $s = +2/T_d$! Consequently, a delay $T_d$ imposes the same kind of hard closed-loop speed ceiling as an RHP zero: at $\omega = 1/T_d$, delay alone consumes $1\text{ rad} \approx 57.3^\circ$ of phase margin, so practical feedback loops must keep $\omega_{gc} \lesssim 1/T_d$.

### Exam Procedure

1. **Keep Signs Explicit in Canonical Form:** Factor $L(s)$ into time-constant form keeping every $(1 - s/\omega_z)$ intact—never pull a minus sign out of $(1 - s/\omega_z)$ into $K_B$.
2. **Draft Magnitude Normally:** Sketch $|L(j\omega)|$ using unsigned corner frequencies ($\omega_z, \omega_p$); an RHP zero still bends slope upward by $+20\text{ dB/dec}$, and $e^{-sT_d}$ adds $0\text{ dB}$.
3. **Subtract Non-Minimum Phase Lag:** For each RHP zero $(1 - s/\omega_z)$, draw a downward $-45^\circ/\text{dec}$ phase ramp across $[0.1\omega_z, 10\omega_z]$; for any delay $e^{-sT_d}$, subtract $\omega T_d (180^\circ/\pi)$ in degrees before computing $\varphi_m = 180^\circ + \angle L(j\omega_{gc})$.
4. **Respect Internal Stability:** Never cancel an RHP zero $(1 - s/z_0)$ with an unstable controller pole $1/(1 - s/z_0)$.

### Key Proofs

- **All-pass decomposition:** Factor $L_{\text{NMP}}(s) = L_{\text{MP}}(s)\frac{1 - s/\omega_z}{1 + s/\omega_z}$ and prove $|A(j\omega)| = 1$ with $\arg A(j\omega) = -2\arctan(\omega/\omega_z)$.
- **Undershoot via IVT:** For $W(s) = \frac{1 - s/z_0}{(1 + s/p_1)(1 + s/p_2)}$ under a unit step $Y(s) = W(s)/s$, show $y(\infty) = W(0) = +1$, $y(0^+) = 0$, and $\dot{y}(0^+) = \lim_{s\to\infty} s\,W(s) = -p_1 p_2 / z_0 < 0$.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistakes">
1. **Cancelling RHP Zeros:** Putting $(1 - s/z_0)$ in the denominator of $C(s)$ hides an unstable pole $s = +z_0$ inside the loop, causing internal actuator signals to diverge under disturbances.
2. **Radians vs. Degrees in Delay Phase:** In $-\omega T_d$, the angle is in **radians**; always multiply by $180^\circ/\pi$ before adding to Bode phase angles in degrees.
3. **Slope-to-Phase Shortcuts:** Reading phase from magnitude slope works **only** for minimum phase systems.
</Callout>

<Exercise client:load id="ex-19-1" title="Exercise 19.1 — Transport Delay Phase Margin" level="Exam Standard">
A robotic servo loop has open-loop transfer function with latency $T_d = 0.05\text{ s}$:

$$L(s) = \frac{15\, e^{-0.05 s}}{s(1 + s/16)}$$

Find the exact gain crossover frequency $\omega_{gc}$, the phase margin $\varphi_m$, and the maximum tolerable delay $T_{d,\max}$ before closed-loop instability.

<StepCheck client:load
  prompt="Find the maximum tolerable transport delay $T_{d,\max}$ before the closed-loop system loses stability (3 decimal places):"
  expected={0.077}
  tolerance={0.002}
  unit="$\mathrm{s}$"
  explanation="At $\omega_{gc} = 12\;\mathrm{rad/s}$, delay-free phase is $-90^\circ - \arctan(12/16) = -126.87^\circ$, leaving $53.13^\circ = 0.9273\;\mathrm{rad}$, so $T_{d,\max} = 0.9273 / 12 \approx 0.0773\;\mathrm{s}$."
/>

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Show Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

1. **Gain crossover $\omega_{gc}$:** Because $|e^{-j\omega T_d}| = 1$ at every frequency, the transport delay drops out of the magnitude equation:
   $$|L(j\omega_{gc})| = \frac{15}{\omega_{gc}\sqrt{1 + (\omega_{gc}/16)^2}} = 1$$
   Testing $\omega_{gc} = 12\text{ rad/s}$ uses the $3\text{–}4\text{–}5$ right triangle ($\frac{12}{16} = \frac{3}{4} \implies \sqrt{1 + (3/4)^2} = \frac{5}{4}$):
   $$|L(j12)| = \frac{15}{12 \cdot (5/4)} = \frac{15}{15} = 1 \implies \omega_{gc} = 12\text{ rad/s}$$

2. **Phase margin at $T_d = 0.05\text{ s}$:** Evaluating each factor's phase at $\omega_{gc} = 12\text{ rad/s}$ and converting the delay shift $\omega_{gc} T_d = 12 \times 0.05 = 0.60\text{ rad}$ into degrees:
   $$\angle L(j12) = -90^\circ - \arctan\!\left(\frac{12}{16}\right) - (12 \cdot 0.05)\cdot\frac{180^\circ}{\pi} = -90^\circ - 36.87^\circ - 34.38^\circ = -161.25^\circ$$
   $$\varphi_m = 180^\circ + \angle L(j12) = 180^\circ - 161.25^\circ = +18.75^\circ$$

3. **Maximum tolerable delay $T_{d,\max}$:** The closed loop reaches the stability boundary when $\varphi_m = 0^\circ$ ($\angle L(j12) = -180^\circ$). Without delay, the rational plant has $180^\circ - 126.87^\circ = 53.13^\circ$ of phase margin available. Converting $53.13^\circ$ to radians:
   $$\Delta\varphi_{\max} = 53.13^\circ \cdot \frac{\pi}{180^\circ} \approx 0.9273\text{ rad}$$
   Dividing by $\omega_{gc} = 12\text{ rad/s}$ gives:
   $$12\, T_{d,\max} = 0.9273 \implies T_{d,\max} = \frac{0.9273}{12} \approx 0.0773\text{ s}\;(77.3\text{ ms})$$

</details>
</Exercise>

<Exercise client:load id="ex-19-2" title="Exercise 19.2 — Critical Gain for an RHP Zero" level="Intermediate">
Consider the open-loop non-minimum phase system $L(s) = \frac{K(1 - s/10)}{s(1 + s/2)}$ with $K > 0$. Setting $\angle L(j\omega_\pi) = -180^\circ$ gives $-90^\circ - \arctan(\omega_\pi/2) - \arctan(\omega_\pi/10) = -180^\circ$, so $\omega_\pi^2 = 20$ and $\omega_\pi = \sqrt{20} \approx 4.47\text{ rad/s}$.

Evaluate $|L(j\sqrt{20})|$ to find the critical proportional gain $K_{\max}$ where the closed loop loses stability, and verify your answer via Routh-Hurwitz.

<StepCheck client:load
  prompt="Enter the critical proportional gain $K_{\max}$:"
  expected={10}
  tolerance={0.05}
  unit=""
  explanation="At $\omega_\pi = \sqrt{20}$, $|L(j\sqrt{20})| = \frac{K}{\sqrt{20}}\sqrt{\frac{1 + 20/100}{1 + 20/4}} = \frac{K}{10}$. Setting $K/10 = 1$ gives $K_{\max} = 10$."
/>

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Show Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

1. **Phase crossover $\omega_\pi$:** Using the trigonometric identity $\arctan(a) + \arctan(b) = 90^\circ \iff a \cdot b = 1$ on $\arctan(\omega_\pi/2) + \arctan(\omega_\pi/10) = 90^\circ$:
   $$\frac{\omega_\pi}{2} \cdot \frac{\omega_\pi}{10} = 1 \implies \omega_\pi^2 = 20 \implies \omega_\pi = \sqrt{20} \approx 4.472\text{ rad/s}$$

2. **Bode magnitude at $\omega_\pi = \sqrt{20}$:** Substituting $\omega_\pi^2 = 20$ into $|L(j\omega)|$:
   $$|L(j\sqrt{20})| = \frac{K}{\sqrt{20}}\sqrt{\frac{1 + \omega_\pi^2 / 100}{1 + \omega_\pi^2 / 4}} = \frac{K}{\sqrt{20}}\sqrt{\frac{1 + 20/100}{1 + 20/4}} = \frac{K}{\sqrt{20}}\sqrt{\frac{1.2}{6}} = \frac{K}{\sqrt{20}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{5}} = \frac{K}{\sqrt{100}} = \frac{K}{10}$$
   At the marginal stability boundary $|L(j\omega_\pi)| = 1$, we obtain $K / 10 = 1 \implies K_{\max} = 10$.

3. **Routh-Hurwitz cross-check:** Forming $1 + L(s) = 0$:
   $$s(1 + 0.5s) + K(1 - 0.1s) = 0 \implies 0.5 s^2 + (1 - 0.1 K)s + K = 0$$
   For $K > 0$, all coefficients are positive if and only if $1 - 0.1 K > 0 \iff 0 < K < 10$, matching the Bode threshold $K_{\max} = 10$ identically.

</details>
</Exercise>

<Exercise client:load id="ex-19-3" title="Exercise 19.3 — Stability, Cancellation & Initial Slope" level="Exam Standard">
Suppose a unity-feedback loop yields the closed-loop transfer function:

$$W_{\text{CL}}(s) = \frac{4(1 - s/8)}{s^2 + 3s + 4}$$

Use **Routh-Hurwitz** ([Chapter 15](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/04-stability-routh/05-parametric-feedback-gain-stability-bounds/)) and **Laplace IVT** ([Chapter 4](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/01-unilateral-laplace-transformation-primitives/)) to evaluate its stability, cancellation options, and step behavior.

<StepCheck client:load
  mode="choice"
  prompt="Which statement about $W_{\mathrm{CL}}(s)$ is true?"
  options={[
    "$W_{\\mathrm{CL}}(s)$ is unstable because its zero $s = +8$ lies in the right half-plane",
    "$W_{\\mathrm{CL}}(s)$ is BIBO stable with initial step slope $\\dot{y}(0^+) = -0.5$, and cancelling $(1 - s/8)$ with a controller pole violates internal stability",
    "$W_{\\mathrm{CL}}(s)$ is BIBO stable and can be improved safely by cascading $C_{\\mathrm{comp}}(s) = \\frac{1}{1 - s/8}$"
  ]}
  correctIndex={1}
  explanation="The denominator $s^2 + 3s + 4$ has roots in the LHP, so $W_{\mathrm{CL}}(s)$ is BIBO stable. Its RHP zero forces $\dot{y}(0^+) = \lim_{s\to\infty} s\,W_{\mathrm{CL}}(s) = -4/8 = -0.5 < 0$, and cancelling $s = +8$ creates an unstable internal mode."
/>

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Show Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

1. **Closed-loop poles:** Stability depends strictly on denominator roots. Since $s^2 + 3s + 4 = 0$ has all positive coefficients, its roots $s = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{9 - 16}}{2} = -1.5 \pm j1.323$ lie strictly in the LHP, so $W_{\text{CL}}(s)$ is asymptotically and BIBO stable.
2. **Forbidden RHP cancellation:** Cascading a prefilter or controller with denominator $(1 - s/8)$ introduces an unstable pole at $s = +8$ that diverges exponentially whenever disturbed.
3. **Initial step slope via IVT:** For unit step response $Y(s) = W_{\text{CL}}(s)/s$, we have $y(0^+) = \lim_{s\to\infty} sY(s) = 0$ and
   $$\dot{y}(0^+) = \lim_{s \to \infty} s\big(s Y(s) - y(0^+)\big) = \lim_{s \to \infty} \frac{4s(1 - s/8)}{s^2 + 3s + 4} = \lim_{s \to \infty} \frac{-0.5\,s^2 + 4s}{s^2 + 3s + 4} = -0.5 < 0$$
   confirming negative initial undershoot before settling to $y(\infty) = W_{\text{CL}}(0) = \frac{4}{4} = +1$.

</details>
</Exercise>

<Callout type="bridge" title="Robotics Note: Non-Collocated Links & Camera Latency">
In flexible robotic arms with tip sensors (non-collocated sensing), structural torsion introduces RHP zeros that limit closed-loop joint bandwidth before tip recoil causes vibration. Similarly, vision feedback over a camera adds pure latency $T_d$ ($20\text{–}60\text{ ms}$), forcing $\omega_{gc} \ll 1/T_d$.
