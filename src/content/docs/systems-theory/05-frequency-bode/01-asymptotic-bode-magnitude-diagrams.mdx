## Introduction

Up to this point, we have predicted how a system behaves by solving differential equations in time $t$ or tracking poles and zeros in the complex $s$-plane. In laboratory practice and feedback design, however, engineers rarely excite an aircraft wing, audio amplifier, or motor drive with an ideal mathematical Dirac delta impulse—Instead, they sweep a sinusoidal input across frequency $\omega$ and measure how strongly the hardware amplifies or attenuates each oscillation speed.

**Bode magnitude diagrams** turn that entire frequency sweep into a modular, ruler-drawn geometric picture. By plotting angular frequency $\omega$ on a logarithmic axis and gain $|W(j\omega)|$ in **decibels** ($\text{dB}$), multiplication and division of high-order polynomial factors collapse into simple addition and subtraction of straight lines with slopes of $\pm 20\text{ dB/decade}$, while analytical correction formulas predict exact corner offsets and structural resonance peaks.

### Piecewise-Linear Magnitude of the Part V Loop

Before deriving the general rules for arbitrary transfer functions, let us trace how a concrete rational function behaves across frequency when converted to decibels. Consider our Part V running example with unit proportional gain $K = 1$:

$$L(s) = \frac{1 + s/4}{s(1 + s/2)}$$

To find its steady-state sinusoidal gain at angular frequency $\omega\text{ rad/s}$, substitute $s = j\omega$ and take the modulus of every factor:

$$|L(j\omega)| = \frac{|1 + j\omega/4|}{|j\omega|\cdot|1 + j\omega/2|} = \frac{\sqrt{1 + (\omega/4)^2}}{\omega\sqrt{1 + (\omega/2)^2}}$$

Plotting $|L(j\omega)|$ directly against $\omega$ on linear graph paper produces a curved hyperbola that crushes low-frequency behavior into a thin sliver near $\omega = 0$ and hides high-frequency roll-off near zero height. Instead, convert the gain into **decibels** by taking $20\log_{10}|L(j\omega)|$ and apply the logarithm product rule $\log_{10}(a\,b / c) = \log_{10} a + \log_{10} b - \log_{10} c$:

$$|L(j\omega)|_{\text{dB}} = \underbrace{-20\log_{10}\omega}_{\text{integrator } 1/s} - \underbrace{20\log_{10}\sqrt{1 + (\omega/2)^2}}_{\text{pole at } \omega_p = 2} + \underbrace{20\log_{10}\sqrt{1 + (\omega/4)^2}}_{\text{zero at } \omega_z = 4}$$

Notice how the three factors now add together independently across three frequency bands separated by the **corner frequencies** $\omega_p = 2\text{ rad/s}$ and $\omega_z = 4\text{ rad/s}$:

1. **Low-Frequency Band ($\omega \ll 2\text{ rad/s}$):**
   When $\omega$ is much smaller than $2$, both $(\omega/2)^2 \ll 1$ and $(\omega/4)^2 \ll 1$. Therefore $\sqrt{1 + (\omega/2)^2} \approx 1$ and $\sqrt{1 + (\omega/4)^2} \approx 1$, whose logarithms are $20\log_{10}(1) = 0\text{ dB}$. Only the integrator term survives:
   $$|L(j\omega)|_{\text{dB}} \approx -20\log_{10}\omega$$
   Against the horizontal coordinate $x = \log_{10}\omega$, the equation $y = -20x$ is a **straight line** with slope $-20\text{ dB/decade}$ that passes through the exact anchor point $(\omega = 1\text{ rad/s},\; 0\text{ dB})$. By the time this line reaches the first corner $\omega_p = 2\text{ rad/s}$, its asymptotic height is:
   $$y(2) = 0 - 20\log_{10}(2) \approx -6.02\text{ dB}$$
2. **Intermediate Band ($2 \le \omega < 4\text{ rad/s}$):**
   Once $\omega$ passes $\omega_p = 2\text{ rad/s}$, the pole term $\sqrt{1 + (\omega/2)^2}$ is approximated by its high-frequency asymptote $\omega/2$, contributing $-20\log_{10}(\omega/2)$ (a downward slope of $-20\text{ dB/dec}$), while the zero term at $\omega_z = 4$ still sits near $0\text{ dB}$. Combining the integrator ($-20\text{ dB/dec}$) and the activated pole ($-20\text{ dB/dec}$) gives a steeper cumulative slope of $-40\text{ dB/dec}$. Walking from $\omega_p = 2$ to $\omega_z = 4$ (one octave, $\log_{10}(4/2) \approx 0.3010$ decades) drops the asymptotic height to:
   $$y(4) = y(2) - 40\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4}{2}\right) \approx -6.02 - 40(0.3010) = -18.06\text{ dB}$$
3. **High-Frequency Band ($\omega \ge 4\text{ rad/s}$):**
   Above $\omega_z = 4\text{ rad/s}$, the numerator zero term $\sqrt{1 + (\omega/4)^2} \approx \omega/4$ wakes up and contributes $+20\log_{10}(\omega/4)$, adding $+20\text{ dB/dec}$ to the slope. The cumulative slope bends back from $-40\text{ dB/dec}$ to $-40 + 20 = -20\text{ dB/dec}$ for all $\omega > 4\text{ rad/s}$.

How far does the smooth exact curve $20\log_{10}|L(j\omega)|$ stray from those three ruler-drawn line segments? At an isolated corner $\omega = \omega_c$, replacing $\sqrt{1 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$ by $1$ introduces a vertical offset of $\pm 20\log_{10}\sqrt{2} \approx \pm 3.01\text{ dB}$. ### Harmonic Response & Semi-Log Axes

Why does evaluating a Laplace transfer function $W(s)$ along the imaginary axis $s = j\omega$ describe physical sinusoidal testing? Recall from [Chapter 5](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/02-laplace-resolvent/02-partial-fraction-expansion-heaviside-residues/) and [Chapter 16](/dynamic-courses/systems-theory/04-stability-routh/06-bounded-input-bounded-output-stability/) that when a BIBO-stable LTI system (all poles of $W(s)$ strictly in the open left half-plane, $\operatorname{Re}(p_k) < 0$) is driven by a sinusoidal input $u(t) = U\sin(\omega t)\,\mathbb{1}(t)$ with Laplace transform $U(s) = \frac{U\omega}{s^2 + \omega^2}$, partial fraction expansion splits the output $Y(s) = W(s)U(s)$ into two parts:
1. **Natural Transient Modes:** Terms arising from the LHP poles $p_k$ of $W(s)$, which decay exponentially to zero as $e^{\operatorname{Re}(p_k)t} \to 0$.
2. **Sinusoidal Steady-State Response ($y_{\text{ss}}(t)$):** The residue contribution from the imaginary-axis input poles at $s = \pm j\omega$, which persists indefinitely and oscillates at the exact same driving frequency $\omega$:

$$y_{\text{ss}}(t) = U\,|W(j\omega)|\,\sin\!\big(\omega t + \arg W(j\omega)\big)$$

Every term in this formula has a direct oscilloscope meaning: $|W(j\omega)| = \frac{\text{Output Amplitude}}{\text{Input Amplitude}}$ is the dimensionless amplitude gain at frequency $\omega$, and $\arg W(j\omega)$ is the phase shift.

To plot $|W(j\omega)|$ across several orders of magnitude of frequency and gain, Hendrik Bode introduced **semi-logarithmic coordinates**:
- **Horizontal Axis (Logarithmic Frequency):** Although the grid lines are labeled with physical angular frequencies $\omega$ in $\text{rad/s}$ ($0.1, 1, 10, 100, \dots$), physical horizontal distance on paper is proportional to $x = \log_{10}\omega$. Equal horizontal steps represent equal **frequency ratios**: a ratio of $\omega_2 / \omega_1 = 10$ ($\Delta\log_{10}\omega = 1$) is one **decade**, and a ratio of $\omega_2 / \omega_1 = 2$ ($\Delta\log_{10}\omega = \log_{10} 2 \approx 0.3010$) is one **octave**. Notice that $\omega = 0$ can never appear on a Bode plot because $\log_{10}(0) = -\infty$.
- **Vertical Axis (Linear Decibels):** Vertical height is plotted linearly in **decibels** ($\text{dB}$):
  $$|W(j\omega)|_{\text{dB}} = 20\log_{10}|W(j\omega)| \iff |W(j\omega)| = 10^{|W(j\omega)|_{\text{dB}} / 20}$$
  Under this logarithmic scale, unity gain $|W| = 1$ maps to $0\text{ dB}$; multiplying amplitude by $10$ adds $+20\text{ dB}$ ($100 \leftrightarrow +40\text{ dB}$, $0.1 \leftrightarrow -20\text{ dB}$); and multiplying amplitude by $2$ adds $+20\log_{10}(2) \approx +6.02\text{ dB}$ ($1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707 \leftrightarrow -3.01\text{ dB}$).

### Evans vs. Bode Canonical Form

In root-locus and state-space analysis, a rational transfer function is usually given in **Evans (pole-zero-gain) form**, where the highest power of $s$ inside each factor has coefficient $1$:

$$W(s) = K \frac{\prod_{i=1}^m (s - z_i)}{\prod_{k=1}^n (s - p_k)}$$

Why is Evans form inconvenient for Bode sketching? Because at low frequencies ($\omega \to 0$), each factor $(j\omega - z_i)$ approaches $-z_i$ rather than $1$, so every single pole and zero contributes to the low-frequency gain! To isolate the low-frequency behavior into a single constant prefix, we factor the constant term $-z_i$ out of every non-zero finite zero factor $(s - z_i) = -z_i(1 - s/z_i)$ and $-p_k$ out of every non-zero finite pole factor $(s - p_k) = -p_k(1 - s/p_k)$. Defining the **time constants** $\tau_{z,i} = -1/z_i$ and $\tau_{p,j} = -1/p_j$ for real singularities and natural frequency $\omega_n$ plus damping ratio $\zeta$ for complex conjugate pairs produces the **Bode canonical (time-constant) form**:

$$\boxed{W(s) = \frac{K_B}{s^g} \frac{\prod_{i} (1 + \tau_{z,i} s) \prod_{k} \left( 1 + \frac{2\zeta_{z,k}}{\omega_{n,z,k}} s + \frac{s^2}{\omega_{n,z,k}^2} \right)}{\prod_{j} (1 + \tau_{p,j} s) \prod_{l} \left( 1 + \frac{2\zeta_{p,l}}{\omega_{n,p,l}} s + \frac{s^2}{\omega_{n,p,l}} \right)}}$$

In this canonical representation:
- $g \in \mathbb{Z}$ is the **system type**, counting the net number of poles at the origin $s = 0$ ($g > 0$ for $g$ integrators $1/s^g$; $g < 0$ for $|g|$ differentiators $s^{|g|}$; $g = 0$ when no origin poles or zeros exist).
- $K_B = \lim_{s \to 0} s^g W(s)$ is the **Bode gain**. Because every normalized factor $(1 + \tau s)$ and $(1 + \frac{2\zeta}{\omega_n}s + \frac{s^2}{\omega_n^2})$ evaluates to $1$ as $s \to 0$, the entire low-frequency behavior of $W(s)$ collapses to the monomial $W_{\text{LF}}(s) = \frac{K_B}{s^g}$.
- $\omega_c = \frac{1}{|\tau|}$ is the **corner (break) frequency** of each real pole or zero, and $\omega_n$ is the **corner (natural) frequency** of each complex conjugate pair.

Always distinguish the Evans leading coefficient $K$ from the Bode gain $K_B$: for example, in $W(s) = \frac{10(s + 2)}{s(s + 5)}$, the Evans gain is $K = 10$, whereas factoring out $2$ from $(s + 2) = 2(1 + s/2)$ and $5$ from $(s + 5) = 5(1 + s/5)$ gives:

$$W(s) = \frac{10 \cdot 2\,(1 + s/2)}{s \cdot 5\,(1 + s/5)} = \frac{4}{s}\frac{1 + 0.5s}{1 + 0.2s} \implies K_B = 4 \neq 10$$

### Decibels and Straight-Line Asymptotes

Substituting $s = j\omega$ into the Bode canonical form and taking $20\log_{10}|\cdot|$ converts the product of elementary factors into an algebraic sum of decibel curves:

$$|W(j\omega)|_{\text{dB}} = \underbrace{20\log_{10}|K_B| - 20g\log_{10}\omega}_{\text{low-frequency line } W_{\text{LF}}(j\omega)} + \sum_i 20\log_{10}|1 + j\omega\tau_{z,i}| - \sum_j 20\log_{10}|1 + j\omega\tau_{p,j}| + \dots$$

Let us analyze each elementary building block in detail:

1. **Low-Frequency Monomial $W_{\text{LF}}(j\omega) = K_B / (j\omega)^g$:**
   Its exact decibel value is $y_{\text{LF}}(\omega) = 20\log_{10}|K_B| - 20g\log_{10}\omega$. In terms of the horizontal log coordinate $x = \log_{10}\omega$, this is an exact straight line of slope $S_0 = -20g\text{ dB/decade}$. At $\omega = 1\text{ rad/s}$ ($x = \log_{10} 1 = 0$), the second term vanishes for any $g$, pinning the low-frequency asymptote (or its straight-line extension) through the universal **anchor point** $(\omega = 1\text{ rad/s},\; 20\log_{10}|K_B|\text{ dB})$.
2. **First-Order Real Pole $(1 + j\omega/\omega_c)^{-1}$ (and Real Zero $1 + j\omega/\omega_c$):**
   Writing $\omega_c = 1/|\tau|$, the exact decibel contribution of a single real pole is:
   $$|F_p(j\omega)|_{\text{dB}} = -20\log_{10}\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_c}\right)^2} = -10\log_{10}\!\left(1 + \frac{\omega^2}{\omega_c^2}\right)$$
   Inspect its behavior below, above, and right at the corner frequency $\omega_c$:
   - **Below the corner ($\omega \ll \omega_c$):** Since $(\omega/\omega_c)^2 \ll 1$, we neglect $(\omega/\omega_c)^2$ relative to $1$, yielding $-10\log_{10}(1) = 0\text{ dB}$. A pole or zero is invisible in magnitude below its corner frequency.
   - **Above the corner ($\omega \gg \omega_c$):** Since $(\omega/\omega_c)^2 \gg 1$, we neglect $1$ relative to $(\omega/\omega_c)^2$, yielding:
     $$|F_p(j\omega)|_{\text{dB}} \approx -20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_c}\right) = -20\log_{10}\omega + 20\log_{10}\omega_c$$
     This is a straight line of slope $-20\text{ dB/decade}$ (or $-6.02\text{ dB/octave}$) that intersects the $0\text{ dB}$ axis precisely at $\omega = \omega_c$.
   - **Exact Error at the Corner ($\omega = \omega_c$):** At $\omega = \omega_c$, the two straight asymptotes meet at $0\text{ dB}$, whereas the exact formula gives $-20\log_{10}\sqrt{1 + 1^2} = -20\log_{10}\sqrt{2} \approx -3.01\text{ dB}$ (and $-10\log_{10}(1 + 0.5^2) \approx -0.97\text{ dB}$ one octave away at $\omega = 0.5\omega_c$ and $2\omega_c$).
   Reversing the sign for a real numerator zero $(1 + j\omega/\omega_c)$ flips the high-frequency slope to $+20\text{ dB/decade}$ and the corner offset to $+3.01\text{ dB}$.

### Complex Poles and Resonant Peaking

Now consider a second-order complex conjugate pole pair with natural frequency $\omega_n > 0$ and damping ratio $0 < \zeta < 1$:

$$F_{p2}(s) = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{2\zeta}{\omega_n}s + \frac{s^2}{\omega_n^2}}$$

Substituting $s = j\omega$ separates the denominator into its real part $1 - (\omega/\omega_n)^2$ and imaginary part $j\,2\zeta(\omega/\omega_n)$. Taking the squared modulus and converting to decibels gives:

$$|F_{p2}(j\omega)|_{\text{dB}} = -20\log_{10}\sqrt{\left(1 - \frac{\omega^2}{\omega_n^2}\right)^2 + \left(2\zeta\frac{\omega}{\omega_n}\right)^2}$$

Let us first extract its piecewise-linear asymptotes:
- For $\omega \ll \omega_n$, the fourth-degree polynomial under the square root approaches $1$, giving $0\text{ dB}$.
- For $\omega \gg \omega_n$, the $(\omega/\omega_n)^4$ term dominates under the square root, giving $-20\log_{10}\big((\omega/\omega_n)^2\big) = -40\log_{10}(\omega/\omega_n)$: a straight line of slope **$-40\text{ dB/decade}$** breaking at $\omega = \omega_n$ (twice as steep as a single real pole because two poles break together at $\omega_n$).

Near the corner frequency $\omega = \omega_n$, however, the exact curve depends strongly on the damping ratio $\zeta$. At $\omega = \omega_n$, the real part $1 - (\omega_n/\omega_n)^2 = 0$ cancels completely, leaving only the imaginary damping term $2\zeta$:

$$|F_{p2}(j\omega_n)| = \frac{1}{2\zeta} \implies |F_{p2}(j\omega_n)|_{\text{dB}} = -20\log_{10}(2\zeta)\text{ dB}$$

Even more importantly, does $|F_{p2}(j\omega)|$ reach a local maximum (a **resonant peak** $M_r$) at some frequency $\omega_r$ before rolling off at $-40\text{ dB/dec}$? To find out, maximize $|F_{p2}(j\omega)|$ by minimizing the denominator squared modulus $D(u) = (1 - u)^2 + 4\zeta^2 u$ with respect to the normalized squared frequency $u = (\omega/\omega_n)^2 \ge 0$:

$$\frac{dD(u)}{du} = -2(1 - u) + 4\zeta^2 = 2u - 2 + 4\zeta^2 = 0 \implies u_r = \frac{\omega_r^2}{\omega_n^2} = 1 - 2\zeta^2$$

Because $\omega_r$ must be a real positive frequency ($u_r > 0$), this interior minimum exists **if and only if** $1 - 2\zeta^2 > 0$, which yields the classic resonance threshold:

$$0 < \zeta < \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707$$

When $0 < \zeta < 1/\sqrt{2}$, taking the square root of $u_r = 1 - 2\zeta^2$ and substituting $u_r$ back into $D(u_r) = (2\zeta^2)^2 + 4\zeta^2(1 - 2\zeta^2) = 4\zeta^2(1 - \zeta^2)$ gives the exact **resonant frequency** $\omega_r$ and **resonant peak** $M_r$:

$$\boxed{\omega_r = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2} < \omega_n}, \qquad \boxed{M_r = |F_{p2}(j\omega_r)| = \frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} > 1}$$

On the other hand, if $\zeta \ge 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707$, then $dD/du \ge 0$ for all $u > 0$, meaning $|F_{p2}(j\omega)|$ decreases **monotonically** from $0\text{ dB}$ without any resonant overshoot above $0\text{ dB}$. Notice also that as $\zeta \to 0^+$, $\omega_r \to \omega_n$ and $M_r \to \infty$, corresponding to undamped poles right on the imaginary axis $\pm j\omega_n$.

### Drafting Procedure

1. **Convert to Bode Canonical Form & Sort Corners:** Factor constants out of all $(s - z_i)$ and $(s - p_k)$ terms to find $g$, $K_B$, and $20\log_{10}|K_B|$. Sort all corner frequencies $\omega_{c,1} < \dots < \omega_{c,r}$ and pick a plotting window $[\omega_{\text{start}}, \omega_{\text{end}}]$ with $\omega_{\text{start}} \le 0.1\,\omega_{c,1}$ and $\omega_{\text{end}} \ge 10\,\omega_{c,r}$.
2. **Draw the Low-Frequency Line:** Start at $y(\omega_{\text{start}}) = 20\log_{10}|K_B| - 20g\log_{10}(\omega_{\text{start}})$ and draw a straight segment of slope $S_0 = -20g\text{ dB/dec}$ aimed through the anchor point $(1\text{ rad/s},\, 20\log_{10}|K_B|)$ up to $\omega_{c,1}$.
3. **Walk Vertex-to-Vertex:** Compute each next corner height using the general logarithmic step formula:
   $$\boxed{y(\omega_{k+1}) = y(\omega_k) + S_{\text{current}} \cdot \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\omega_{k+1}}{\omega_k}\right)}$$
   and update the outgoing slope $S_{\text{new}} = S_{\text{current}} + \Delta S_k$ from the table above.
4. **Add Exact Offsets:** Mark $-3.01\text{ dB}$ beneath simple real poles, $+3.01\text{ dB}$ above simple real zeros, and the resonant peak $M_r = \frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$ at $\omega_r = \omega_n\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}$ whenever $0 < \zeta < 0.707$.

### Key Proofs

- **Logarithmic slope of a real pole:** Show that $-20\log_{10}\sqrt{1 + (\omega/\omega_c)^2} \to 0\text{ dB}$ for $\omega \ll \omega_c$, $-20\log_{10}(\omega/\omega_c)$ ($-20\text{ dB/dec}$) for $\omega \gg \omega_c$, and $-20\log_{10}\sqrt{2} \approx -3.01\text{ dB}$ at $\omega = \omega_c$.
- **Resonant peak derivation:** Differentiate $D(u) = (1 - u)^2 + 4\zeta^2 u$ with respect to $u = (\omega/\omega_n)^2$ to prove $u_r = 1 - 2\zeta^2 > 0 \iff \zeta < 1/\sqrt{2}$ and $M_r = \frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistakes">
1. **Confusing Evans $K$ with Bode $K_B$:** Always factor constants out of every $(s + a)$ binomial before computing $20\log_{10}|K_B|$.
2. **Treating Corner Error as a Slope Change:** Never change the line slope by $-3.01\text{ dB}$; $-3.01\text{ dB}$ is purely a vertical offset between the sharp asymptotic vertex and the smooth exact curve at $\omega_c$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="bridge" title="Robotics Note: Structural Resonance in Drone Frames">
Lightweight carbon-fiber quadrotor frames often show low structural damping ($\zeta \approx 0.05$) near $\omega_n \approx 150\text{ rad/s}$, creating a resonant spike $M_r \approx \frac{1}{2(0.05)} = 10$ ($+20\text{ dB}$) that digital notch filters at $\omega_r$ remove in flight firmware.
</Callout>

   $$W(s) = \frac{10 \cdot 2 (1 + s/2)}{s \cdot 20 (1 + s/20)} = \frac{20}{20s}\frac{1 + 0.5s}{1 + 0.05s} = \frac{1}{s}\frac{1 + 0.5s}{1 + 0.05s}$$
   Thus $g = 1$ (one integrator at $s = 0$), $K_B = 1$, and $20\log_{10}|K_B| = 20\log_{10}(1) = 0\text{ dB}$.
2. **Corners & vertex walking:** Zero at $\omega_z = 2\text{ rad/s}$, pole at $\omega_p = 20\text{ rad/s}$.
   - For $\omega < 2\text{ rad/s}$: slope is $S_0 = -20(1) = -20\text{ dB/dec}$ passing through $(1\text{ rad/s},\, 0\text{ dB})$. At $\omega_z = 2\text{ rad/s}$:
     $$y(2) = 0 - 20\log_{10}(2/1) \approx -6.02\text{ dB}$$
   - For $2 \le \omega < 20\text{ rad/s}$: the zero at $\omega_z = 2$ adds $+20\text{ dB/dec}$, giving a flat slope $S_1 = -20 + 20 = 0\text{ dB/dec}$. Walking across $[2, 20]$ leaves the height constant:
     $$y(20) = y(2) + 0 \cdot \log_{10}(20/2) = -6.02\text{ dB}$$
   - For $\omega \ge 20\text{ rad/s}$: the pole at $\omega_p = 20$ subtracts $20\text{ dB/dec}$, returning the slope to $S_2 = 0 - 20 = -20\text{ dB/dec}$.

Consider the open-loop transfer function:
$$W(s) = \frac{200 (s + 5)}{s (s + 2)(s + 50)}$$
1. Convert $W(s)$ to Bode canonical form and find $20\log_{10}|K_B|$.
2. Compute asymptotic levels at $\omega = 0.1, 1, 2, 5, 50, 100\text{ rad/s}$.
3. Solve for the asymptotic $0\text{ dB}$ crossover frequency $\omega_c$.

<StepCheck client:load prompt="For $W(s) = \frac{200(s+5)}{s(s+2)(s+50)}$, compute $20\log_{10}|K_B|$:" expected={20} tolerance={0.05} unit="$\mathrm{dB}$" explanation="$K_B = \frac{200 \cdot 5}{2 \cdot 50} = 10 \implies 20\;\mathrm{dB}$." />

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Show Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

1. **Canonical form:** Factoring $5$ from $(s+5)$, $2$ from $(s+2)$, and $50$ from $(s+50)$ gives:
   $$W(s) = \frac{200 \cdot 5(1 + s/5)}{s \cdot 2(1 + s/2) \cdot 50(1 + s/50)} = \frac{10(1 + 0.2s)}{s(1 + 0.5s)(1 + 0.02s)}$$
   so $g = 1$ and $K_B = 10$ ($20\log_{10}|K_B| = 20\text{ dB}$).
2. **Vertex levels:**
   - $y(0.1) = 20 - 20\log_{10}(0.1) = 40\text{ dB}$, and $y(1) = 20\text{ dB}$ on the initial $-20\text{ dB/dec}$ segment.
   - At pole $\omega_{p,1} = 2\text{ rad/s}$: $y(2) = 20 - 20\log_{10}(2) \approx 13.98\text{ dB}$ (outgoing slope drops to $-40\text{ dB/dec}$).
   - At zero $\omega_{z,1} = 5\text{ rad/s}$: $y(5) = 13.98 - 40\log_{10}(5/2) = 13.98 - 40(0.3979) \approx -1.94\text{ dB}$ (outgoing slope rises to $-20\text{ dB/dec}$).
   - At pole $\omega_{p,2} = 50\text{ rad/s}$ (1 decade above $5$): $y(50) = -1.94 - 20(1) = -21.94\text{ dB}$ (outgoing slope drops to $-40\text{ dB/dec}$).
   - At $\omega = 100\text{ rad/s}$: $y(100) = -21.94 - 40\log_{10}(100/50) \approx -33.98\text{ dB}$.
3. **Crossover on $[2, 5]$:** Since $y(2) = +13.98\text{ dB} > 0$ and $y(5) = -1.94\text{ dB} < 0$, the $0\text{ dB}$ crossing lies inside $[2, 5]$ where the slope is $-40\text{ dB/dec}$:

<Exercise client:load id="ex-17-3" title="Exercise 17.3 — Complex Conjugate Resonance Peak" level="Intermediate">

A second-order accelerometer stage has the transfer function:
$$W(s) = \frac{100}{s^2 + 2s + 100}$$
1. Write $W(s)$ in Bode canonical form and identify $K_B$, $\omega_n$, and $\zeta$.
2. Determine whether a resonant peak exists, and compute the exact resonant frequency $\omega_r$ and peak amplification $M_r$ in decibels.

<StepCheck client:load prompt="Compute the resonant peak amplification $20\log_{10}(M_r)$ in $\mathrm{dB}$ (round to 2 decimal places):" expected={14.02} tolerance={0.1} unit="$\mathrm{dB}$" explanation="Here $\omega_n = 10\;\mathrm{rad/s}$ and $\zeta = 0.1 < 0.707$. Thus $M_r = \frac{1}{2(0.1)\sqrt{1 - 0.01}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{0.99}} \approx 5.025 \implies 20\log_{10}(5.025) \approx 14.02\;\mathrm{dB}$." />

<details className="dc-solution">
<summary>Show Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

1. **Bode canonical form:** Factor $100$ from the denominator:
   $$W(s) = \frac{100}{100\left(1 + \frac{2}{100}s + \frac{s^2}{100}\right)} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{2(0.1)}{10}s + \frac{s^2}{10^2}}$$
   Matching $1 + \frac{2\zeta}{\omega_n}s + \frac{s^2}{\omega_n^2}$ gives $g = 0$, $K_B = 1$ ($0\text{ dB}$), $\omega_n = 10\text{ rad/s}$, and $\zeta = 0.1$.
2. **Resonance parameters:** Since $0 < \zeta = 0.1 < 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707$, a strong resonant peak occurs at:
   $$\omega_r = \omega_n\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2} = 10\sqrt{1 - 0.02} = 10\sqrt{0.98} \approx 9.90\text{ rad/s}$$
   $$M_r = \frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} = \frac{1}{0.2\sqrt{0.99}} \approx 5.025 \implies 20\log_{10}(M_r) \approx +14.02\text{ dB}$$

</details>
